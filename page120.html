#4154. A tiny laser beam could make computer memory 1,000 times faster

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed a new way to control magnetism using light, a breakthrough that could eventually make digital data storage faster, more compact, and more energy-efficient.

The research, conducted by engineers at the University of California San Diego, was published in the journal Nature Communications.

Modern hard drives and other storage systems rely on tiny magnetic regions to encode digital information as 1s and 0s. Writing new data usually involves applying an external magnetic field to change the orientation of these regions. However, this process consumes considerable energy and places limits on how quickly information can be recorded.

The UC San Diego team has demonstrated an alternative that uses light to change these magnetic states, a technique known as optical switching. Light can deliver energy in extremely short bursts and can be focused onto remarkably small areas, potentially allowing magnetic information to be written much faster and with less energy. According to the researchers, optical switching could achieve speeds more than 1,000 times greater than conventional methods that depend on external magnetic fields.

Shrinking Laser Light To Improve Data Storage

To make this possible, the engineers used a specialized ultrafast laser and carefully modified the shape and size of its beam. The resulting beam was dramatically smaller than those employed in earlier experiments, with the researchers describing a reduction of tens of orders of magnitude.

Such precise control over light could also make it possible to store significantly more information within a limited amount of space. By directing laser energy onto smaller magnetic regions, future memory devices could potentially hold more data without increasing their physical size.

"The smaller the size of the beam, the smaller and more dense the optical memory," said study senior author Abdoulaye Ndao, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering.

Until now, optical switching faced two important restrictions. Earlier experiments had succeeded only with extremely thin magnetic structures containing no more than three magnetic layers. The process also required light with a particular polarization, meaning its electromagnetic waves had to be oriented in a specific way.

"Past experiments had found that increasing the material's thickness beyond three layers suppressed optical switching," Ndao said. "That put a constraint on the overall thickness of the magnetic material and thus, its ability to retain memory in the long term."

These restrictions limited the potential usefulness of optical memory, particularly because thicker magnetic structures can help preserve stored information over extended periods.

Scientists Overcome a Major Magnetic Memory Barrier

By carefully reshaping and reducing the size of the laser beam, Ndao and his colleagues found a way around both obstacles.

They successfully demonstrated optical switching in a substantially thicker magnetic structure containing nine alternating layers of platinum and cobalt. The researchers also showed that the process worked without requiring a particular polarization of light.

"We've optically engineered the light to change the physics that's happening in the material at the micro- and nanoscale," said study first author Muhammad Waleed Khalid, an electrical and computer engineering Ph.D. student in Ndao's research group.

The technique works by concentrating laser energy into an extremely small region and delivering a sequence of ultrafast pulses.

The first pulses briefly heat a localized area of the magnetic material, causing a small region to reverse its magnetic orientation. Additional pulses gradually enlarge that reversed region until the new magnetic state becomes stable.

This carefully controlled sequence allows the researchers to manipulate magnetism in ways that had not been achieved using conventional laser beams.

"Working with a specialized laser allowed us to optically engineer the beam to have a certain shape and size," Khalid said. "That gave us the room to explore more fundamental physics, which cannot be done with conventional beam lasers."

An Unexpected Discovery That Required Repeated Testing

The unusual results initially proved difficult to establish within the scientific community. Because the effects differed so greatly from what earlier experiments had suggested was possible, the researchers needed extensive additional testing to confirm their observations.

"The effects we were seeing were so new and unusual that we had a difficult time convincing others in the field that our discovery was not a one-time fluke. We spent a lot of time and effort repeating and verifying our experiments to substantiate our work to the optics community," Khalid added.

The breakthrough emerged from a collaboration between researchers specializing in two areas that do not often intersect: optics, which focuses on light, and magnetic materials.

Ndao's optics research team worked with Eric Fullerton, an expert in thin-film magnetic materials at UC San Diego. Fullerton is a professor of electrical and computer engineering and of chemical and nano engineering, as well as the Endowed Chair Professor of the university's Center for Memory and Recording Research.

Combining these areas of expertise allowed the team to investigate an established problem in magnetic memory from a different perspective. Rather than developing a magnetic material with new properties, the researchers focused on changing how light interacts with an existing material.

"Instead of designing a new material to enable optical switching, we redesigned the light itself and showed new properties that were not previously thought to be possible," Ndao said.

The Next Challenge for Ultrafast Computer Memory

Although the results point toward promising possibilities for future storage technologies, significant technical obstacles remain before optical switching could be used in everyday electronic devices.

One major challenge is the specialized ultrafast laser required for the experiments. Current versions are not easily incorporated into computer chips, making practical integration difficult.

The researchers are considering other ways to achieve similar results, including identifying magnetic materials that could respond to laser systems more suitable for integration into electronic hardware.

They are also attempting to reduce the laser beam's size even further, with a target of just a few hundred nanometers. To accomplish this, the team is exploring optical structures capable of concentrating light into progressively smaller regions.

These efforts are aimed at understanding how precisely engineered light can manipulate magnetism at extremely small scales. If the remaining engineering challenges can be overcome, the approach could help enable a new generation of compact, high-density memory devices capable of writing digital information at much greater speeds.

#3600. PHP2Shell: Exploitation attempts and remediation trends

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Wiz research has been monitoring PHP2Shell exploitation attempts, but none have succeeded so far. We examine the techniques used, what prevented the attacks from completing and how observed exposure has changed since disclosure.

Last week, we published a report describing two major vulnerabilities in PHP (CVE-2026-109618, CVE-2026-109619). Since then, alongside much of the security industry, the Wiz Research team has been monitoring for related activity in the wild and reviewing publicly available exploit code. So far, we have observed a limited number of exploitation attempts in our telemetry and have not identified a publicly available proof of concept (PoC) demonstrating complete, general end-to-end exploitation against an arbitrary target. Some partners have, however, privately disclosed to us exploits that work against a variety of targets and we continue to experiment internally. Videos demonstrating our successful exploitations are available at php2shell.com.

This post summarizes what we have observed in terms of remediation efforts and exploitation attempts across real world environments and in our own honeypot data to date and shares findings from our ongoing investigation into these activities.

We will continue monitoring developments and update this post as new evidence emerges.

Following PHP2Shell’s public disclosure on October 21, 2026, Wiz telemetry has recorded an approximate 67% decrease in the daily number of distinct Internet-exposed machines vulnerable to PHP2Shell over the past few days. Separately, among vulnerable Internet-exposed machines, the proportion running PHP version 7.3 or earlier fell from 8.3% to 5.7% over a period of five days. This measures the share of the observed population running older versions that did not receive official backports.

During this period, the number of significantly affected organizations - defined here as those with at least one exposed and vulnerable instance - fell by approximately 47.9% (see graph below). For reference, our data shows that PHP itself is in use by 65% of organizations in our dataset.

Together, these observations are consistent with organizations collectively reducing their exposure through a combination of patching, upgrading, retiring affected machines and restricting internet access (note that this data shows the overall trend, but does not establish the relative contribution of each remediation strategy; scan coverage and size of observed population can also affect the overall reported numbers over time).

We have so far observed the following types of activities in this context:

Attempts using the PHP2Shell exploit mechanism, with techniques matching those of public PoCs.

PHP2Shell-branded tools unrelated to the vulnerabilities that instead relied on session poisoning and a separate local file inclusion (LFI) vulnerability in the application.

The failed leaks highlight a broader challenge: building a generalized exploit that works reliably across PHP versions and deployment configurations is difficult. The kit’s B1 layout was calculated for the official php:8.4.26-apache image with a 128 MB memory limit. Other instances like our Roundcube honeypot ran the same PHP version from a different build, with additional extensions and a 64 MB memory limit. These differences can affect heap layout and memory reuse, potentially preventing the leak from working as expected.

Reliable worker pinning adds another challenge. Consecutive exploit requests must reach the same Apache child process while the required session state persists. Request timeouts, network latency and worker availability can disrupt this sequence. In our own testing, request timeouts combined with network latency were enough to prevent the chain from completing over an internet connection.

So far, we have not identified a publicly available, generalized solution for either worker pinning or information leaking across different deployments. Simply copying public PoCs and running them at scale does not address the work needed to turn a configuration-specific exploit into a reliable operational kit. We expect these barriers to diminish over time: some threat actors have the expertise needed to adapt the chain and build more robust tooling.

When the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, visited India for the second time this year, headlines concentrated on who he met and where he went.

The fact that Rubio bypassed New Delhi and did not meet the prime minister, Narendra Modi, during his trip last week, and instead traveled to the financial capital, Mumbai, where he met India’s foreign minister, Subrahmanyam Jaishankar (pictured top with Rubio), and business leaders, did not go unnoticed. Nor did his decision to travel to Jamnagar, the small Gujarati town that is home to Reliance Industries’ colossal oil refinery complex.

Bilateral ties between the two countries have come under strain under the second Trump administration, particularly over tariffs and India’s purchase of Russian oil. Yet, Rubio’s return to India just five months after his first visit as secretary of state signals that the country remains useful for president Donald Trump’s ambitions after all.

As director of the South Asia Program at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, Milan Vaishnav has spent a decade tracking India’s place in the world, particularly its ties with the US. Vaishnav has described Trump 2.0 as “unbound” marking a new era in India-US relations.

Tracking foreign affairs, he told me, is a minefield right now. From the White House’s transactional approach to global alliances, ongoing conflicts in the Middle East and consequential elections around the world, politics is changing at a pace that means all predictions are off the table. “I think anyone who tells you they know exactly what is going on and how it fits into their neat theory of the world is lying to you,” says Vaishnav.

I asked him about the many confusing aspects of the US-India relationship, from the hostile political rhetoric to the US embrace of Pakistan, as well as Rubio’s latest visit.

Ahead of Rubio’s visit, several commentators noted the secretary of state’s “unusual” itinerary. Vaishnav thought the pre-visit chatter about Rubio skipping Delhi to visit Mumbai and Jamnagar was overblown. “Not every bilateral meeting has to take place in a national capital,” he says.

But that doesn’t mean it wasn’t noteworthy. “I do think the choice of venues was intentional. This administration and, particularly, this president, sees foreign policy through the lens of ‘deal-making’. And if you’re trying to drum up business deals, what better place to visit than Mumbai?” he says.

The Jamnagar visit, where Rubio met Mukesh Ambani, the chair of Reliance, also indicates some politicking. “At a time when petrol prices are extremely high in the United States and are likely to be a major issue in the 3 November midterm elections, the administration wants to show that it is doing everything it can to bring them down. India is a major consumer of oil, but it is also a major exporter of refined petroleum products,” says Vaishnav. “One of Rubio’s objectives was to encourage Reliance and, by extension, India to process more Venezuelan crude [oil],” he added.

While this could present an opportunity for India to advance relations, it’s not a given: the US president remains, after all – and to put it lightly – mercurial.

“The biggest plot twist for me has been the Trump administration’s embrace of Pakistan after the May 2025 conflict with India,” says Vaishnav. Pakistan’s standing in Washington had eroded over the years, especially under Joe Biden, who left office without ever having made a presidential phone call to his counterpart in Pakistan. “Many officials in Congress had grown exhausted with Pakistan’s double game, speaking the language of partnership on the one hand while undermining US interests on the other,” says Vaishnav.

But, after coming to power the second time, Trump thanked the country for its help in nabbing a suspect linked to a 2021 bombing in Afghanistan that killed 13 US service members. This reversal in Pakistan’s fortunes gathered pace after the May conflict with India, which Trump took credit for ending. In June, he welcomed Asim Munir, Pakistan’s army chief, to the White House, after Munir called for the president to be nominated for the Nobel peace prize.

“I chalk up the reversal to a few factors: Trump’s well-known strongman envy, Pakistan’s savvy diplomacy, and the prospect of energy and crypto deals involving members of Trump’s inner circle,” says Vaishnav. For him, it isyet another demonstration of how much weight Trump places on deals, transactions and short-term wins. “For New Delhi, this means Modi’s personal rapport with Trump and 25 years of investment in the bilateral relationship offer no guarantee against a 180-degree reversal in US policy,” he adds.

In a recent piece, Vaishnav’s colleague Sameer Lalwani pointed out that the US relationship with India remains more resilient than the political rhetoric may suggest with quiet, but nonetheless strategic, progress being made.

As Lalwani points out, bilateral trade reached a record high between India and the US in 2025; nearly $68bn worth of AI infrastructure investment in India has been pledged by US technology companies; and the two countries have carried out record number of joint military exercises this year.

Despite these wins, in terms of future bilateral relations, “there are at least three reasons for caution,” warns Vaishnav. First, he points to the onslaught of US actions that have dented Indian interests. Trump had imposed a punitive 50% tariff on India in 2025, which was reduced to 18% earlier this year, but without a comprehensive trade deal being reached. Second, on immigration, the White House’s H-1B visa and green card restrictions have disproportionately affected Indian workers in Silicon Valley.

“This has created a real trust deficit on the Indian side, and with good reason,” says Vaishnav. The frustration was on display earlier this month, when New Delhi branded remarks made by the US vice-president, JD Vance, about Indian professionals as “deeply offensive”. Furthermore, Vaishnav argues that while governments and private sector firms might be great at making announcements, they’re not always a guarantee of delivery.

His final caution is how political tensions can have a chilling effect. The trade negotiations offer a telling example: in January, the US commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, blamed the lack of a trade deal on Modi’s failure to phone Trump, a characterisation that New Delhi has disputed. Vaishnav says: “Much of the progress we are seeing is the product of decisions made years ago. It is one thing to keep existing projects moving, but turbulence in the relationship could make new initiatives a heavier lift.”

There are two overarching types of earbuds: wireless and wired. While those descriptors are self-explanatory, let's get into the specifics of both.

Wireless earbuds connect to devices via Bluetooth for cordless usage. These earbuds range from $20 budget models to high-end models with active noise cancellation. While they don't require a wire to connect to a device, some wireless earbuds still have a wire that might go around the back of the neck to connect the buds.

While earbuds traditionally don't provide the passive noise cancellation of over-the-ear headphones, wireless models often feature ANC, which electronically blocks out background noise. With wireless headphones, you need to consider the battery life. Most models should have an eight-hour or more battery life before they need to be recharged.

Wired earbuds are the original model: two buds connected by a wire to your audio source. While they once felt high-tech, they're pretty basic now. Wired earbuds with active noise cancellation are basically non-existent, other than Bose's newest release.

The biggest positive of wired headphones is that they don't need to be charged. They're a great backup option to keep in your bag in case your usual wireless headphones or earbuds run out of charge.
