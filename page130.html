#390. Do spiders dream? Scientists just found a surprising clue

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Bonn have discovered that spiders experience distinct stages of sleep, including a REM-like state that shares surprising similarities with human sleep. During this stage, the spiders move their eyes and twitch their muscles, behaviors also associated with REM (rapid eye movement) sleep in humans and other vertebrates.

The findings provide the first comprehensive experimental confirmation that spiders experience genuine, regulated sleep rather than simply remaining inactive. They also raise intriguing questions about how sleep evolved and whether animals with very different nervous systems might share some of its fundamental features.

Researchers at the University of Bonn's Institute of Organismic Biology made the discovery while studying the Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata, a small but remarkably sophisticated predator.

A Tiny Spider With Remarkable Hunting Skills

The Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata is only about the size of a one-euro coin when fully grown, but its hunting abilities make it particularly interesting to scientists.

Unlike many spiders that primarily capture insects, members of the genus Portia specialize in hunting other spiders. They use a variety of sophisticated strategies to catch their prey, including imitating the vibrations made by struggling insects trapped in spiderwebs.

These deceptive signals can lure web-building spiders toward what appears to be an easy meal, only for them to encounter the hunting Portia instead.

Such complex behaviors suggest that these spiders have unusual learning abilities, making them valuable subjects for investigating the relationship between sleep, nervous system function, and cognition.

Scientists Discover Two Distinct Stages of Spider Sleep

Although sleep has been extensively studied in humans and other vertebrates, scientists still know relatively little about how it works in animals with very different nervous systems.

To investigate whether spiders experience genuine sleep and whether that sleep includes different stages, the researchers closely monitored Portia fimbriata over several days and nights.

They discovered that the spiders exhibit two clearly distinguishable sleep stages. One of these resembles REM sleep, the phase in humans associated with rapid eye movements, muscle activity, and vivid dreaming.

During this REM-like stage, the spiders displayed characteristic eye movements and muscle twitches. These observations suggest that some features of sleep previously studied mainly in vertebrates may also occur in spiders.

The researchers emphasize that identifying REM-like movements does not establish whether spiders dream. However, the discovery provides important evidence that sleep in these animals is more complex than previously understood.

How Researchers Confirmed That Spiders Really Sleep

To study the spiders' sleeping behavior, the team placed individual Portia fimbriata spiders in plastic dishes measuring 12 centimeters in diameter. They monitored the animals under different lighting conditions to examine their daily activity and rest cycles.

Infrared cameras allowed the researchers to observe the spiders throughout the night without relying on visible light. Detailed recordings captured their sleeping positions, periods of quiet rest, and episodes of REM-like movement.

The team also conducted experiments to determine whether the spiders were genuinely asleep rather than simply sitting motionless.

Using sound stimuli, the researchers measured how quickly the spiders responded to disturbances. These tests helped distinguish sleep from ordinary inactivity.

Another important part of the investigation involved sleep deprivation. The researchers examined whether the spiders displayed "sleep rebound," meaning they compensated for interrupted sleep by increasing their subsequent sleep.

Together, the behavioral observations and experimental tests provided strong evidence that Portia fimbriata experiences regulated sleep with distinct stages.

Studying Spider Sleep Presented Unusual Challenges

Determining whether a spider is truly asleep is considerably more complicated than observing a sleeping human.

Because spiders sense their surroundings differently from people, the researchers first had to identify a sound stimulus that would produce a consistent response during their experiments.

They also needed to avoid assuming that the observed eye movements and muscle twitches necessarily represented the same processes seen during human REM sleep.

One major limitation was that the researchers could not measure the spiders' brain activity. Instead, they relied entirely on carefully documented behavior to identify different sleep states.

This required extensive high-resolution video recordings collected over many days and nights, producing enormous amounts of data for analysis.

Despite these obstacles, the combination of behavioral monitoring, response testing, and sleep disruption experiments allowed the researchers to build a detailed picture of how these spiders sleep.

What Spider Sleep Could Reveal About Evolution

The discovery has implications that extend well beyond spiders.

Most scientific knowledge about sleep comes from studying vertebrates, particularly mammals and birds. Finding comparable sleep features in animals with radically different nervous systems could help researchers understand when and why different types of sleep evolved.

The results also open new opportunities in behavioral science, neuroscience, and chronobiology, the study of biological rhythms.

By investigating sleep in a wider variety of animals, scientists hope to identify features that are shared across species and uncover the fundamental biological functions that sleep may have served early in evolutionary history.

The researchers argue that future investigations should move beyond traditionally studied animals and include species with unusual adaptations or much simpler nervous systems.

Such comparisons could reveal whether certain sleep mechanisms are widespread throughout the animal kingdom or developed independently in different evolutionary lineages.

Even after decades of intensive research, many basic questions about sleep remain unanswered, including important mysteries surrounding human sleep.

Could Spiders Dream? Scientists Have More Questions

The University of Bonn team is now expanding its research to investigate how sleep, particularly REM-like sleep, varies across different spider species.

Scientists are also comparing spiders sleeping in natural environments with those observed in laboratories to determine how surroundings influence their behavior.

Another major goal is to investigate the relationship between sleep and cognitive abilities. Researchers want to understand what happens inside the brains of spiders and insects during sleep, and whether different sleep stages contribute to learning and other mental processes.

Environmental changes are also becoming an important focus. The team plans to examine how climate change and human activities, especially artificial light at night (ALAN), may interfere with the sleep patterns of spiders and other arthropods.

For now, the findings establish that even a tiny jumping spider can experience a surprisingly complex form of sleep. Whether spiders actually dream remains unknown, but their nighttime behavior is offering scientists new clues about one of biology's most enduring mysteries.

Playing Chekov in "Star Trek" was far from Walter Koenig's only contribution to the science fiction genre. Sadly, Koenig, the original Chekov on "Star Trek," has died at the age of 90. While we'll always remember him for what he did within the landscape of that franchise, he certainly touched the genre elsewhere. "Babylon 5" is a prime example. But in the late '80s, he starred alongside another genre icon in a forgotten sci-fi movie.

Directed by Robert Dyke, 1989's "Mooncrash" starred Koenig as an astronaut with none other than "Evil Dead" franchise star Bruce Campbell playing his counterpart. One sci-fi icon and one horror icon came together for one oft-forgotten relic of genre filmmaking.

The movie centers on astronauts (Koenig and Campbell) who are sent to the moon to investigate what appears to be signs of life. They reanimate the body of a woman (Leigh Lombardi) who warns them that the moon is under the control of a race of alien cyborgs, who are waiting for the opportunity to invade Earth. They realize that desperate measures must be taken to prevent the cyborgs from leaving the moon

The premise is intriguing and its stars seem like they would make for a fun match. However, while there are plenty of '80s sci-fi movies that deserved more attention, the consensus seems to be that this isn't one of them.

"Moontrap" was a relatively low-budget affair and went direct-to-video. It also came at a unique time for both of its main stars.

Walter Koenig was in the midst of his later era as Chekov, with this movie released right between "Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home" and "Star Trek V: The Final Frontier." This was as the OG crew of the Enterprise was starting to run out of steam. William Shater has apologized for "Star Trek V: The Final Frontier," as it's not terribly well-regarded. The larger point is that, for as much as Trekkies love "The Voyage Home," it could be viewed as the last hoorah of the franchise's original cinematic era.

As for Bruce Campbell, he was firmly a B-movie actor at this time. Not yet a legend and still a working man's actor, he wasn't exactly in a position to be picky about the roles he was taking. The "Maniac Cop" trilogy was ahead of its time and Campbell was in those movies around this time as well, but they weren't exactly mainstream hits. He also starred in the underrated slasher "Intruder" in '89 which, again, wasn't a blockbuster by any stretch.

There's very little by way of critical opinion available online when it comes to "Moontrap," but it holds a not-so-good 27% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes. More than anything, it's a forgotten relic of '80s sci-fi that was largely allowed to exist thanks to the advent of VHS and the home video market. In there here and now, it would probably be a direct-to-streaming release on Shudder or something along those lines.

#103. Chinese markets lose trillions as race for AI supremacy with US takes its toll

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Lack of profits and the threat posed by western countries are raising concern about the sustainability of Beijing’s AI boom

Trillions of dollars have been wiped from China’s stock markets in recent months amid increasing anxiety about the future of artificial intelligence, as Beijing races against the United States for dominance in the sector.

The STAR 50 Index, which tracks technology and innovation companies on the Shanghai stock exchange, has plunged 40% since June. Other indexes of major companies dropped by about 20% over the same time period.

The precipitous drop has wiped out $2.4tn from the Chinese stock market since May, according to Bloomberg.

AI stocks account for over 40% of the stock market value in China and Hong Kong, according to Goldman Sachs, meaning that any dent in value will send shockwaves across the whole domestic market.

The fall suggests that despite the government’s best efforts to boost China’s technology industries, particularly those which harness the power of AI, the domestic economy does not have the power to sustain a booming market.

The Chinese government has invested heavily into AI, including a 60bn yuan state-backed AI investment fund which started pumping money into domestic developers this month. But Chinese consumers have proved stubbornly reluctant to part with their cash in many parts of the economy – including technology – meaning that many tech companies struggle to turn a profit.

The lack of profits is one of several reasons for the sell-off. Another is the threat of western countries, especially the US, extending bans on Chinese technology.

“This year, the profit margin is not as good as expected. And the US is raising rates. And China’s domestic demand is so weak,” said Dan Wang, the China director for the Eurasia Group. She said that there was a “general anxiety” among technology companies about whether or not the state-backed model would work in the face of weak consumer demand.

The US has banned humanoid robots from China, citing national security risks, and is thought to be considering a ban on Chinese components in data centres. China is heavily reliant on exports and restrictions in the US hurt its domestic companies, which struggle to sell enough products at home.

As China races with the US to pull ahead in AI, Beijing has doubled down on robotics – also known as embodied AI – as the key to rolling out the technology across society. Robotics is also seen as the answer to China’s demographic problems, such as the ageing population and the shrinking workforce.

But the demand for robotics is being driven by government agencies and research institutions, rather than everyday consumers, for whom the products are still too expensive and not yet practical for home use. Humanoid robotics and robotic dogs have been popular in the entertainment industry but are yet to be useful in home settings.

Shares in Unitree, China’s leading robot-maker, have halved since they started trading in August.

“It doesn’t look like embodied intelligence could turn into profits in the in the mass market,” said Wang. Thin profit margins, rising rates in the US, and weak domestic demand were all contributing to a “general anxiety” among technology companies, she said.

China’s government wants local investors to invest their money domestically. China has in recent months restricted overseas investment, with more regulations taking effect from 1 December, but these moves have not led to greater investment in the Chinese markets.

Wang added that there was still optimism in other technology sectors, such as AI research and satellites, the latter because their links to the military indicates long term state support for the sector. And globally the AI boom is expected to last for at least another year.

On Monday, US AI company Anthropic, which makes the chatbot Claude, filed paperwork ahead of a planned IPO that could be the biggest stock market debuts of all time. The company is expected to bring in as much as $100bn.

China has no such blockbuster IPOs on the horizon. And despite pressure on policymakers to introduce stimulus measures to boost investment, none are expected to be announced during a politically sensitive period in China.

This week the top officials in the Chinese Communist party are gathering for the fifth plenum, an important meeting that will lay the groundwork for next year’s 21st Party Congress. The Congress is expected to anoint Xi Jinping, China’s leader, for a fourth term. There is no appetite for risky or potentially destabilising policy measures before then, even if there is a chance they could boost the economy.

Any stimulus measure would be “high risk”, Wang said. “What if it doesn’t work out? Then whoever came up with that policy, that guy would be held accountable”.

#4244. Roco Kingdom preview - "The monster taming RPG I've long been waiting for"

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

When you think about magic academies, you almost always assume it has something to do with a skinny boy in round glasses getting whisked away by a friendly bearded giant to a land of witchcraft and wizardry. And when you think about creature collecting, it's always about a boy in a red cap stuffing capture balls in his backpack and setting off from his small town to discover the world.

It's incredibly refreshing, then, to have both of these elements combined in Roco Kingdom, where you enrol in a magic academy not to learn spells with a magic wand but to catch the aforementioned creatures in the wild - red cap and round glasses optional.

Catching 'em all, finally I'm not going to beat around the bush - I honestly think that Roco Kingdom is the creature-collecting game I've been waiting for. Too many entries in the genre have tried to emulate that Pokémon feel but somehow still manage to either "modernise" it in the wrong way or end up copying the popular Nintendo property. But what MoreFun Studios has done here is take the monster-taming experience and improve it in more ways than one, and I absolutely love the result.

For one thing, the creatures themselves - called Jinies - don't look like distant cousins of Pikachu and the gang. And for another, despite the "Pokeballs" still being, well, balls, they're magical in nature, which is perfectly in sync with the magical theme of the world of Carosia.

And because it's an open world, you get to venture out from your magic school and really explore every nook and cranny of the fantasy land laid out beautifully before you. Carosia's colourful setting has effectively reignited that sense of excitement inside me that disappointing games in the genre have long since snuffed out. It's been a long time since I really felt the desire to explore the world around me, and the equally colourful Jinies in the wild only add to that exhilaration.

Because yes, it really is all about the creature-collecting here - not the crafting, not the farming, and certainly not the tedious resource gathering. While there's the option to build your own home and decorate and farm and all that, that's exactly it - optional. Building your home is a side-quest, and farming crops equally so. Roco Kingdom, from the get-go, wants to tell you that it's not a survival crafting or cosy life sim - it's a monster-taming RPG first and foremost, and at its purest and most beautiful form.

And that - to someone like me who's never been a fan of life sims and all the tedious grinding and crafting that come with them - is a humongous sigh of relief. Jini (not) in a bottle As for the Jinies themselves, they're really just living their best lives out in the wild, each one doing their own thing. You spot some of them goofing around with each other or travelling in packs, and whenever you approach, they can either run away, attack you, or just think you're totally "meh". I love how natural this living, breathing world feels - almost like the Jinies' lives don't revolve around you. Shocking!

You can try to aim your adorable star-themed wand at them to stun them before you approach, as bumping into them triggers turn-based combat. If they're stunned, they'll be Dizzy and will miss the first turn, giving you the upper hand in battle. You might want to sneak up behind them too to further boost your advantage, which is handy if you ever want to weaken them before catching.

If you defeat them instead, you'll gain experience points for your Jinies, which you'll need to level them up and eventually evolve them. I didn't feel like levelling up was a chore either, because, like the devs during the media brief I attended said, they wanted to make sure that players can focus on the fun of collecting and levelling up their Jinies. Because if they added all the grindiness that usually comes with evolving Jinies, then what would be the point, right?

And I appreciate that decision so very much, because it seems like the team really wants us to enjoy the creature-collecting aspect of the RPG the most. And right now, I think it's working - I spent a good chunk of my entire weekend just exploring Carosia and catching all sorts of Jinies I spotted in the wild. And they're incredibly adorable too, because you can pet them and feed them to give them the TLC they deserve. It's a not-so-small world, after all

Outside of the very, very strategic combat (thanks to all the distinct skills they have), Jinies also help you out when you're adventuring, as you'll often stumble upon unknown points of interaction in the wild. This usually involves a chest or a shiny spot that can only be "activated" when you chuck a specific Jini element at it, or you can mount your Jini to swim, ride through open fields or glide through the skies from one cliff to the next.

Sometimes, mounting a Jini means they actually mount you by either propping themselves up on your shoulder or having you carry them instead, which is just the most wholesome thing ever, and something other monster-taming adventures out there should definitely have more of.

You'll also encounter a bunch of secret spots and intriguing side-quests while you're roaming around the world, like this Adventurous Duckiji I happened to chance upon. It was teetering on the edge of a cliff while staring intently at something, and right next to it was a bunch of wooden planks nailed together with a drawing of a Duckiji haphazardly put together. It also had a "Satisfied Treasure Hider" description on it - oh, the horrors this Duckiji must have seen!

If you ever get tired of exploring the land, you can rest in the Manaboughs you've activated, which act as save points that let you recover your HP and Vitality. You can also pass the time here, as various Jinies will only pop up during the day/night. There are Nightmare versions of Jinies and Alpha Jinies to take on, Jini Blossom challenges to defeat, and Outdoor Duels to accept - really, there's no limit to what you can do out there, so it's hard to believe it's all free to play. The best things in life are free Of course, this is where concerns about monetisation creep in, but the devs have assured us that microtransactions will only be for cosmetics, and everything can be traded and obtained through gameplay - which is incredibly generous, to be honest. And for the quality of what I've played and the huge amount of content on offer, I'd even say it might be worth making this a premium game instead. Again, the devs have said that there's really no point in paywalling the Jinies because it would defeat the whole purpose of the RPG.

I suppose it's pretty obvious by now that Roco Kingdom has indeed won my heart from this early access build alone. The whole experience feels wonderfully polished, the voice acting is stellar, and the soundtrack is just superb. I wouldn't mind having the music tracks (that you can collect too!) playing in the background on loop while I get some work done during the day.

There's a bit of a multiplayer element here too, by the way, since you can be invited into another player's world by holding hands with them and adventuring together. I admit I wasn't able to experience this much because I was far too engrossed in doing my own thing for hours on end, but it's a nice option for social butterflies looking to play with their besties online.

No grinding, no RNG progression, and mobile-optimised with full cross-platform play - suffice it to say that Roco Kingdom is one to watch when it launches in early to mid-2027. We were told that the global version will have its first two seasons before it syncs up with the Chinese version, but if you're keen on getting first dibs, the Closed Beta Test - adorably dubbed the "Quack Quack Test" - starts today!

#4886. Ripple gets a final go-ahead to launch its own U.S. national trust bank

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ripple, a fintech company building blockchain and crypto for cross-border transactions, and tokenization, can now run a specialized bank under U.S. federal supervision.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the regulator that oversees national banks, has approved Ripple National Trust Bank (RNTB), Ripple’s CEO Brad Garlinghouse announced on X late Monday.

In other words, one of crypto’s biggest players now has a traditional banking-style license, signaling that digital assets are being pulled further into the regulated traditional financial system. Ripple first applied for the banking license in July 2025, and the OCC gave it conditional approval that December before signing off fully this week.

The approval is consistent with Garlinghouse’s long-running pitch that crypto companies should build products that banks and big institutions can actually use.

The RNTB will be tasked with managing the reserves backing Ripple’s RLUSD stablecoin. These reserves are essentially real money and assets that provide the token its value.

The bank will also act as a trustee for these reserves and offer regulated custody services, which means it will safely hold digital assets for big companies as they use crypto in payments, trading, and treasury management.

RLUSD will still be issued by Ripple’s New York-regulated unit, Standard Custody & Trust Company. The new bank adds another layer of oversight: RLUSD now has both state and federal regulators watching over parts of its setup.

RLUSD debuted in December 2024 and has since grown to a market value of about $2.5 billion, putting it among the world's top 50 stablecoins. It is used in Ripple’s payments business and as collateral on Ripple Prime. The stablecoin is backed by U.S. dollar deposits, U.S. government securities, and other cash-like assets. BNY holds most of those reserves. Ripple also uses XRP, the world’s 5th largest digital asset, to facilitate cross-border transactions.

Ripple said it will keep working with the OCC, New York regulators, and others as the bank starts operating, especially as the U.S. rolls out its new stablecoin rules under the GENIUS Act.

It is 25 years since Owen’s award and 70 years since Matthews won the inaugural Ballon d’Or, the serendipitous anniversary that brought this ceremony from Paris to Kane’s hometown.

It had been a tense wait over recent weeks as 100 journalists around the world decided the best player of the 2025/26 season. Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal turned on the PR campaigns in an effort to woo swing voters: Mbappe played to the African bloc while Yamal tried to directly undermine Kane’s candidacy, hinting he lacked the “magic” of a true winner.

On the night there was some controversy too, when the Ballon d’Or’s official website published separate articles proclaiming all three favourites had won, hours before the ceremony began. Someone at head office presumably had a heart attack and swiftly deleted them, but not before the internet had screenshotted receipts.

Outside the Palladium there was chaos, as every surrounding street was choked with people poking phones over security barriers to get a digital glimpse of their favourite players. Inside the theatre, an announcement was made of a half-an-hour delay which turned into an hour. A Frenchman named Charlie came out to pump up the audience, calling for “massive energy tonight”, like a beleaguered manager talking to a dressing room he’d long since lost.

When the show finally began, Spain’s Alexia Putellas won her third Ballon d’Or, and Bukayo Saka was recognised for his charity work. Yamal was named the world’s best young player, but he finished behind Kane for the main men’s prize.

No doubt the result will set off sirens of outrage on social media among faceless football accounts, that this was all a conspiracy against Barcelona or Real Madrid, those famously downtrodden institutions. And sure, Yamal and Mbappe will one day be worthy winners themselves.

But amid the heated debate around this year’s award, there was a risk of overthinking it. Kane scored an absurd 73 goals in 64 games, a tally achieved only once before in a top European league, by Lionel Messi, who scored 82 in 2011/12. Cristiano Ronaldo’s best campaign was 69 goals in 69 games, and nobody else has come close.

Kane scored 36 Bundesliga goals despite starting only 25 games. He scored in every round of the German Cup including a hat-trick in the final. He scored in every Champions League knockout game he played, against both Real Madrid and Paris Saint-Germain. He scored six goals in seven World Cup games, including those crucial strikes against Panama, DR Congo and Mexico to keep England’s tournament on track.

Turn the numbers on their head, and had Mbappe scored 73 goals, there would have been no question about the winner here.

Perhaps Kane is a victim of his own reluctance to play the publicity game. Perhaps it is a reflection of a talent which is altogether harder to appreciate than his rivals. His finishing is obvious, but then there’s his movement, the use of his body as a shield, the faintest touches that open a shooting lane only he knew existed.

In some ways, any individual award increasingly jars with modern football, a complex game of carefully choreographed patterns and hyper-tuned systems. The best team in the world are PSG and this is their narrative arc: they signed Messi, Mbappe and Neymar and kept losing; then they sold them, built a team who ran for each other and conquered Europe, twice. Football has arguably never been more collaborative.

But perhaps that is all the more reason to appreciate the Ballon d’Or, a gaudy event of inflated egos and bad tuxedos, but one which remains a celebration of artistry in an increasingly scientific field. Yamal may not appreciate it, but Kane’s art is finishing and this Ballon d’Or is, above all, a monument to his craft.

Kane is something of a rebel figure in that sense, someone not born into this world of gilded superstars but who turned himself into one by sheer force of will. Mbappe and Yamal were World Cup winners by 19 years old, around the same age as Kane was cycling through loan moves to Leyton Orient, Millwall, Norwich and Leicester. They rocketed to stardom while Kane took the Megabus.

Kyle Walker recently described the teenaged Kane as “overweight” and “not very mobile”. Fellow Spurs academy graduate Adam Smith said last month: “I didn’t think he would go to this level, I don’t think anyone thought he would.”

But every coach, teammate and loan manager said the same few things about Kane: that his finishing was peerless, that he was uniquely driven, that he absorbed information and trained relentlessly to improve every aspect of his game. “Even as a 14-year-old, he was always a little bit different,” Smith said. “You know how a lot of youngsters would just follow each other? He’d always do his own thing. He had the mental edge on a lot of players.”

Kane’s self-improvement was not Jake Humphrey chugging Huel at 5am while reading Steve Jobs quotes, but in the intelligent, consistent application to his task. It was in the details, training himself to shoot in the realistic chaos of match conditions, balls bouncing, tight spaces, defenders everywhere. He still trains with mannequins who act as opponents in his periphery.

And as twee as it might sound, he is the very best of English men, a picture of wholesome masculinity. He says he wants to compete until he’s 40, and honestly, who’d bet against him? He says he wants to be a placekicker in the NFL, and if it was anyone else you’d laugh. But it’s Kane, and he’ll probably be kicking field goals in 10 years’ time. He is the sort of man who will spend his retirement doing crosswords while his 17th attempt at olive-infused sourdough bakes in the oven.

He has got here without selling himself to every marketing offer. He has done it all with a lateral lisp that has been ridiculed. His wife Kate has received untold online abuse, and she was here by his side as his career reached its personal pinnacle. “We’re in this together,” Kane said on stage. “I know this wouldn’t be possible without you.”

Watching on, Mbappe applauded with a polite smile. This was meant to be the era of Mbappe v Haaland, and it would have seemed bizarre when he won the 2018 World Cup, aged 19, that he would not have won this award eight years later. Messi’s reign extended longer than anyone imagined and Mbappe is running out of road before the Yamal era begins.

But this was not the year for either. Perhaps this has been Kane’s greatest achievement of all, that his extraordinary goalscoring became so ordinary that his right to win the Ballon d’Or was questioned.

It has been a career spent proving people wrong, from the Arsenal coaches who rejected him as an eight-year-old, to the rival fans who labelled him a one-season wonder or mocked his lack of trophies. He might finally feel vindicated and satisfied by this night. But no doubt Kane collected more fuel here, from anyone who thought he didn’t deserve this stage.

#411. Scientists discover a surprising clue to why some brains resist Alzheimer’s

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Some people have the brain changes associated with Alzheimer's disease but never develop dementia. While others experience worsening memory and thinking problems, these individuals remain mentally sharp despite signs of the disease in their brains. Scientists have long struggled to explain why.

Now, researchers at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience have uncovered a promising clue. Their findings suggest that a rare group of brain cells, known as immature neurons, may behave differently in people who resist Alzheimer's symptoms. Rather than simply replacing damaged cells, these neurons could help protect the brain from the harmful effects of the disease.

This remarkable ability is known as cognitive resilience. It describes how some people maintain their memory and thinking abilities even when their brains show signs of damage or disease.

"Around 30 percent of older adults who develop Alzheimer's disease never experience its symptoms," says Evgenia Salta, senior author of the research. "We really don't know why. That's a big mystery, and a very important one."

Understanding this mystery could transform how scientists approach Alzheimer's, shifting attention from what causes brain cells to deteriorate toward what allows certain brains to remain healthy and functional.

One possible explanation for cognitive resilience is that some brains have a greater ability to repair or protect themselves as Alzheimer's progresses.

"Perhaps they can add new brain cells to a network that is degenerating," Salta says.

This possibility involves a biological process called adult neurogenesis, which refers to the production of new nerve cells (neurons) in an adult brain. Neurons are specialized cells that transmit information throughout the nervous system, allowing us to think, learn, remember, and respond to our surroundings.

Scientists have established that adult neurogenesis occurs in several animal species. Whether it continues to happen in the adult human brain, however, has been a subject of scientific debate for decades.

If the human brain can produce new neurons later in life, those cells might help maintain important connections as aging and disease damage existing networks. However, finding immature neurons does not necessarily prove that they were recently formed.

To investigate this possibility, Salta and her colleagues examined donated human brain tissue obtained through the Netherlands Brain Bank, a research resource that collects and preserves brain samples.

The researchers compared three groups: people without detectable brain pathology, patients who developed Alzheimer's dementia, and individuals whose brains showed Alzheimer's pathology but who remained cognitively resilient.

The team concentrated on a small region within the brain's memory center, an area believed to be among the few locations where new neurons might develop.

Finding the relevant cells was far from straightforward.

"These cells are extremely rare, so we had to develop new ways to find them," Salta says. "We really zoomed in on the exact spot where we expected them to be."

The researchers also developed new approaches to analyzing their data, allowing them to identify the cells more precisely without relying heavily on assumptions drawn from animal studies.

Scientists Find Immature Neurons in Brains Over 80

The search revealed something remarkable. Immature neurons were present across all three groups, including in the brains of older adults.

These cells resemble neurons that have not yet fully matured, suggesting that the aging brain may retain cellular features associated with development and adaptability.

"Even at an average age of over 80, we still found these immature neurons in all groups," Salta says.

But the most intriguing discovery came when researchers compared the brains of people who had developed dementia with those who had remained mentally sharp despite Alzheimer's pathology.

Initially, the scientists expected resilient individuals to have substantially more immature neurons. If these cells help replace damaged neurons, having greater numbers might explain why some brains withstand the disease.

Instead, the difference in cell numbers was much smaller than anticipated.

That unexpected result pointed toward another explanation. The important distinction might not be how many immature neurons a brain contains, but how those cells respond when the brain comes under attack.

A Hidden Defense Against Alzheimer's Damage

When the team examined the activity of immature neurons more closely, they identified striking differences between the groups.

In cognitively resilient individuals, the cells showed signs of activating biological processes associated with survival and protection against damage.

"In resilient individuals, these cells seem to activate programs that help them survive and cope with damage," Salta says. "We also see lower signals related to inflammation and cell death."

Inflammation is part of the body's normal defense system, but when it becomes persistent in the brain, it can contribute to damage and interfere with communication between neurons. Cell death, meanwhile, gradually disrupts the networks responsible for memory and thinking.

The findings suggest that immature neurons in resilient brains may be better equipped to withstand these harmful conditions.

More surprisingly, their role might extend beyond protecting themselves.

"It might not be (only) about replacing lost neurons," Salta explains. "It could be that these cells support the surrounding tissue and help the brain stay functional and 'youthful'. They may act as a sort of fertilizer in a garden that has started falling apart."

In other words, rather than serving only as replacements for dying neurons, immature cells could potentially help maintain the environment surrounding existing brain cells.

Such support might allow neural networks to continue functioning even as Alzheimer's related damage accumulates.

Why Scientists Are Cautious About the Discovery

Although the results offer an intriguing explanation for cognitive resilience, Salta emphasizes that the researchers have not established exactly what these immature neurons do.

Their conclusions about the cells' possible protective roles are based on patterns observed in brain tissue, not direct experiments demonstrating those functions.

"We assume the cells' function based on the data, but we cannot confirm it in this type of study," she explains.

That distinction is particularly important because research on adult neurogenesis has attracted considerable public attention, sometimes leading to claims that go beyond what scientists have demonstrated.

The presence of immature neurons does not establish that new neurons are continuously being produced in older humans, nor does it prove that these cells are responsible for preventing dementia.

Other biological mechanisms almost certainly contribute to cognitive resilience as well.

"This is one piece of a very large puzzle," Salta concludes. "There will never be just one factor that explains resilience."

Could Alzheimer's Research Shift Toward Protecting the Brain?

The findings raise a broader question about aging: Why do some people's brains remain relatively stable while others experience progressive cognitive decline?

"Somewhere along this trajectory, there's a kind of decision point," Salta explains. "Some people remain stable, others develop dementia. We want to understand what drives that difference."

To explore this further, the researchers plan to investigate how immature neurons communicate and interact with neighboring brain cells. Understanding those relationships may reveal whether the cells actively contribute to resilience and how their protective responses might work.

The research also reflects a growing change in how scientists think about Alzheimer's disease.

Traditionally, much of the field has focused on understanding the processes that damage the brain, including the accumulation of abnormal proteins and the loss of neurons. Increasingly, researchers are also asking why some brains remain functional despite those changes.

By identifying the mechanisms behind this natural resistance, scientists hope to uncover new ways to preserve memory and cognitive abilities as people age.

"Cognitive resilience is extremely exciting," Salta says. "If we understand what protects these brains, it could eventually lead to new therapeutic strategies."

Although practical treatments remain a distant possibility, the discovery suggests that even in old age, the human brain may possess protective abilities that scientists are only beginning to understand.

For most of the world, the diplomatic spat between the United States and Panama—over U.S. President Donald Trump’s incorrect claim in his 2025 inaugural speech that China controlled the Panama Canal and that he would retake it—has been resolved. Following the president’s threat, the Trump administration negotiated access for U.S. military training in the Panamanian jungle and Panama’s Supreme Court overturned contracts for two Hong Kong-owned port facilities.

For Panamanians, however, the dispute around the canal continues. Only the White House has moved on.

Between March and July 2026, more than 400 Panama-flagged ships were detained at Chinese ports. The unprecedented rate in detentions began on March 8, reportedly following what the U.S. Federal Maritime Commission has called “informal directives” intended to “punish” Panama-flagged ships. (China’s ambassador to the United States has denied that Panamanian ships are being targeted and says that the increased inspections are due to fishing boat collisions.)

By April, China was responsible for 91 percent of all detentions of Panama-flagged ships across the Asia-Pacific region. Fearing additional detentions and penalties from China, an estimated 500 ships left Panama’s registry between the end of 2025 and this August—about 12.3 percent of the country’s total fleet by tonnage.

Though not yet an open trade war, Beijing’s recent actions against Panama signal a new way of punishing countries for crossing Chinese interests. Beijing is applying economic pressure through the infrastructure of trade: shipping, ports, and logistics.

With the world focused on another maritime chokepoint, Panama is paying the price for angering Beijing. And as freedom of navigation is very much in the news, the implications are broader still. Lawfare now encompasses maritime lawfare as well, and Panama and the United States cannot address this issue alone.

The China-Panama dispute originated from a much broader fight between the United States and China over commercial control of a crucial waterway. At the start of the second Trump administration, the United States repeatedly accused Beijing of exercising undue influence over the Panama Canal, which moves more than 40 percent of all U.S. container traffic.

At the center of the dispute were two port facilities at opposite ends of the waterway: Balboa on the Pacific coast and Cristóbal on the Atlantic. Both terminals were operated by Panama Ports Company, a subsidiary of Hong Kong-based CK Hutchison. Together, they occupy strategically important positions along one of the world’s busiest maritime routes, but in neither case were they operating the canal. Nonetheless, U.S. lawmakers have mentioned the possibility that the facilities were being or could be used to listen to sensitive communications.

In response to the Trump administration’s concerns and warnings, Panamanian officials began to look into the contract between the company and the Panamanian government. The concession, originally granted in 1997 and extended in 2021, was already controversial in Panama amid suspicions of backroom deals in the extension of the contract.

Later, in an audit of the concession, Panama’s comptroller alleged that unpaid dues and improperly granted tax exemptions had cost the country approximately $1.3 billion. On Jan. 30 of this year, Panama’s Supreme Court ruled the original concession and its renewal unconstitutional, ending the legal basis for operating the two terminals.

The Panamanian government took control of the ports and assigned interim operations to two other major shipping companies, both European: Maersk and MSC. Beijing condemned the court ruling as an act of bad faith, attributing it to U.S. pressure. CK Hutchison, meanwhile, rejected the takeover, calling it unlawful, and initiated arbitration proceedings through the International Chamber of Commerce, though there has been little progress so far.

Shortly afterward, Chinese authorities dramatically ramped up their detention of Panama-flagged vessels. In March, China’s Ministry of Transport summoned executives from Maersk and MSC and demanded that the companies withdraw from Balboa and Cristóbal. Chinese firm COSCO Shipping Lines suspended its container services at Balboa, while other Chinese state-owned firms paused new investments in Panama.

In April, Chinese Premier Li Qiang signed a State Council order expanding Beijing’s authority to retaliate against foreign regulatory actions deemed harmful to Chinese interests. The measure added to concerns that China was developing a broader legal and administrative toolkit for responding to disputes with other countries.

Administrative measures such as the ones that China is using to justify stopping the Panama-flagged ships impose significant economic costs while preserving the appearance of routine regulation. China’s efforts are technically legal; they just exploit customs requirements to create supply chain delays and cascading trade disruptions that impose punitive costs on those caught in Beijing’s sights—in this case, Panamanian shipping.

A ship held in port for additional inspections may not make the headlines, but the delay and the signal that it sends to shipping companies is expensive. The stopping of ships for inspection disrupts schedules, raises insurance and operating costs, and can leave cargo waiting for days or weeks.

This is no small threat for one of the world’s logistical leaders. The Panamanian government earns around $125 million to $150 million annually from licensing the 7,700 to 8,500 ships that fly its flag, historically registering more than any other country. For China, it’s a subtle way to punish the country whose Supreme Court overturned concessions for CK Hutchinson on either end of the Panama Canal.

In the long term, the costs to Panama could be much greater. Panama’s registry value relies on its reputation for neutrality and predictability. If shipowners begin to view the Panama flag as politically risky, the erosion of Panama’s market share could be worse than the immediate loss of registration fees.

The pressure has extended into ship financing. According to reports, some Chinese leasing companies have begun requiring shipowners to reflag their vessels away from Panama as a condition of obtaining new financing. As the second-biggest user of the canal after the United States, China accounts for more than one-fifth of all the canal’s cargo traffic.

Response to China has been mostly limited to shipping circles. In April, the United States, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Guyana, Paraguay, and Trinidad and Tobago issued a joint statement supporting Panama’s sovereignty. And in July, the U.S. Federal Maritime Commission threatened to investigate what it described as the “weaponization of port state control inspections” against Panama-flagged vessels. So far, it has failed to spark any change.

Panama’s experience raises a difficult question: How can countries respond when the rules and infrastructure of global commerce become instruments of geopolitical pressure? It’s a challenge that’s not unique to Panama; recently, the Trump administration has been raising concerns about the Chinese-built and now partially owned Chancay port in Peru.

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) would be a natural starting point for investigating this new form of maritime lawfare. While the body focuses on maritime safety, port-state control, and the development of common standards for international shipping, given China’s targeted efforts to single out individual governments for retribution, member states should consider strengthening existing procedures to identify unusual patterns of vessel inspections and detentions.

Likewise, the Word Trade Organization (WTO) provides a forum for resolving disputes over measures that violate international trade agreements. However, current shipping dispute is focused on the infrastructure of trade rather than the regulating of ships and their cargo. And, of course, the WTO has also been severely weakened by the United States.

If it should want to, the White House could engage in efforts to strengthen the WTO’s dispute-settlement system and ensure that members have effective avenues for challenging measures that fall within its rules. In this, the WTO and IMO could play complementary roles: the former in disputes covered by trade agreements and the latter in maritime regulation and inspection practices.

Expecting this administration to lean into the multilateral system to address this fallout from its own actions is, at best, difficult to imagine. But the Trump administration has turned to multilateral organizations when it has determined that they could further specific interests, as in Haiti. In that case, after resolutions for a U.N. peacekeeping mission for Haiti were vetoed by China and Russia, the United States and other countries crafted a U.N. Security Council resolution to create a Gang Suppression Force that is technically separate from the U.N.

Another more positive approach is to compete with China in building ports and infrastructure that challenge China’s ability to threaten supply chains.

Research shows that Chinese contractors’ participation in regional infrastructure projects in Latin America and the Caribbean has increased by more than 50 percent in the past five years. Infrastructure has now displaced energy as the most important sector for investments from Chinese firms. And while the United States remains the biggest investor in Latin America by far, averaging about $28.7 billion per year in greenfield investments, these investments are mostly focused on energy, digital infrastructure, manufacturing, and services.

Beijing has also invested in a number of strategically important ports that bracket Panama throughout Central America and the Caribbean. On the Pacific Ocean, this string of oﬃcial investments includes ports in Nicaragua, El Salvador, and Colombia.

Institutions such as the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC), regional development banks, and private investors incentivized by multilateral seed capital should focus on port capacity and alternative shipping connections. But if the DFC is to play a role, it will need to step up its attention and deals.

There is also a more ad hoc, multinational option. Other countries with maritime interests have a shared stake in ensuring that international shipping remains reliable and that commercial disputes do not become a pretext for discriminatory treatment by Beijing or other capitals.

Over the past two years, countries such as Panama, Greece, and Denmark have co-sponsored proposals for global maritime governance and freedom of navigation within the United Nations Security Council. With U.S. diplomatic, technical, and financial support, these countries could work together to improve information sharing, coordinate responses to unusual inspection patterns, and develop common expectations for transparency and due process.

There is one remaining wrinkle. Under the 1977 Panama Canal Neutrality Treaty, the United States and Panama committed to maintaining the canal’s permanent neutrality and ensuring peaceful transit for vessels of all nations on equal terms. China is not a party to the treaty. Although the agreement does not govern commercial disputes, it underscores the United States’ long-standing treaty-based interest in the canal remaining open, under which—despite Trump’s bombast—the U.S. Navy and U.S.-flagged merchant marine vessels have always paid a lower toll.

More attention on Beijing’s retaliatory tactics is needed, not just for Panama but also for other governments that seek to minimize Chinese influence in their economies and infrastructure. The United States, alongside other countries, should reinforce international means to respond.

In a Monday research report, StoneX Senior Equity Research Analyst Mark Palmer reiterated a Buy rating on the stock. Strive's ASST shares lost 9.15% to close at $26.32 on Monday, putting the price target 63% above their current prices.

On Oct. 20, Strive announced that it raised $630 million in gross proceeds after closing an upsized initial public offering of 7 million shares of its 15% SATS preferred stock. Strive's traditional warrants also expired during the same week, according to its filing.

"Taken together, we view the two events as a clean reset of the company's capital structure that underlines its durable, all-preferred bitcoin treasury model," Palmer said.

The analyst noted that the company has spent the past year proving that it can finance bitcoin acquisitions entirely with perpetual preferred equity, "with its SATA preferreds doing the heavy lifting."

"Now, with the initial public offering of SATS, the company is showing that it can run two preferreds side by side, each built for a different kind of income investor," he added.

SATS carries a 15% annual dividend, and the company has set aside enough to cover the first 18 months of payments. SATA, meanwhile, is the firm's earlier variable-rate Series A perpetual preferred stock.

Palmer also said that SATA holders should view the introduction of SATS as a positive. "Because SATS sits beneath SATA in terms of priority, every dollar it raises adds assets without adding claims ahead of the senior preferreds," the analyst added.

Separately, Strive announced Monday that it purchased 6,182 BTC between Oct. 17 and Oct. 23, bringing its total holdings to 43,000 BTC. That ranked it fourth among public corporate bitcoin holders, according to Bitcointreasuries data.

#5414. Wouldn’t It Be Funny If Audi Manufactures EVs In The US?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Audi is the only upscale automaker without a factory of its own in the US, and that is about to change. On Saturday, Audi announced plans for a new, $4 billion factory in Peach County, Georgia. US President Donald Trump could not resist making political hay out of the news, but then again, the joke may be on him. Audi’s plan for the US market reportedly includes plug-in hybrids with a battery range of range of 70-80 miles. So much for the war on EVs…

Plug-in hybrid EVs have fielded a fair share of criticism for only going halfway — or less — down the decarbonization trail. However, here in the US where long distance commutes are common, plug-in hybrids provide drivers with a gasoline-soaked security blanket that is difficult to shake. For those with less onerous driving schedules, the high cost of gas provides ample incentive to log as many miles on battery power as possible.

Regardless of driver habits in the US, Audi CEO Gernot Döllner is evidently among those anticipating that the US market will come around to full battery EVs eventually, following the lead of China and Europe. “In China, we see a complete transition to battery electric, and the same is true for Europe in a 10-year perspective,” Döllner told a group of reporters in August, including US News & World Report managing editor Alex Kwanten.

“And we see it totally differently in North America, especially the U.S.; we see for the next 10, 15 years still a dominance in combustion engine and hybrid drivetrains, and we will have to reflect that in our global portfolio,” Döllner added.

While waiting for the US market to come around to full battery EVs, Audi has been leaning on plug-in hybrids as a workaround. “We will take first steps to bring plug-in hybrids to the US,” Döllner affirmed to US News & World Report in July.

“I can definitely say that the RS model that will come to the market will be a plug-in hybrid one, and we are evaluating which other segments also could make sense in bringing that to the U.S. market,” he added.

According to reports, the new Audi RS 5, 630-horsepower high-performance plug-in will be the first to arrive, sometime later this year or early next year. For the performance class, drivers can expect a battery range of 50 miles. Regular hybrids will clock a range of 70-80 miles.

Automotive News also added some details in July. “Audi of America President Vito Paladino confirmed the German luxury brand will offer hybrids beyond the upcoming RS 5 sedan and Nuvolari supercar, which feature different plug-in hybrid systems,” Automotive New summarized in a Facebook post.

“We will have hybrids in our lineup,” Paladino told Automotive News, though reporter Jack Walsworth noted that Paladino was rather vague on the details.

Another detail left dangling is how much, if any, of Audi’s plug-in hybrid lineup for the US will be manufactured at the new US factory. That’s a good question. Maybe, maybe not. Grading of the site scheduled for 2027 and construction will begin the following year. With full production not expected until 2031, the timeline provides Audi with some wiggle room to adjust its electrification strategy for the US market.

The office of outgoing Georgia Governor Brian Kemp (he’s term-limited) issued a celebratory press release on October 24 that also suggests Audi may adjust its plans as the market evolves, noting that the new factory “will be designed around Audi’s future manufacturing requirements, with a focus on efficiency, flexibility, and the ability to respond to evolving customer needs and market demand.”

“In choosing Georgia, Audi cited speed-to-market, workforce, access to consumer markets, and proximity to an established automotive supply chain,” the Governor’s office further explained, while also highlighting the 4,500 new jobs Audi is expected to bring to the state.

Left unmentioned in the October 24 press release was Georgia’s vanguard position in the domestic EV manufacturing field. That’s a funny position for a politically conservative state to be in, but Governor Kemp has been among the rare Republican office holders to support the vehicle electrification movement.

If the Audi factory does churn out hybrid EVs in Georgia, it will be in good company. “Since 2018, 35 EV-related projects have contributed $23 billion in investments in Georgia,” the Georgia Department of Economic Development reported back in 2023.

And then there’s EV startup Rivian. In November of 2024, Rivian scored a $6.5 billion loan from the Biden administration to build a new factory in Georgia. Somehow the automaker managed to keep much of the funding after Trump took office and began waging war against EVs. A groundbreaking ceremony took place in September of 2025, and if all goes according to plan, Rivian’s electric cars will roll off the assembly line in 2028.

As for Trump, EVs were probably not top of mind when he stumped for Republican candidates at a rally in Perry,  Georgia on Saturday. Somehow he convinced Döllner to step on stage with him to announce the new factory.

Döllner’s appearance with Trump was met with a storm of criticism from both sides of the pond, including from Christiane Benner, who heads the leading German trade union IG Metall and also sits on the supervisory board of Volkswagen as its deputy chair.

“Christiane Benner…called Döllner’s US appearance ‘extremely embarrassing, politically unacceptable and unworthy of the company, but above all of Audi’s employees,” The Guardian reported on October 26.

“She accused Döllner of being in ‘flagrant breach’ of the company’s code of conduct, ‘which expressly requires political neutrality in the run-up to elections,’” The Guardian added.

The Guardian further noted that Benner threw down the gauntlet, advising that the supervisory board is supposed to be involved in major decisions like the Georgia factory.

Oopsies! Maybe that new factory won’t get built after all. Adding insult to injury, The Guardian notes that Volkswagen “aims to slash tens of thousands of positions and close plants in Germany.”
