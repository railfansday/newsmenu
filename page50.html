#3258. GLP-1 drug coverage is being dropped by employer plans: What to know about 2027 weight-loss options

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

To learn more about the CNBC CFO Council, visit cnbccouncils.com/cfo

With open enrollment season in swing, many employees may notice something conspicuously missing from their benefits menu — coverage of GLP-1 drugs for weight loss.

More companies are dropping coverage completely or offering employees less generous payouts. In some cases, they're shifting to a different model altogether — offering employees a consolation prize of sorts — access to a weight management program through a third party.

Coverage of these drugs for weight loss is pricey for employers — typically costing in the mid-$500s to mid-$600s per user monthly. Some companies say whatever the price, covering the drugs is worth it based on employee health. "We spend about $250 million or more on GLPs, and that's up from zero" four or five years ago, Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan told CNBC's Andrew Ross Sorkin in August. "We see a great impact on the employees. ... Believe me, we're pounding everybody on price and trying to get as cheap [as possible]. But our view is that [because of] the long-term health benefits, plus there may be more short-term health benefits ... it's a good investment."

Nevertheless, as healthcare costs continue to climb, more employers have been looking for an exit ramp on GLP-1 coverage for weight loss, and that includes bigger companies. A WTW annual health benefits survey fielded over the summer and being released in full on Wednesday found that 53% of employers with more than 100 employees cover GLP-1s for weight loss, down from 57% in 2025. That's the first decline in three years. What's more, 16% of employers said they cover the drugs now but are considering removing them, up from 9% last year.

According to a recent Marsh survey of over 1,800 US employers, health insurance cost per employee is expected to rise 8.2% on average in 2027, the biggest increase since 2003, with GLP-1 use accounting for a full percentage point of the overall cost growth. Its experts have cited the soaring costs among reasons employers are considering dropping coverage for 2027.

"Employers are really balancing empathy with economics," said Caroline Susie, principal in Marsh's total health management practice.

Some workers who use employer-sponsored health plans are being provided with options in the event of GLP-1 coverage being dropped, though nothing may match full health insurance coverage. Here are alternatives to expect.

Some employers are directing workers to buy GLP-1s through direct-to-consumer options like Novo Nordisk's NovoCare or Eli Lilly's LillyDirect. WTW's survey showed that 8% of employers have gone this route, and 28% were planning or considering it.

Seth Friedman, pharmacy and health plans practice leader at Gallagher, estimated that 20% to 30% of the firm's clients have dropped coverage for 2027 and are sending employees the direct route. For employers, this move can mean significant savings. The percentage of U.S. adults who take GLP-1 medications for weight loss purposes climbed to 11% in 2026, up significantly from 3% in 2024, according to Gallup. What's more, 15% report having used the medicine for weight loss at some point, a nine-percentage-point boost.

"Many people will choose to pay for this on their own even if it's not covered by the plan," said Jeff Levin-Scherz, population health leader at WTW.

The starting price for the lowest dose of Novo's Wegovy for self-pay individuals is currently $149 a month for the pill and $199 a month for the shot. Those prices are subject to change in 2027; Novo Nordisk says current promotional pricing may be modified or canceled. Lilly's Zepbound starts at $299 a month for a single-dose vial or multi-dose KwikPen.

Some companies are moving to a direct-to-employer model, where they drop coverage through their traditional pharmacy program, but offer to reimburse some portion of an employee's out-of-pocket costs.

"These 'alternative coverage' models enable employers to access more advantageous pricing and greater control over their contributions, without covering the drug on their PBM formulary," said a spokesperson for Vida Health, one of multiple vendors that works with employers in this area. Others include Noom and GoodRx.

Employers can often design the program that works for their employees' needs, at a significant savings from paying for GLP-1 drugs through a pharmacy benefit manager, said Alysha Fluno, national pharmacy practice leader at Marsh. A PBM is a third-party administrator that manages prescription drug benefits for health insurers and employers.

Based on direct-to-consumer prices, employers could fully subsidize, pay a vendor fee, and still spend much less than they would to purchase Wegovy, for example, through a PBM, according to WTW's Levin-Scherz.

Some employers are dropping coverage and contracting with third parties like CVS Weight Management, Noom, Omada Health, UnitedHealthcare, Vida and Virta, to offer weight-management programs to employees. Some employers offered these types of programs before, while others made them available only after dropping GLP-1 coverage for weight loss.

According to 2025 data from Marsh, 25% of employers of 500 or more employees had a vendor partner for lifestyle modification and nutrition support. Employers, not employees, typically foot the bill for these programs, at a significant savings compared with GLP-1 coverage. Services can vary by member, but offerings generally include live or online coaching on behavior change, diet, exercise, nutrition and other health topics. Costs for these programs can run about $65 to $100 per employee per month, depending on what's offered, according to Marsh's Susie. Employers who choose a vendor that offers prescription services as well could pay $150 or more per month per participating employee.

Service providers cite good results from their programs in recent years. Data from Omada Health show that 63% of members evaluated at 12 months after stopping a GLP-1 maintained or continued to lose weight. UnitedHealthcare's Real Appeal program data show that 88% of at-risk participants lost weight during the 52-week program designed to promote healthy eating, physical activity, improved well-being and a better health outlook. What's more, 37% of participants lost 5% or more in body weight. Meanwhile, initial program results reported by CVS Weight Management showed 90% weight loss maintenance for members up to 10–12 months after ending drug therapy.

Provider offerings vary somewhat, but professional support is often a hallmark. "We want to make sure that if they go off GLP-1s for whatever reason, they maintain the behavior changes," said Cody Fair, chief commercial officer of Noom. "We're able to keep that weight off because they've retrained their brain and changed their habits," he said.

Employee utilization, however, can be a challenge, said Gallagher's Friedman. "It's absolutely a commitment, and that's probably why you don't see a whole lot do it. Especially when they know they can just have their doctor write a script and go directly to Novo or Lilly."

While these tax-advantaged accounts are not alone a replacement for a comprehensive health plan, GLP-1 medications are eligible for flexible spending account (FSA) and health savings account (HSA) reimbursement when prescribed by a licensed provider to treat a diagnosed medical condition, such as obesity. The disadvantage of using an HSA to pay for GLP-1s is that the dollars invested don't have a chance to grow tax-free for use later on in retirement.

The annual employee contribution limit for an FSA is $3,400 in 2026. For 2026, the maximum annual HSA contribution limits set by the IRS are $4,400 for individuals with self-only coverage and $8,750 for those with family coverage. That will rise in 2027 to $4,500 for individuals and $9,000 for families. Official 2027 IRS contribution limits for FSAs have not yet been announced.

"Benefits are there to cover those out-of-pocket costs," said Andrew Stocker, president of employee benefits at Voya Financial. "Employers need to educate employees about the benefits that do exist."

Macdonald’s teams, whenever the I see them, work hard and play to intelligent, creative patterns. But injuries have hampered Town recently - and you sense, perhaps, that they want to play September football, when the weather is feeling like early winter.

One Saffrons regular summed it up ruefully but succinctly: "We are a step five club, playing at step four."

For the most part, Phantom Blade Zero's epic ambition is exciting—PC Gamer senior guides writer Sean Martin promises me that I've got about a hundred thrilling boss fights left to come. Its first dozen hours also illustrate the dangers of a long-in-development game ballooning in scope.

While playing Phantom Blade Zero I've been thinking a lot about this season's other big action game, which also spent more than half a decade in development: Capcom's revival of Onimusha. That game wastes little time in introducing its protagonist Miyamoto Musashi or the forces he'll be up again. He's brash and headstrong, taunting a group of samurai after whipping up on their master—then leading them on a Looney Tunes-style chase when there are too many to fight at once. The game punctures this silly tone shortly after introducing Musashi's rival, Ganryu, when both are murdered by demonic Genma.

Musashi's saved when he's given an oni gauntlet in the afterlife and sent back to the land of the living to harness souls with it; the rest of the game is about him deciding who and what he fights for. It's about that personal journey first and foremost, with the machinations of the game's villain spurring him along—and fantastic acting and facial motion capture do a ton of the work to make the story compelling.

In Phantom Blade Zero, by contrast, I've already lost track of the tangled web of characters, relationships, betrayals and motivations with dozens of hours to go. I've seen (and played) flashbacks to Soul's childhood, teenage years, and recent past; I've watched cutscenes about his enemy doing some Machiavellian scheming with another guy I can't name; I've read dozens of cryptic messages scattered throughout each zone that are meant to explain more about why I'm fighting puppets, dudes whose bodies have "transcended" into pure energy, monks who coat their bodies in hardened gold, and mean dogs. (Actually, nothing has explained the dogs yet).

I appreciate PBZ's attempt to start in media res and give Soul a sense of urgency—we flash back to him being stabbed through the heart, then shortly after flash forward to him getting a temporary fake heart that will fail on him in 66 days. But the flashbacks and bits of incidental storytelling often feel like infodumps from someone who's spent years writing a massive story bible and has lived with the whole thing in their head for so long that they don't realize how hard it is to follow when you're coming in with zero context.

Take these flowers, Phantom Blade Zero's way of doling out health upgrades. You'll find them scattered around each area, and finding all of them (usually around 3-6) will net you about 10% more health. Each plays 30 seconds or so of dialogue, and the writing's often vague references to characters and events I've never witnessed mostly makes me want to mash the skip button. We're far from the evocative audio vignettes of BioShock, here.

Onimusha: Way of the Sword has some plenty forgettable sidequests, too, but it also features a number of quests that reinterpret Japanese fables written by Lady Murasaki, the author of The Tale of Genji. These sidequests are great, embellished with unique art that inspired me to read more Japanese mythology. Like Onimusha's main story, they're simple and straightforward, but with just enough flair to make them compelling.

So far Phantom Blade Zero is a lot more convoluted than my favorite wuxia films, with small casts of characters and straightforward motivations—a love story, a "save the small town from a corrupt official" story, a ghost story. But when its epic sweep means I get to play with an absolutely luxurious variety of weapons, I'm not exactly heartbroken that the story is less than riveting.

In Onimusha: Way of the Sword, I started the game with sword, and I ended the game with sword. At no point in between did I wield anything but sword.

This is not a complaint. I'm not sure another videogame has done sword better than Onimusha frankly ever—the physicality of it, the dance between blocks, deflects and parries, the lightning strike of an issen—the animations and weighty physics in Onimusha combine to create a game rich in sensory feedback even with none of the complex combos of, say, Devil May Cry.

What makes Phantom Blade Zero special for me are all the ways it isn't a soulslike—it's even less of one than Black Myth Wukong was. It abandons many of those rigid structures in favour of an action experience that focuses more on feeling good. Enemies don't respawn, you don't lose souls on death, if you die on a boss's second phase, it'll still be in its second phase when you return.

Most bosses also don't take more than one or two attempts, but each is long enough that you get to see everything they have and learn their attacks. Most of all, Phantom Blade Zero feels like a kung-fu epic, dripping in martial mastery as your boss parries turn into veritable cutscenes of you styling on them, and Soul is immediately adept in every weapon or Phantom Edge he picks up.

The old Capcom playbook in that vein of character action game is to give you a handful of weapons, each with a lengthy list of button inputs that create slightly different attacks; stringing them together is visually satisfying and keeps your combo counter ticking up and up. Modern Soulslikes offer way more weapons, but at the expense of that rich variety—each weapon type has a couple unique animations to its name, and that's it.

Phantom Blade Zero blends those two approaches, with the weapon variety of a modern action RPG but the feel and combo mad flourishes of 2000s character action games. Each of Soul's weapons has a fairly limited basic combo string, but also wholly unique mechanic that impacts how you use it in a fight. And each weapon has an upgrade path that adds one or two new actions to its repertoire.

The Outlaw Warblade, one of those massive two-handed swords I mentioned at the start, initially drains enemy health with basic attacks, and lets you convert your own health into recoverable damage to perform enhanced guard-breaking attacks. After five upgrades, it gains a new attack that lets you tank damage while you charge it up and instantly restores a chunk of HP if you land the hit. After 15 upgrades, it grants you an "emergency shield" for continuously sapping your own health, rewarding living on the edge.

Like Onimusha, Phantom Blade Zero's boss fights are definitely its highlights, with the arena designs and one-off mocapped stunts making it clear why this game took so long to make. The big difference between the two, though, is how much world PBZ has in and around those fights. So far I've found progression a bit more linear than I hoped, with levels that are Dark Souls-esque but with fewer surprising paths linking back to previous areas. Still, they're peppered with diversions that are closed off until you acquire certain weapons, and lots of overlooks and out-of-reach treasures that taunt you to double-check for routes you might have missed.

It's a way, way more engaging way to spend time between fights than what Onimusha offered in its hub world of Kyoto, and I appreciate that Phantom Blade Zero keeps its map very vague—there's no compass waypoint telling me where to go for a quest. So far the storytelling around these quests has been barebones, but at least I'm not turning my brain off and following an arrow to each and every one.

Instead of designing Phantom Blade's world around paths you'll tread and retread and enemy placements you'll learn to carve your way through, S-Game seems to have opted to craft a 50-hour game by buckling down and making so much stuff. How is it this big without a timesuck of an open world or infinite respawning fodder or roguelike gimmicks? Because it may be the most maximalist action game of its type we've ever seen, from its torrent of text notes to its luxurious animations. Even when it doesn't all work, it's hard not to be won over by a game that goes this all-out.

England will face Australia, Japan and New Zealand throughout November, in a series that represents their final opportunity to test themselves against southern hemisphere opposition before the Rugby World Cup.

It means Borthwick is already keeping a close eye on his players, knowing that he will soon be approaching the selection of his 33-man squad to travel down under and face Tonga, Zimbabwe and Wales in the World Cup pool stage.

Although the challenge is considerable, Borthwick is eager to see how his side perform as players seize their opportunities to prove they deserve to represent England on rugby's biggest stage.

"It is a hugely exciting period. I think this team is an exciting team. It's got so much talent and so many young players coming through with so much ability," Borthwick told Sky Sports.

"As I framed it to the players, we've got 12 Tests now to the World Cup. We have got 10 Tests until I name a 33-man squad that goes to the World Cup.

"I said, effectively, I am not going to bring in a large training squad, I am going to bring in a squad that has everybody in contention for selection for that 33.

"So, there's only nine Tests until I name that squad. This is the last chance we will have the team playing against the Southern Hemisphere opposition before we get to that World Cup.

"I am really looking forward to seeing how the players face against this calibre opponent, this type of opponent, the way they play, coming off the schedule they come off, where they've been in camp for a long period of time and played a number of Test matches.

So, just how close is Borthwick to settling on his strongest side? Perhaps closer than many might expect, with the England head coach making it "clear" that more than 20 players are already firmly in contention for selection. He will continue to back those players as they look to cement their places in the squad.

"I think it is a very consistent feeling. I think out of the 33-man Rugby World Cup squad, I'd be very confident that somewhere in the mid-to-high 20s of players, I think they'll be at the World Cup," added Borthwick.

"I have been very clear to the players about that. If you look at my selections, you will see there's been a lot of consistency of selection and backing the players.

"So, not chopping and changing them from one game to the next, but backing the players to go and perform.

"As with every World Cup squad I've selected, a huge amount of time is spent debating the final few places. Positional versatility and flexibility are invaluable, as are players who are ready to step up and bring the right character to the squad."

In September, reports emerged that Borthwick was set to have his contract extended beyond the 2028 Six Nations in a move designed to avoid uncertainty over his future ahead of and during next year's Rugby World Cup.

The England head coach has retained the backing of the Rugby Football Union (RFU) despite a "challenging" review of the Six Nations campaign, in which England suffered four defeats in five matches.

Although speculation surrounding his future has intensified in recent months, Borthwick insists he has always understood the pressure that comes with leading England and is prepared to embrace the responsibility.

"With the amount of preparation time we have, we need to maximise every minute. So I don't think it really comes into a factor within consideration.

"When you coach in England, the expectation of the millions of people that watch it, it is wonderful.
