#4581. CD Projekt Red says its mysterious Hadar game is its "biggest project to date", but even after "several good years" it's seemingly a long way off

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

"We're not quite at the pre-production stage yet"

CD Projekt Red has described its mysterious new game, which is currently known by its codename Hadar, as its "biggest project to date", revealing it's taken "several good years" to reach its current state, and it's still not even in pre-production.

That's according to CDPR joint-CEO Michał Nowakowski, who told Polish outlet PAP Biznes (translation by DeepL), "At this point we can say that we have a coherent vision of [Hadar's] world and its key elements. The team has started working on the game, although we're not quite at the pre-production stage yet. We're currently developing small prototypes, and we're also testing gameplay elements."

In the four years since its announcement, CDPR has said little about Hadar. However, we do know it won't be based on any existing property, unlike CD Projekt's other major titles - The Witcher having been created by Polish author Andrzej Sapkowski, and Cyberpunk 2077 by TTRPG maker Mike Pondsmith. (Incidentally, I've talked to both Sapkowski and Pondsmith about their work in this regard.)

Earlier this year, Nowakowski shared "an important development update" regarding Hadar's status. "Work on the project is progressing dynamically, and the team has established the foundations of this entirely new IP," Novakowski said in March. "Now, we have clearly defined distinguishing features of this world, and established key pillars that will not only provide the foundation for the game itself, but also, potentially, for other future products." He also mentioned that prototypes were being made in Unreal Engine.

We know from CDPR's most recent financial report in September that there were 33 people working on Hadar as of 31st July, so it's a small project compared to the studio's other titles. 519 people were working on The Witcher 4 at last count, 184 on Cyberpunk 2, and 84 on multiplayer Witcher game Project Sirius. However, Project Sirius was recently hit by layoffs.

Elsewhere in the interview, Nowakowski spoke more about The Witcher 4, which he called "our most ambitious project to date". He also said the studio wanted to "avoid repeating ourselves", so each game should "introduce new narrative and gameplay solutions" as well as "innovations" in main and side quests. "We want players to be confident that when they sit down to play this title, it pushes the boundaries of what an RPG can be and is technologically advanced," he said. The Witcher 4 was recently given a release target of 2028.

He also referenced the recent Remastered update for The Witcher 3, saying the company was "pleased" with sales, which "remain at a higher level than before the remaster", even now the launch discount period has ended. The Witcher 3 broke its Steam concurrent player records after the Remastered edition was released.

Finally, Nowakowski discussed the impact of Sony's decision to phase out PlayStation disc production starting 2028 - putting a possible Witcher 4 physical release in jeopardy. He reiterated much of what he's said before on the matter, saying that because console manufacturers make the discs, there isn't much CDPR can do if they decide to cease production. "A studio doesn't simply decide to release a game and then distribute the physical version itself," he said. "If a particular manufacturer completely shuts down the option to release games on physical media, they simply won't be available."

The latest game to pass a 1 million copies sold milestone is Control Resonant. Developer Remedy Entertainment broke the news yesterday, roughly three weeks after the game's release, revealing that Resonant is now the fastest-selling game in the Finnish studio's 31-year history.

Remedy didn't share a digital/physical split, nor say which platform led sales, but these are good numbers for a Remedy game, and even more impressive considering the unusually packed September release schedule Resonant released into.

Remedy CEO Jean-Charles Gaudechon, who took over as boss of the company in February, called this a "very strong start". "Control Resonant is Remedy's first self-published release of this scale, launched into one of the most competitive windows in years alongside the industry's biggest names," Gaudechon said.

"We have always believed Control deserved an even bigger audience, and this launch shows what's possible: we see the game connecting with long-time fans while bringing a significant number of new players into the world of Control, which strengthens its long-term potential."

Although we don't have like-for-like sales data for the original Control, the game sold 2 million copies by the end of 2020, 15 months after launch. #5178. The Biggest Credit Hurdle for Renters Who Want To Buy

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

What’s the biggest payment you make every month? If you’re a renter, it’s probably your rent.

But did you know that you can pay your rent on time, every time, for years, and it may never show up in your credit history? It’s pretty frustrating, especially if you’d like to buy a home someday and you’re trying to build your credit.

The good thing is that’s changing right now. Here’s what you need to know and why it could open a door you thought was closed.

You’re not the only one frustrated. According to Zillow, nearly a third of applicants (32.88%) get denied a mortgage due to insufficient credit history – not missed payments or too much debt. Just not enough credit history for a lender to say “yes.”

It’s the most common hurdle would-be buyers have. As Zillow explains:

“Nearly one-third of mortgage applicants are denied a loan because of insufficient credit history, making it the most common reason for a denial.”

But there’s finally a way in now that how credit gets measured is changing.

As Michael Sherman, General Manager and Senior VP at Zillow Rentals, explains:

“Affordability is the headline in housing right now, and access to financing is a very important part of the story. Credit history is a barrier standing in the way of too many potential buyers. Rent reporting is a direct way to address that.”

Here’s what Sherman is talking about. New credit models are emerging that take on-time rent and some utility payments into consideration to help renters get credit for the bills they already pay.

VantageScore is one company with a new credit model that has been approved for use by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). And that roll out could have a big impact on renters like you.

A recent study by VantageScore found that nearly 4 million U.S. renters could finally become eligible for a mortgage if their on-time rent payments were counted as part of their credit history. Now, this isn’t a guarantee that it will suddenly count for every would-be buyer, but it does crack open the door.

Here’s the catch though, you still have to walk through it.

There are more options available to build credit, but you have to be proactive. Your rent won’t necessarily count unless you make sure it does. And the sooner you take credit for your payments, the better.

Here’s how to get started based on a few tips from Experian, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), and FICO:

Ask your landlord or property manager if your rent is being reported. If it’s not, opt in.

If your landlord doesn’t offer rent reporting, there are services available. Be sure to check fees and which bureaus they provide your data to.

Check your credit reports for errors. Mistakes happen and fixing them is one of the quickest ways to strengthen your profile. The CFPB offers step-by-step instructions on how to fix errors on your report.

Follow basic, important credit rules. Pay your bills on time, keep your card balances low, and don’t apply for a bunch of new credit at once.

Don’t close any accounts – even if you have paid them off and don’t use them often.

Diversify your profile by getting a secured credit card or becoming an authorized user on someone else’s account to help you build history beyond rent.

These steps take a little time and effort, but it could be worth it if it starts building up your credit history.

Just remember, not every lender uses the same scoring model and there are more changes on the horizon. So, the sooner you get credit for the work you do, the better.

Even if you’re not sure when you might be ready to buy a home, talking to a lender is a good place to start. A professional loan officer will help you make a plan and help you figure out a timeline to get ready to officially apply and buy.

#4600. Jagex cancels RuneScape Reignited less than two weeks after it was announced, due to "a ton" of player feedback

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

RuneScape: Reignited, a shiny new version of the long-running British MMORPG, has been cancelled less than two weeks after it was revealed.

RuneScape Reignited - a major reveal during RuneFest earlier this month - was to be a game mode which would allow players to start fresh characters with a separate player-economy from regular RuneScape accounts, meaning they could only buy and sell items between themselves. Reignited accounts would also be prohibited from tackling bosses with regular RuneScape accounts. It was an attempt to court new players to the game. However, it was immediately met with fierce feedback from long-time RuneScape players.

Jagex announced the cancellation of RuneScape: Reignited in both a blog post and YouTube video, in which associate creative director Mod Ryan explained why the sudden U-turn had occurred: "We received a ton of different perspectives from all of you, and we truly appreciate everyone sharing their views.

"It is super important to us because the last time we didn't listen to player feedback, combat changed forever. And it has taken us over a decade to recover from that. Now I said on stage that we would not repeat the same mistakes of the past, and I truly stand by that. Considering this, we have made the decision to cancel Reignited."

The question on the mind of the RuneScape layperson is: why was the feedback so strong against Reignited? The core reasons come down to splitting the playerbase, and Reignited's potential to leave loyal players stranded.

Because of Reignited characters' totally separate player economies to pre-existing RuneScape accounts, and given their fresh-start nature, they would have been the obvious avenue for newcomers to the MMO. As such, the community would have been split in two, and non-Reignited accounts would have been starved of new blood that could keep it healthy and active in the months and years to come.

There was no option to transition an old RuneScape account to a Reignited one, either, so for veteran players who wanted to hop into this new exciting version of RuneScape, they'd have to throw away potentially years of progress and memories to do so, leaving their old accounts to wilt. We're talking thousands of hours and heaps of cash here.

However it's also worth noting Jagex did do this for a reason. The in-game economy of RuneScape has been hyper-inflated for a while now, making the process of creating a new character and progressing through to the endgame a daunting progress due to gargantuan prices. For example, a Bond (exchangeable for a month of premium membership) sells for 11.2m coins on Old School RuneScape. On regular RuneScape, a bond sells for 141.3m coins.

Reignited accounts would have - for all their faults - solved this problem, providing an easy on-ramp for those curious to check the game out. With Reignited's cancellation this problem still persists, leaving Jagex with the same hurdle it had in front of it a month ago, just with an extra bruise or two.

Thankfully for Jagex, RuneScape: Reignited was not the only reveal from RuneFest, and as Ryan went on to mention, RuneScape is getting an Adventure Guide update and other additions in December, and a free trial. In perhaps more exciting news, RuneScape 4, an entirely new MMO, was also revealed at the show.

#4774. CD Projekt is in "a really dangerous place", says Anthem's executive producer, pointing to ballooning costs of making games of that scope and quality

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mark Darrah predicts a "bifurcation" is coming

"The triple-A industry has become over-specialised, specialised to the point of hyper-fragility," Mark Darrah states in a foreboding interview found deep in the pages of the Summer Eternal Anthology, recently covered by Edwin. As a former executive producer at Bioware, who corralled teams on Anthem and Dragon Age, after starting his career as a programmer on Baldur's Gate, Neverwinter Nights, and Jade Empire, Darrah has seen firsthand the growing scale of video game production and the challenges it can bring.

Darrah's also been vocal about the difficult development of Anthem in the past, and the struggles he had at Bioware. However, while what he says in the interview may be familiar, I found it gained sharper definition when read in light of games industry news and some of the interviews I've had with developers this year. So let me pull together some of the choice bits with the extra context from our own RPS vaults.

I should say, I've not laid hands myself on the Summer Eternal Anthology - a 500-page manifesto/design document/making-of book for Red Rooster, the new RPG from a splinter group of former Disco Elysium developers. But Edwin, who acknowledges that he's acknowledged in the acknowledgements section, did send me some photos of the interview with Darrah that's found deep within its pages.

Anyway, back to the Darrah's warning of over-specialisation. One of the problems, he says, is that it's caused teams to balloon in size because "No one person can add anything into a triple-A game. It requires at least five people." Obviously, the immediate implication of this is that larger teams cost more to run. The more staff you have on payroll, the more you will need to pay out each month on salaries, the more office space you will need to rent, the more software licenses and equipment you will need to pay for. This isn't the first time this has been said, just last month I ran an interview with Saber Interactive's chief creative officer Tim Willits, who put some numbers on those costs. "Each one of those developers is expensive," Willits said. "You have 200 people that you're paying $15,000 per person per month, which gets you way over two million."

But Darrah then links the complexity of projects to the increased risk of a delay. "The reality is that the bigger your team, the more vulnerable you typically are to disruption in terms of time," Darrah says. "If you have a 500-person team and you go one month late, that's probably adding five, ten million to your budget right there. It doesn't take much. And it's very easy for a 500-person team to go one month late, just because of the sand in the gears of the machine."

To put that in another context, consider Grand Theft Auto VI. As Jason Schreier detailed in his Making Of feature for Bloomberg, where Rockstar's head of publishing Jennifer Kolbe had to go to Take-Two Chief Executive Officer Strauss Zelnick and tell him they needed to delay their mega game from a 2025 release until May 2026 and then go back to him a few months later to say it had to be delayed until November. "I think he said, 'You’re kidding. Please tell me you're kidding,'" she said. Now, bear in mind that there aren't 500 people working GTA 6, there are more than 4,700. Using Darrah and Willit's back of the napkin maths, that delay may have cost Rockstar more than $500m.

Darrah speculates that the growing complexity may also be encouraging larger studios to stay in the prototyping phase because "prototyping is the one place where the devs are still allowed to be creative, so they don't want to leave that phase because they don't want to get down to just executing lists." Though, as Willits told me, having a fully-staffed team working on a prototype is terrible news for a studio's bottom line – "you do not need 200 people before you have a vertical slice, you're burning money."

Even if a game does come out at the end of that long, expensive production, it now has to earn back significantly more to break even. But, more and more, we're seeing executives get scared of the mounting costs of an in-development game and cutting bait while it's still in the pre-production phase. Just look at Perfect Dark and Everwild.

Darrah touches on the risk of that long, hard-to-leave-behind prototyping phase, because while you want developers to add things to the game "you don't want them to be able to generate work for other people. And as people have gotten more specialised, the ability for people to add things to the game that doesn't generate work for other people is basically vanishingly small."

Threaded through the interview with Darrah is his prediction that the games industry is headed toward a "bifurcation", in which several games will tear away from the rest of the market and become an unreachable target for other publishers, forcing them to scale their expectations down. "GTA 6 is going to rip itself off and take five or six games with it, up into the upper echelon of 'we can spend any amount of money on our games and it's going to be all right,'" Darrah says. Along with Rockstar's crime 'em up, he suggests Assassin's Creed, Call of Duty, and Battlefield could join it. He says there are just too many games that have $100m budgets that aren't making that money back.

Darrah points to his old studio Bioware as an example of one of the victims of this split. "BioWare sat right at the point where it doesn't quite work." Shortly after Dragon Age: Veilguard's launch, EA CEO Andrew Wilson said the RPG had significantly underperformed, reaching only 1.5m players, less than the 3m the company had planned for. (Also, worth noting, that's not 1.5m sales, as the game had a free trial available and was available through EA's subscription service.)

As much as this may sound like something out of Bioshock, with all of the top-selling games breaking away and making a home under the sea where they just eat all of the money for the rest of time, Darrah paints a surprisingly optimistic picture. "If that is happening, then I think you're going to see a lot more studios the size of Sandfall Interactive's Expedition 33. These are teams that are pretty small, pretty focused – Clair Obscur was very small with unusually massive outsourcing – a roughly 40-person team."

As Nic (RPS in peace) explained in the past, the claim that Clair Obscur was made by 30 people hides the sheer number of people involved in its production. Darrah explains that the difference between Sandfall and so many other teams in the industry is that typically a studio has a representative of all the disciplines it needs in-house – designers, artists, programmers, testers, audio people – and outsourcing is used to expand the capacity of the team, providing artists to work with the art director, for instance. But, Darrah says, Sandfall is different. "It looks to me like what Sandfall did was to keep entire holes in their structure – they don't appear to have any internal animators at all, and they outsourced 100% of their animation." The nature of the game, a turn-based RPG, helps with that, but even then that's a case of the developer recognising a significant way to reduce the team's overhead.

(Another example of a studio seemingly outsourcing smartly to keep their headcount low, look at what Neon Giant's achieving with immersive sim No Law.)

I will also point out that, while outsourcing can reduce costs for a studio, for the recipients of the work it can mean low wages and a figurative straitjacket. South American developers told James earlier this year how, despite their evident expertise, it was still difficult to convince publishers they were perfectly capable to leading projects. All to often, they are only contracted to assist American studios on theirs.

If that great schism happens, with publishers giving up their dreams of making the next GTA or the next Call of Duty, it could, Darrah says, reshape the industry. "If that all goes away, then you'll get a bunch of room to make it work with titles that need to sell 3-4 million units," he says. "That's much more attractive. I don't want to be working on something like GTA, where if this thing doesn't sell 50 million units, it's going to lose money. That's insane."

Though, within this prediction there is something a smidge worrying: the future of CD Projekt. Darrah says the Witcher maker has entered that rarefied space of the mega game – "Their games now need to sell 15, 20 million units to make money." And with that capacity for success but also that necessity for success, well, Darrah says, "that's a really dangerous place to be."

Darrah doesn't offer any predictions for The Witcher 4 or Cyberpunk 2's prospects, but I can't help but think of the news in May that there are already twice as many people working on The Witcher 4 than the 240 who worked on The Witcher 3.

As I say, none of what Darrah is saying is new exactly, but when you look at what's been taking place in the last year, it does sound like a move away from the mega budgets of the early 2020s has to be on the cards for all but the very biggest studios, right?

#4635. Women With Autoimmune Diseases More Likely to Be Misdiagnosed Than Men

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new international study found that women with autoimmune diseases often experience longer delays in getting a correct diagnosis compared to men.

The research, published in eClinicalMedicine on October 12, also shows that females report higher rates of being misdiagnosed, especially with mental health or psychosomatic conditions.

These findings highlight challenges in diagnosing autoimmune diseases and suggest that sex differences affect patients’ experiences in healthcare.

This is significant given that autoimmune diseases, conditions in which the body’s immune system attacks its own healthy cells, are estimated to affect around 4.6% of the U.S. population. Additionally, despite the observed disparities in diagnoses, women are almost two times as likely as men to be diagnosed with an autoimmune disease.

Researchers conducted an online survey between December 7, 2024, and March 7, 2025, inviting adults ages 18 and older who had a confirmed autoimmune or rheumatic disease.

The survey was carefully designed and tested with input from patients, charities, and medical experts to ensure it captured important information about patients’ diagnostic journeys.

Participants were asked about the time it took from when they first noticed symptoms to when they received their autoimmune diagnosis.

They also reported how many healthcare visits they made before diagnosis and whether they felt their symptoms were ever misdiagnosed as mental health issues, psychosomatic problems (physical symptoms caused or worsened by psychological factors), or another physical disease.

The survey was widely shared on social media, patient support groups, and community organizations to reach a broad audience.

Special efforts were made to include underrepresented groups such as males, younger individuals, and people from minority ethnic backgrounds.

The survey was available in five languages, and participants could complete it with help from researchers if needed.

To analyze the data, the team used statistical methods, adjusting for factors like age, type of autoimmune disease, continent of residence, and financial situation.

These methods helped compare the experiences of males and females while accounting for differences in disease types and other factors

The study included 6,607 participants, with 91.2% identifying as female and most living in the United Kingdom. On average, women were younger than men in the study.

Autoimmune thyroid disease was the most common condition reported. Non-autoimmune conditions like anxiety were more commonly reported by women, while men reported higher rates of hypertension.

One of the main findings was that women reported longer delays before getting a diagnosis. Nearly 32% of females experienced delays of four years or more from symptom onset to diagnosis, compared to about 26% of males.

On the other hand, men were more likely to be diagnosed within a year of symptom onset (nearly 50% of males versus 40% of females).

Women also made more visits to healthcare providers before diagnosis, with 26.2% having 11 or more visits compared to 18.4% of men.

When it came to misdiagnoses, women were significantly more likely to report that their symptoms had been mistaken for mental health issues (43.3% of females vs. 32.7% of males) or psychosomatic causes (33.9% of females vs. 21.5% of males).

However, there was no significant difference between men and women in being misdiagnosed with another physical disease.

The type of autoimmune disease also influenced misdiagnosis rates. Diseases with clear physical symptoms or definitive lab tests, like type 1 diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis, had lower rates of mental health and psychosomatic misdiagnoses.

In contrast, conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis or undifferentiated connective tissue disease had higher rates of these misdiagnoses (over 50%).

After adjusting for disease type and other factors, women still had higher odds of reporting mental health or psychosomatic misdiagnoses compared to men.

Other factors affected misdiagnosis rates, too. People living in continents outside the United Kingdom, those with lower household income, and certain disease groups were more likely to report psychiatric or psychosomatic misdiagnoses. Age, however, was not linked to the risk of being misdiagnosed.

Muhammad Qasim, MD, a pulmonologist and medical writer with MySleepApneaTeam, told Healthline that autoimmune diseases are often difficult to diagnose. Qasim wasn’t involved in the study.

“Early symptoms can be vague, fluctuate over time, and affect several parts of the body,” he said. “Fatigue, pain, sleep problems, brain fog, and mood changes do not immediately point to one diagnosis.”

Qasim echoed the study’s findings, explaining that similar symptoms may be interpreted differently in women versus men, with complaints being attributed to anxiety, stress, or psychosomatic causes.

However, there are probably multiple factors contributing to this phenomenon, he said.

“Differences in disease presentation, gaps in research, fragmented care, and unconscious bias can all contribute,” said Qasim. “Once a psychiatric explanation enters the medical record, there is also a risk that later clinicians view new symptoms through that same lens.”

Regarding unconscious bias, the study’s authors noted that women have historically been treated by doctors as “less credible” and more “emotional” in reporting their symptoms, which can influence how doctors interpret them.

These disparities in diagnosis are important, however, because longer waits and erroneous diagnoses can delay proper treatment and increase women’s frustration, leading them to stop asking for help.

The researchers suggest that healthcare providers need to be aware of these concerns and consider autoimmune diseases carefully when patients, especially females, present with more complicated symptoms.

#5691. The Nuclear Deal That Could Save NATO

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

IVO H. DAALDER is Senior Fellow at Harvard’s Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs. He served as U.S. Ambassador to NATO from 2009 to 2013.

How Europe Can Take More Responsibility—Without Starting an Arms Race

At a meeting earlier this year, top foreign ministry officials from several European countries were asked who among them still believed that the United States would come to their defense if they were attacked. Not a single hand went up. It was a stark indication of the extent to which European governments have concluded that the United States—for so long the backbone of European and NATO security—is no longer a reliable ally. Over the past 18 months, conversations in Brussels and in other NATO capitals have revolved around how to boost the continent’s conventional defense to make up for Washington’s retrenchment. But a quieter and more urgent discussion is also taking place, focused on the status of the ultimate deterrent: the U.S. nuclear guarantee.

More than half a century ago, the Great Debate, as the French strategist Raymond Aron termed it, broke out between European countries and the United States over how to preserve European nuclear deterrence in the face of the Soviet Union’s expanding nuclear capabilities. Then, European leaders feared that Washington’s growing emphasis on the conventional defense of the continent reflected an increasing reluctance to protect Europe with nuclear weapons—especially as Moscow developed missiles that could reach the U.S. homeland. Would the United States be willing to risk Boston to defend Berlin? That debate was ultimately resolved through a new grand bargain: in return for a commitment from European countries to forgo developing nuclear weapons, the United States would invite more European participation in nuclear decision-making. That essential bargain stuck for 60 years, helping secure both the United States and Europe and preventing nuclear proliferation.

Now, however, that bargain has come undone. Washington says that it wants a new division of labor: the Europeans need to take on primary responsibility for their conventional defense while the United States continues to ensure their nuclear defense. But that hardly addresses the problem. “If you don’t believe in the United States’ Article 5 commitment,” one of the officials attending the meeting told me, “then extended deterrence is effectively dead.” Already, alternative options are being considered. In some European capitals, officials are quietly raising the possibility of acquiring their own nuclear weapons. France, meanwhile, has offered to adapt its nuclear policy to help defend its European allies.Each of these options has real logic. But none of them adequately addresses the problem of ensuring European security without risking further proliferation. A better approach would be a fourth alternative: a renewed U.S.-European nuclear bargain, this time with a real European core. Led by France and the United Kingdom, NATO’s European members need to take on a larger role in their nuclear deterrence while working to restore the transatlantic defense link that has been the foundation of U.S. and European security for so many decades.

The Soviet Union’s 1957 launch of the Sputnik satellite posed a profound dilemma for NATO. Until then, the alliance had counted on U.S. nuclear weapons deployed in Europe to deter an attack by a much larger Soviet conventional force. But once Moscow demonstrated its ability to launch intercontinental rockets, Europeans feared that the United States would no longer be willing to use nuclear weapons to prevent a Soviet invasion of Europe.

These concerns prompted a fierce debate within NATO about how the continent could ensure its nuclear defense. Some, led by France, were convinced that European countries needed their own nuclear weapons. Others sought less costly ways to restore some control over nuclear deterrence. One American idea that gained substantial traction was to create a so-called multilateral force—a fleet of vessels carrying some 200 missiles. Such a multilaterally operated and funded force could give Europeans a sense of ownership and control over the use of nuclear weapons, the thinking went, while preserving the U.S. president’s final authority over their launch.

The debate exposed a fundamental problem with extending U.S. nuclear deterrence to allies, especially if an ocean separates those allies from Washington. An adversary might be deterred from attacking an American ally by even the small possibility of a U.S. nuclear retaliation; the consequences of a miscalculation would be catastrophic. But that same small possibility would offer little comfort to the ally, who needs to believe that Washington would actually risk nuclear devastation on its behalf. Denis Healey, the United Kingdom’s defense secretary throughout much of the 1960s, postulated what became known as the Healey theorem: “It takes only a five percent credibility of American retaliation to deter an attack, but it takes a 95 percent credibility to reassure the allies.”

The debate was ultimately resolved through a bargain. Washington’s European allies agreed to forgo their right to acquire nuclear weapons in return for a greater say in U.S. nuclear weapons policy and strategy. Although the idea of a costly multilateral force was abandoned, the creation of the NATO Nuclear Planning Group (NPG) provided a vehicle for Europeans to give input into U.S. nuclear planning and for Washington to demonstrate that European security interests were integral to its nuclear strategy and force posture. Reassured, European countries (aside from France and the United Kingdom) agreed to codify their nonnuclear status in the 1968 Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty.

Many European countries retained ground- and air-based systems that could deliver U.S. nuclear weapons deployed on their territory. Today, about half a dozen European countries still deploy dual-capable aircraft that can carry U.S. B-61 nuclear bombs. These nuclear consulting and sharing arrangements proved far more crucial to reassuring allies than the hard-power measures (such as counterforce targeting, low-yield weapons, and greater precision) that U.S. strategists typically believe were essential. The latter measures focused on resolving the five-percent problem; the former addressed the 95-percent questions.

France was the only country to reject this nuclear bargain. (The United Kingdom had become a nuclear power in the 1950s, but it committed all its weapons to NATO and participated in the NPG and the dual-capable aircraft missions.) Under President Charles de Gaulle, France came to believe that it could not count on another country for its nuclear security. Paris embarked on its own nuclear program, refused to participate in the NPG or the dual-capable aircraft mission, and, in 1966, withdrew from NATO’s military command structure. The overall bargain, however, laid the foundation for what has become a highly successful deterrence, reassurance, and nonproliferation regime.

Unlike in the 1960s, today’s nuclear debate has not been prompted by a change in the global balance of power. It has been precipitated by a unilateral change in the United States’ attitude toward Europe. As I explained in Foreign Affairs last year, in Trump’s second term, the United States has rejected the two core principles of NATO’s collective defense commitments: that there is a shared understanding of the threat to NATO members and that members’ security is indivisible.

European members of NATO can no longer assume that the U.S. commitment to their security—derived from the fundamental idea, born of two devastating world wars, that American security is inextricably linked to the security of Europe—is ironclad. Instead, as the Pentagon’s policy chief, Elbridge Colby, has repeatedly noted, “under President Trump’s leadership, the simple fact is that countries can no longer take America for granted.” European countries have never taken America for granted. They have assumed that it shares their belief in the indivisibility of U.S. and European security—and they proved their own commitment to this belief when NATO invoked Article 5’s collective-defense obligation for the first and only time, in response to the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States.

The American president and other senior officials have made it clear that the United States’ security commitment to Europe is based not on a shared understanding of the threat that both entities face but on secondary considerations, such as European countries’ level of defense spending and their support for U.S. foreign policy. In August, the Pentagon sent its 31 NATO allies a long questionnaire stressing the conditionality of Washington’s security commitment: it asked them to profess their loyalty to Trump’s foreign policy, commit to procuring defense equipment from U.S. firms, and prioritize funding the restoration of the U.S. industrial base by opposing any “buy European” initiatives.

Some European NATO leaders still refuse to admit in public that anything fundamental has changed. Privately, though, most have reached the same conclusion as senior European political officials: that the United States can no longer be counted on to come to Europe’s defense. And if that is true when it comes to conventional defense, it is even more true with respect to the nuclear guarantee. That leaves Europe facing questions it has never had to ask before. What if Russia attacked NATO territory and Washington told Europe it was on its own? How would nonnuclear allies stand up to a nuclear-armed Russia? What if Moscow used nuclear threats, implicit or explicit, to try to force Europe to end its support for Ukraine? None of these scenarios are far-fetched. Russia has issued veiled nuclear threats against Europe repeatedly since 2022, most recently in September, after NATO conducted air exercises above northeastern Poland, near Kaliningrad.

Three options have begun to emerge to fill the gap created by the change in U.S. policy. Washington favors reestablishing the nuclear bargain with a new distribution of responsibilities, which it calls NATO 3.0. “We will continue to provide the U.S. extended nuclear deterrent,” Colby told NATO defense ministers earlier this year, “with Europe taking the lead for its conventional defense.” The United States has even suggested that it might be willing to expand its nuclear umbrella by extending its nuclear sharing arrangements to other countries (Poland, for instance) and developing a next-generation nuclear bomb for potential deployment in Europe.

But this option ignores the core problem. The Trump administration has done nothing to convince its allies that the United States remains committed to NATO other than saying so. No additional weapons deployments can compensate for the central implication that U.S. security commitments to European allies are now conditional on political loyalty.

A second option would end the nuclear bargain altogether. In its place, European countries would acquire their own nuclear weapons. The clearest statement of this idea came in March 2025, when Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk told the country’s parliament that “we would be safer if we had our own nuclear arsenal, that is beyond doubt.” A year later, he told his cabinet that Poland would “strive to prepare . . . for the most autonomous actions possible” on nuclear security. And Poland isn’t the only country thinking seriously about this option. In Sweden, some politicians have invoked the country’s secret nuclear program during the 1960s to indicate their interest in pursuing a national option. Even in Germany, where discussions of defense have historically been constrained by its own history, a Bundeswehr general argued publicly earlier this year that Germany “needs its own tactical nuclear weapons,” an assertion that would have been unthinkable just a few years ago. Similar ideas are being voiced in Asia, especially in South Korea, raising the prospect of a cascade of allied proliferation.

In theory, if more European countries acquired nuclear weapons, it could resolve the extended deterrence dilemma. In practice, it isn’t a workable solution. It would take many years for Poland, Sweden, or even Germany to develop independent nuclear deterrents. European nuclearization would break the Nonproliferation Treaty architecture that the original 1960s U.S.-European nuclear bargain was built to protect, and it could set off a nuclear arms race that could make Europe less, not more, secure. A strategy designed to bolster deterrence would end up weakening the very alliance structure it aimed to strengthen.

As an alternative to these two options, France has spearheaded a third approach that would aim to replace, or at least augment, the U.S. umbrella. French President Emmanuel Macron proposed this solution in a speech in March 2026, announcing a “forward deterrence” initiative that would include European allies in nuclear training exercises, temporarily forward-deploy French nuclear-capable aircraft on allied soil, and host extensive nuclear discussions with participating countries. So far, ten European countries have joined the initiative by establishing bilateral nuclear steering groups to discuss French nuclear strategy and how its evolution could enhance their security.

The Macron proposal represents a major evolution in French thinking by explicitly extending France’s vital interests to include the security of its European partners. Paris’s willingness to engage European neighbors in direct talks about nuclear issues is welcome. But there are real drawbacks to such an initiative. In his announcement, Macron reiterated the long-standing “four nos” that underpin France’s defense doctrine: no joint decisions on nuclear use, no joint nuclear planning, no shared definition of French vital interests, and no nuclear guarantee “in the strict sense of the term.” The framework’s reliance on bilateral discussions also weakens the idea that Europe’s defense ought to be a collective NATO effort.

And crucially, France’s nuclear doctrine—which allows for a warning shot, followed by a devastating response if the warning fails to deter the adversary—gives it fewer options for managing conflict than does NATO’s more flexible strategy, which allows for gradual escalation. This narrower range of options could make its nuclear threats less credible in a conflict over an ally’s security. In the end, the French proposal would add a measure of reassurance and deterrence, but not enough to overcome the voids created by the change in U.S. policy.

None of the three options now being discussed resolve Europe’s extended nuclear deterrence dilemma. But elements of all three point to a possible solution. NATO needs to bolster both the European and the U.S. legs of its nuclear deterrence posture, bring France into its nuclear consultation and planning structures, and extend the existing nuclear sharing arrangements to incorporate additional members. These tasks will require NATO members, especially its three nuclear powers, to make significant adjustments to their existing beliefs and policies. The alternative is not the status quo—which is already unsustainable, given the nature of the Russian threat and the changes in U.S. policy—but rapid nuclear proliferation.

NATO countries will need to agree to three major adjustments in their nuclear posture: in the number and kind of weapons deployed, in how nuclear and nonnuclear NATO members consult one another, and in the alliance’s doctrine for using nuclear weapons. First, NATO nuclear powers need to modernize their nuclear forces. The United States should replace the B-61 gravity bomb, which will be difficult to use effectively against heavily defended targets, with a stand-off missile that can reach deep into Russian territory from NATO airspace. Such a modernization is long overdue. In addition, European nations should agree to develop a long-range missile that could be outfitted with a nuclear warhead developed jointly by France and the United Kingdom. The missile should be financed by European countries and be deployable on aircraft owned and operated by nonnuclear allies. Finally, these new U.S. and European nuclear capabilities need to be based in a wider range of NATO countries, expanding European participation in the nuclear deterrent mission.

Changes to hardware are necessary to restore the credibility of Europe’s nuclear deterrence. But the software of consultation needs to be at the core of the effort, as it was in the 1960s. Back then, France refused to join the NATO consensus and withdrew from the integrated military command structure. Even when it returned, in 2009, its nuclear deterrent remained fully independent, and it did not join the Nuclear Planning Group. That insistence on independence is what led Paris to pursue bilateral nuclear arrangements this year. But these parallel bilateral discussions not only create confusion and distrust about Paris’s true intentions among dialogue partners; they also fail to foster the cohesion that could come from a collective European nuclear deterrence dialogue.

Put simply, Paris needs to join the NPG. Doing so would not automatically place its nuclear capabilities under NATO command and control. But it would give European partners a better understanding of French thinking and afford France more knowledge of European requirements for deterrence and reassurance. European NPG members could agree to meet regularly without the United States to discuss how to integrate the new European nuclear missile forces into NATO’s nuclear strategy. For many years, countries hosting U.S. nuclear forces have met separately from other NATO members in the NPG; a similar, European-only caucus would build on that precedent.

The third necessary adjustment needs to be doctrinal. One element of NATO’s nuclear doctrine cannot change: that the ultimate decision to use nuclear weapons will always reside with the nuclear powers, France, the United Kingdom, and the United States. These decisions cannot be delegated. And national nuclear deterrents will continue to be guided by national doctrine, not subject to NATO control. If France wants to retain its one-warning-shot escalatory doctrine, it can do so, even after it joins the NPG. But it would need to explain how this doctrine would reassure allies and enhance deterrence. At the same time, France’s participation in the NPG and the addition of the new European nuclear missile force would expand Europe’s influence over the development of NATO nuclear strategy. And it would confront Moscow with the reality that the decision to use nuclear weapons in Europe’s defense no longer rests in Washington’s hands alone.

These are major posture adjustments that will require significant changes in policy and perspective. Washington will have to accept that the deployment of additional European nuclear forces means it can no longer dictate NATO’s nuclear policy and strategy—including, ultimately, the decision to use nuclear weapons. Paris, meanwhile, must accept more integration into NATO. London will need to extend its nuclear cooperation with the United States to France, develop a second nuclear system (in addition to its submarine force), and find the resources necessary to design and build a new warhead in collaboration with France. Nonnuclear European countries will have to agree to finance the development and procurement of new missile systems and possibly airplanes (although not nuclear warheads, which would violate their treaty commitments). They must also collectively finance security, communications, and other upgrades to enable the expanded deployment of dual-capable aircraft. Most important, these nonnuclear allies would need to reiterate their commitment to forgoing nuclear weapons of their own, reaffirming the essential bargain that was cemented 60 years ago.

None of this will happen quickly or easily. It will require London, Paris, and Washington to give up an element of control over their nuclear arsenals that each has jealously guarded for more than 60 years. But the alternatives are worse. The security of Europe and the United States is, in fact, indivisible, no matter what the current administration in Washington may believe. If Europe were left to fend for itself and nuclear weapons spread from capital to capital, Americans would be less safe, not more. Yet U.S. retrenchment is also real, and no amount of reassurance from Washington will restore the credibility of a nuclear guarantee that Europeans have come to doubt. With the United States pulling back, a new bargain in which Europe carries more of the nuclear burden and has more say in the choice to use these weapons is the only way to ensure transatlantic security.

How Battlefield Cooperation Is Binding Together America’s Adversaries

Andrea Kendall-Taylor and Richard Fontaine

On July 17, an Iranian missile struck Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, killing three American soldiers and wounding four others. The strike was unusually precise. In the weeks that followed, U.S. officials discovered that before the attack, Tehran had received high-resolution satellite images of the base that could aid its targeting. Afterward, Iran obtained more imagery, potentially helping it assess the damage and hone future attacks. The images came from Chinese entities.

China is not alone in assisting Iran. Russia, too, has helped Tehran find and hit American and allied targets. Two days before the March 27 attack on Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia that wounded 12 American personnel, 14 Russian spy satellites passed overhead. U.S. officials later concluded that Moscow had fed Iran satellite intelligence that aided Iranian precision strikes. Moscow has also provided Iran with weapons and military expertise, including upgraded versions of Iranian drones refined on the battlefield in Ukraine, and Russian engineers are helping Iran develop supersonic cruise missiles that could evade U.S. and Israeli air defenses.

Neither Russia nor China has directly entered Iran’s war with the United States. They haven’t needed to. By providing intelligence on American targets, military technology and components, and economic and diplomatic support, they can give Iran what it needs without joining its war.

The United States entered the conflict with advantages its adversaries lacked: a vast network of formal allies, military bases, intelligence-sharing arrangements, defense partnerships, and interoperable forces spanning Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. Yet many of Washington’s traditional partners were reluctant to associate themselves with the campaign, and some denied the United States specific forms of military access, at least for a time. Others publicly resisted U.S. calls to help secure the Strait of Hormuz.

The contrast illustrates a striking shift since we first described Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea as an axis of upheaval in Foreign Affairs two years ago. The United States’ adversaries began this war with this weaker network, and Washington with the far stronger one. But after helping Russia fight its war in Ukraine over the past few years, members of the axis are now finding ways to make their network work in a new theater. They are fine-tuning their cooperation to make their partners more resilient—and more dangerous to the United States—while limiting costs and risks. Washington, for its part, is creating friction in many of its partnerships instead of deepening them, and risks squandering its network just as its adversaries are learning to make more of theirs.

Much has changed since 2024. Most obviously, the United States itself has become a source of upheaval in the international system. The Trump administration has challenged international institutions, strained traditional alliances, and adopted a more transactional approach to foreign policy. It has upended trade agreements, threatened the sovereignty of allies such as Canada and Denmark, and embraced unpredictability. It has also pursued improved relations with China, North Korea, and Russia while seeking to weaken Iran. None of this has loosened the ties among the United States’ adversaries.

Early in the Trump administration, U.S. officials expressed hope that normalizing relations with Moscow might peel Russia away from the group. Yet President Donald Trump’s oft-expressed bonhomie toward the Kremlin has opened no gaps with the other three. As seen from Moscow, the administration has been unable to deliver a deal on Ukraine, and any Trump-driven rapprochement with Washington is unlikely to endure: Congress continues to pressure Russia, and a future administration may well adopt a tougher approach.

More fundamentally, attempts to weaken the axis misunderstand what holds it together. Its members object to an international order that they see as enshrining Western dominance at their expense. But more pointedly, they oppose the United States’ extraordinary power to constrain, isolate, and punish them, and potentially to threaten their regimes. Recent U.S. actions have only rekindled those concerns. The United States’ capture of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro and the killing of Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei reinforce long-standing fears in Beijing, Moscow, Pyongyang, and Tehran about U.S. intervention and regime security.

The axis countries may moderate their anti-American rhetoric when doing so is tactically useful, but their motivation to reduce their vulnerability to American power remains. The Trump administration’s own skepticism about international order will not ease minds in axis capitals. On the contrary, Trump’s efforts to create a more transactional and coercive world may increase the value of their partnerships. As rules and institutions constrain less and brute power matters more, countries will seek partners to help them blunt sanctions, access critical technologies, compensate for military weaknesses, expand their room for maneuver, and resist isolation. Axis cooperation is evolving to meet the moment.

When Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, cooperation among the four countries was relatively easy to map. Iran provided Russia with drones, and North Korea sent ammunition, ballistic missiles, and later, troops. China became Moscow’s most critical lifeline, buying Russian oil and supplying the machine tools, microelectronics, and other components it needed first to sustain and then to expand its military production. Russia, in turn, transferred increasingly sophisticated military technology to its partners.

These exchanges were consequential, but they could still largely be understood as a series of bilateral transactions focused on Russia’s war. That is becoming less true. Cooperation is increasingly networked and geared toward solving new problems in new regions.

Russia’s drone program illustrates the change. Iran initially sold Russia its Shahed drones and then helped Moscow establish production at home. Russia did not simply replicate what it received; it incorporated Chinese components and continually modified the drones’ navigation, communications, payloads, propulsion, and tactics in response to Ukrainian defenses. New military units fed successful modifications back into production, generating an unusually rapid cycle of battlefield adaptation. Russia’s Unmanned Systems Forces, a drone-and-robotics branch of the Russian armed forces, was established last year to help institutionalize this cycle. The combination of domestic adaptation and axis partnership has proved effective. Russia’s latest jet-powered Geran drones, for instance, are faster, carry larger payloads, and are significantly harder for Ukrainian forces to intercept than earlier variants.

Russian improvements do not stay in Russia, either. They circulate through the axis network. For Iran, the technology that originally flowed to Russia is now returning, along with tactical advice drawn from Russia’s experience in Ukraine. If Russia’s jet-powered drones also show up in the Middle East, as they likely will, Iranian attacks on U.S. forces and regional energy infrastructure will become harder and costlier to defend against.

North Korea and China are also benefiting. According to Ukrainian reports, Russia struck a deal to help North Korea expand production of its Shahed-type drones. North Korean engineers have learned from time spent working in Russia’s massive Yelabuga drone factory, and North Korean forces fighting alongside Russia have gained firsthand experience in integrating drones into modern combat. Chinese military personnel have received training in drone warfare from Russian forces, and Chinese suppliers are gaining valuable information about how their technologies perform in combat. Beijing is thus seeing gains from the evolution of drone warfare that began with Iranian technology and has been refined in Russia’s war in Ukraine.

Two years ago, it was not clear whether partnerships among the four members of the axis could outlast the war that accelerated their formation. If the Ukraine war ended and Russia no longer needed Iranian drones, North Korean ammunition, or Chinese economic and technological support, the relationships might lose momentum.

Yet the war did not only create an immediate need for cooperation. It has also provided proof of concept for what these partnerships can accomplish, beyond what any country might attain on its own. Ukrainian defenses, Western sanctions, U.S. military supplies, and diplomatic isolation have all been less effective than would be the case if Russia were operating alone. By helping one another withstand pressure, the axis countries make it harder for the United States and its allies to compel them to change course.

Iran is now showing that the model can travel. Russia’s support for Tehran has been operationally meaningful but carefully bounded. Even as it fights its own war in Ukraine, Moscow has found ways to help Iran strike American targets and sustain the fight through diplomatic cover and material support while stopping short of actions that could draw Russia into direct conflict with the United States. China has been even more cautious, but its sharing of dual-use electronics and purchases of Iranian oil have similarly enhanced Iran’s ability to withstand U.S. pressure.

Such restraint is not evidence that the axis is weakening but rather a defining feature of how these partnerships work. With the striking exception of North Korean soldiers deployed on Russia’s behalf, the axis countries have generally avoided fighting one another’s wars. Instead, they provide political backing, economic support, intelligence, technology, and limited military assistance when doing so advances their own interests at manageable risk.

The wars in Ukraine and Iran have taught the axis another lesson: supporting a fellow member does not merely strengthen the recipient. Conflict in one theater can also create opportunities for partners elsewhere. Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz, for example, sent oil prices soaring, delivering a windfall to Russia and prompting Washington to temporarily ease restrictions on Russian oil exports. Iran’s war thus generated resources Russia could use to sustain its own.

Before attacking Iran, the United States redeployed key military assets, including air and missile defenses, from the Indo-Pacific to the Middle East. As the fighting continued, U.S. forces put two aircraft carriers in the region, leaving none in the Indo-Pacific. China gains operational freedom from a thinner U.S. military presence in the western Pacific and is less vulnerable when U.S. munitions stocks are depleted. If Washington settles into a prolonged strategy of containing Iran, as it did with Iraq in the 1990s, Beijing could enjoy the advantages of those diversions for years.

The experience of the axis of upheaval shows that the size and strength of a network matter less if its members cannot turn their individual capabilities into collective power. The United States and its allies start from a position of considerable advantage: greater economic and technological resources, more capable militaries, and a far larger network of allies and partners. The question is which network can make better use of its assets.

That starts with leadership. For decades, the United States favored an international order that forbade wars of conquest, upheld freedom of the seas, maintained a generally open global economy, and allowed populations to exercise democratic rights, even if autocracies remained. Washington coupled this vision with an implicit bargain: any country that wished to join the order could do so. Upholding the order’s basic principles conferred benefits, including from the United States: trade relations, diplomatic preferences, sometimes military alliances.

For countries that rejected the open international order, as the axis countries do today, the benefits were far fewer and the penalties considerable. The United States sanctioned, pressured, and sometimes went to war against rogue states, and it resisted the establishment of competing orders such as the Cold War communist bloc. U.S. foreign policy started from the seemingly uncontroversial premise that Washington should befriend like-minded countries and treat them better than rejectionists.

The Trump administration demonstrates, at least at times, something like the opposite inclination. The U.S. ambassador to China recently said that Trump offered to sell Beijing weapons, while stalling arms sales to Taiwan. The president has expressed greater interest in and affection for North Korea’s dictator, Kim Jong Un, than for South Korea’s democratically elected president, Lee Jae-myung. The administration has also intermittently pressured Ukraine to cave in its war of survival against Russian aggression. And Trump has threatened to bomb Oman, a longtime partner. Such moves both strengthen the axis countries and weaken the cohesion of the network best positioned to confront them.

Doing no further harm would be a good start in dealing with the axis. Beyond that, Washington should help its partners grow stronger. Just as the axis countries are helping one another work around sanctions, preserve access to critical technologies and components, and sustain their economies under pressure, the United States should aid its own network. That means strengthening supply chains that run through trusted partners, sharing technology, and encouraging allies to take on greater security burdens. Washington must take care, however, not to transfer defense responsibilities faster than allies can develop the capabilities to assume them—and not to do so in ways that erode trust, interoperability, and coordination. Otherwise, the United States and its allies could become individually more capable but collectively less effective.

The United States should also reverse policies that undercut its own network. Tariffs and other protectionist measures harm allied economies and their industrial bases. Expressing a desire to seize allies’ territory and attempting to undermine their political leaders do not make for a more capable international network. Washington should project vision and leadership to its friends, not jarring uncertainty and bullying transactionalism.

Even if the United States changes course, American and allied policymakers should prepare for a long struggle. As the unsuccessful regime-change effort in Iran demonstrated, there are no shortcuts to besting the axis countries, together or individually. The contest of networks will be nearly global, waged in multiple domains, with huge stakes riding on the outcome. The United States and its allies have the capacity to prevail. They should demonstrate the will.

#4117. How to Enable Global Privacy Control in Every Browser (and Why You Should)

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Security-wise, most browsers are an open door, happy to reveal your screen size, operating system, language preferences, or GPU model when a website asks. But telling those websites to stop selling your data to third parties is still more confusing than it should be.

Although about a dozen U.S. states—including California, Delaware, New Jersey, and Texas—now require businesses to honor consumers’ privacy preferences online, most browsers do not offer a native opt-out option that lets you signal those preferences by default. Now, the California Opt Me Out Act, which goes into effect on Jan 1, 2027, is making it mandatory for browsers to do so.

Still, for now, native support for a global privacy opt-out remains inconsistent among web browsers. TSome privacy-forward browsers like Brave, DuckDuckGo, and Mozilla Firefox enable this feature by default, while others, like Chrome or Edge, require you to install third-party extensions such as the EFF Privacy Badger. Since coverage and setup vary wildly, I’ll walk you through the exact steps to enable this feature on every major browser.

On a technical level, GPC combines an HTTP header (Sec-GPC) with a JavaScript property (navigator.globalPrivacyControl) that your browser sends with every website fetch request to let website owners know that they are not supposed to share or sell your data.

Unlike previous iterations like Firefox’s Do Not Track, GPC issues a specific request that’s backed by leading data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), making websites and businesses more likely to honor them. With over 150 million users and more than 66,000 participating websites, the GPC project has garnered support and participation from the Mozilla Foundation, The Washington Post, Automattic, Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF), The New York Times, and others. It has even been made into an official work item at the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C).

When it comes to GPC support, web browsers fall broadly into two camps. There are privacy-focused platforms like Brave Browser, DuckDuckGo, and Firefox that offer native support using a built-in toggle, or just enable it by default. Then there are mainstream browsers like Chrome, Edge, and Safari that offer limited support through third-party extensions, or sometimes none at all. Here are the exact steps to follow to enable Global Privacy Control on Firefox, Chrome, Edge, Brave Browser, and others:

Being a privacy-first Chromium-based web browser, Brave has GPC enabled by default on Windows, macOS, and Android. However, due to certain platform limitations on iOS, Brave can only enable the feature through the JavaScript API and not the HTTP header on iPhones.

You don’t need to do anything to enable GPC on Brave Browser, but there’s an easy way to confirm whether it’s active and running:

Launch Brave. In the address bar, type in “brave://flags/#brave-global-privacy-control-enabled” and hit Enter.

Confirm that the Settings page reads Default or Enabled, not Disabled.

To disable GPC on your browser, navigate to Settings > Brave Shields and Privacy to find the corresponding toggle.

DuckDuckGo, like Brave Browser, enables GPC by default on Windows, macOS, Android, iOS, and iPadOS. There’s no built-in toggle to disable it, which tells you just how seriously this browser takes its privacy implementation. (There's even a DuckDuckGo Search & Tracker Protection extension you can add to Chrome, Edge, and Firefox to enable GPC on those browsers, too!)

If you’d like to confirm that GPC is on when you’re using DuckDuckGo, there’s an easy way to do that:

Open a new tab on DuckDuckGo Private Browser and visit the Global Privacy Control website.

Check the notification banner at the very top to see if it reads “GPC signal detected.”

To avoid breaking certain websites on Android, iOS, and Mac devices, DuckDuckGo only sends the GPC signal to websites that are known to respect it. However, you still get full coverage on Windows or if you use the Chrome extension.

Firefox has offered GPC support since version 120. However, it’s not enabled by default, and you need to hunt for a specific toggle to switch it on.

Click on the menu button on your Firefox app, then navigate to Settings.

Access the Privacy & Security tab from the panel on the left.

Keep scrolling until you see Additional protections. On Android version 122+, look for a section that says Enhanced Tracking Protection.

You should see a checkbox next to an option that reads “Tell websites not to sell or share my data.” This is what you’re looking for.

Make sure you check the option to enable GPC, then relaunch Firefox.

As of this writing, Google Chrome does not offer a built-in toggle or native support for GPC. However, you can install a third-party browser extension like the Privacy Badger by EFF or DuckDuckGo Search & Tracker Protection to enable Global Privacy Control very easily.

Search for Privacy Badger on the Chrome Web Store.

Once you find it, click Add to Chrome. Make sure to enable any required permissions for the extension to work too.

That’s it, GPC is enabled on all sites now. You don’t need to fiddle around with the settings since everything comes pre-configured.

As with Chrome, Microsoft Edge users must also rely on third-party extensions to enable Global Privacy Control. And like before, you can choose between Privacy Badger or the DuckDuckGo extension to activate this feature.

Open the Edge Add-ons store and look for Privacy Badger. EFF’s website lists both Edge and Edge for Android as supported browsers for the extension.

GPC is now on by default. You don’t need to configure anything else.

First, GPC is a signal. It merely informs websites of your privacy preferences, but does nothing to actively block or prevent tracking or sharing of your data. Second, GPC is narrower in scope because it only covers selling or sharing your data with third parties, not collecting or storing data for a website’s own use.

When you’re enabling Global Privacy Control instead of using an anti-tracking app or ad blocker, you’re trading coverage and effectiveness for convenience. You’ll have to just trust that websites will honor your privacy preferences, which many don’t, even when they are required to. For example, Disney just settled a $2.75 million lawsuit with the California AG’s office because it didn’t honor opt-out requests for logged-in users on every device like it was supposed to.
