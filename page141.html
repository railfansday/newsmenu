#5926. My Favorite Airbnb: A Restored 200-Year-Old Trullo in Puglia With Room for My Whole Family

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As anyone who has ever planned a family vacation knows, booking a home that satisfies all of your party’s needs is a true art. By the time my parents, brother, and I embarked upon six days in Puglia in September, I had spent hours searching for a home that checked all the boxes. The nonnegotiables: My dad was set on staying in a trullo (the historic, conical stone homes native to the Itria Valley), my mother wanted laundry, and we all wanted a pool. And for me, a former design editor, aesthetics were always critical.

These criteria triangulated to find us at Trullo Giulio, 15 minutes outside of Martina Franca. Our host coordinated with her neighbor, Anna, to meet us at the property after the 90-minute drive from Bari. We could hear Anna’s tiny red farm truck puttering down the road to greet us, which should give you an image of just how rural this part of the region is—nothing but olive trees as far as the eye can see. Between my father’s three years of Italian lessons and some universal sign language, we got the lay of the land, which consisted of the main trullo (two beds, one bath) and two outbuildings that hosted two additional bedrooms, a bathroom, and the kitchen.

The decor was almost Santorinian in its whitewashed and blue-accented simplicity. The owners had taken great care to decorate the trullo with local textiles and pottery (I highly recommend a day trip to Grottaglie, the ceramic destination of the region) and features that allowed the original stone walls and vaulted ceilings to breathe. It’s modernized, but still retains its charm. And yes, there were a few quirks. The bedrooms in the main trullo were curtained off from the living room—don’t worry, the bathroom had a door—and the advertised AC units were only in the outbuildings. The Wi-Fi and cell service were pretty much nonexistent, which all but highlighted the fact that the true heart of the home was really the outdoor space.

The first thing we did after our tour with Anna was take in the pool. As the sun was setting, we dunked our tired feet in the water while sipping the local rosé and snacks Anna had left for us. Saltwater, with a whirlpool and an infinity edge that lets you gaze straight into the olive groves—it’s the showstopper of the property hands down. There’s also an outdoor shower for a quick rinse and multiple lounging spaces both in and out of the sun. We spent the majority of our time—when we weren’t on the pool deck—at the massive dining table under a shaded pergola.

A quick bite to eat featuring pizza and cheese

While we easily could have spent all of our days lounging poolside, the location of the trullo also made it a convenient launchpad for exploring the region. Aside from the much-slept-on town of Martina Franca, it's within an hour's drive of Alberobello, Ostuni, Polignano a Mare, and the many smaller beach towns along the coast—don’t miss the restaurant Ricciolandia if you’re a fan of urchin. Within a five-minute drive of the house is a delightfully well-stocked mini market and local fave Sale e Pepe, where we had more than one night of phenomenal pizza and grilled meats.

My favorite meal in Puglia, however, was one of (mostly) our own making. The final nonnegotiable we’d all agreed upon was that we had to take a pasta-making class before leaving Italy, which we also found on Airbnb. We joined Cristiana and her mother at their beachside trullo in Cozze for a sunny afternoon spent in the outdoor kitchen, where the humbling experience of making orecchiette by hand was enhanced by homemade limoncello and sparkling conversation.

No number of ancient churches, fun beach spots, or magical little towns could outweigh the best part of a family trip: The moments spent around the table. Trullo Giulio was the home base we needed to make those moments happen.

#2978. More than a championship hangover: Four reasons behind Knicks' troubling 0-3 start

In the entire 80-year history of the NBA, only three champions have started their title defense with an 0-3 start the next season: The 1969 Celtics, 2011 Mavericks, and this year's Knicks, who've taken losses to the Sixers, Celtics and, on Sunday night, a Magic team that had also come in winless and was playing without Paolo Banchero.

Last year's title was the product of a legendary postseason run, but this start has provided an opening for all the skeptics to question its sustainability. Were last year's Knicks really that good? They only won 53 regular-season games. They trailed against the Spurs in the Finals for more than 70% of the total minutes played before pulling off multiple basketball miracles.

There was, of course, the largest comeback in Finals history (29 points in Game 4). The OG Anunoby tip-in. The Victor Wembanyama pass off his teammate's back in Game 2. The 16-point rally in the clincher. The Knicks had to overcome a double-digit deficit in every game and Jalen Brunson had to turn into a fourth-quarter folk hero -- which he did, and all credit to him and them, but again, is this a repeatable formula in a much tougher Eastern Conference than the one the Knicks navigated a year ago?

Fair or not, these are the questions this 0-3 start is giving life to. Everyone from Knicks diehards to haters to the few objective ones in between want to know, what's wrong with the Knicks? Was last year a one-year, team-of-destiny wonder, or is this just an early blip on the radar? Let's examine what's behind this 0-3 start.

This is a popular one, but statistically, it holds little water. Of the 26 NBA champions this century, only the 2006-07 Miami Heat won fewer than 51 games in the season following their championship (not counting the strike- and COVID-shortened seasons), and only two -- those same Heat and the 2002-03 Lakers -- have started with a sub-.500 record through the first 20 games.

Do the math, and you'll see that defending champions are a combined 366-154 through the first 20 games of the season following their title this century. A 70% win percentage to start the following season with only one sub-51 win team lends zero credibility to the hangover theory.

If you're thinking about a repeat, which was already unlikely (it hasn't happened since the 2018 Warriors, it's not looking good. The only team in history to start 0-3 and go on to win the championship that same season is the 1990-91 Chicago Bulls.

That's not to say the Knicks can't do it. They defied a lot of historical odds last season, particularly in the Finals. They're going to have to figure this out realtively quickly, though, if they don't want to be digging out of seeding hole the rest of the way. This may not matter much to them as long as they're in the top six, but it should. Again, evidence does not support teams winning titles as anything less than a No. 3 seed. It's only happened two times in history with the 1969 Celtics (No. 4 seed) and 1995 Rockets (No. 6 seed).

Knicks fans looking for an excuse will surely cite the distractions of the Karl-Anthony Towns contract debacle and maybe even the James Dolan phishing trip he sprung on his employees as reasons for the slow start. They've played two tough teams in the Sixers and Celtics, and the Magic shot uncharacteristically well from 3. But that doesn't explain New York's own shortcomings in this winless start. So let's address those.

I hate the make-or-miss-league crutch that everyone from coaches to analysts loves to lean on. More times than not, when you're missing shots it's because they're bad shots (either from the wrong people or the wrong spots or they are highly contested adn thus being cause by the defense), and when you're making them it's because they're good shots. That's about process -- ball movement, individual creation, proper spacing, and everything else that goes into sound NBA offense.

Still, there is some truth to even good shots simply not going in sometimes, and the bottom line is the Knicks are ice cold to start this season. They have the lowest true-shooting percentage in the league, and only Charlotte has a worse traditional shooting clip than New York's 40.8%. Add in a 32.4% 3-point mark, and you can see why the Knicks own the league's second-worst offensive rating to start the season.

This is in stark contrast to the Knicks team we watched buzz-saw its way to the title last season, when they were the playoff leader in both field-goal and 3-point percentage. Think about all the tough shots we watched Jalen Brunson make on that postseason run, particularly late in games. So far, those shots, most of which are pretty similar looks are not going in at anywhere close to the same clip.

Other than Towns, who has shot the cover off the ball to start, New York's starters are all struggling to find their early stroke. Brunson, who shot 12 for 28 against the Magic and missed all six of his 3s before finally making one in the final seconds when the game was long over, is at 39% overall and 26% from 3. Anunoby is shooting 41% from the floor and 28% from 3. Josh Hart, who is back to getting the silent treatment as defenses basically don't guard him at the 3-point line to allow for a roaming defender in the paint, is at 41% and 33%, respectively.

Every 3-point shot that Hart takes is a win for the defense, and right now he's taking twice as many per game (7.0, up from 3.7 last year) as he has at any point in his career. As stated above, these are the wrong shots. If defenses are going to force Hart into seven 3-pointers each game, the Knicks are going to feel that offensively.

And we haven't even gotten to the worst shooter yet ...

Bridges was unquestionably one of the playoff heroes. After a slow start in the first round against Atlanta, he was incredible on both ends throughout New York's run. To say that he's been dreadful to start this season would be putting it politely.

Bridges is averaging three points -- 3.3, to be exact -- on 5-for-23 shooting to open the season. He's missed all 10 of his 3-point attempts and, in fact, hasn't made a single jump shot outside the paint. He's got two airballs and has been a shell of his postseason defensive self, and the numbers reflect all of this.

So far, the Knicks are registering a minus-26.1 net rating over 183 possessions with Bridges on the floor. Over 109 possessions with him on the bench, that number completely flips to +26.6 with what would rank as the best offense in the league.

Bridges, whose 10 points are the fewest he has scored over any three-game span since his second year in the league, still has three years and $109 million left on his current deal with a fourth-year player option for another $41.5 million in 2028-29.

As the Knicks come into a salary squeeze with Towns, Hart and, eventually, Brunson needing new deals, you have to wonder if Bridges might end up on the trade market if his play doesn't improve considerably. The Knicks likely aren't going to be able to keep everyone if Towns doesn't take a major discount and/or James Dolan backs off his no-second-apron stance.

The Knicks are turning the ball over on almost 17% of their possessions so far, up from 13.9% last season. That might not sound like a lot (it's about three turnovers per game more), and it's also true that New York turned the basketball over on 16.6% of its possessions in the playoffs and obviously fared more than fine. But it's the points these turnovers are directly leading to for their opponents that are killing them.

Through three games, the Knicks are conceding almost 21 points per game off their own turnovers. That's up almost five points from last year's mark, and almost six points from their playoff mark when they were really humming.

Against Orlando, the Knicks committed six fourth-quarter turnovers and lost the points-off-turnovers battle 23-12. All told, they are minus-20 in this category for the season. As Mike Brown said following Sunday's loss, "that's a hard gap to make up" when you're not shooting well.

New York's lagging transition defense has in part been due to the live-ball turnovers, but they're also just not consistently getting back with enough urgency and shooters are being left open.

Late in the second quarter against Orlando, Brunson missed the 3 on one end, then jogged back with his back turned to the play and nobody picked up a knockdown shooter in Desmond Bane, who hit a warmup 3. On the very next possession, Hart turned the ball over at half court and it led to another Bane transition 3. Two mistakes, a seven-point lead became a one-point lead in a matter of seconds.

In the opening-night loss, the Knicks gave up 24 fast-break points to the 76ers. According to Tom Haberstroh, that's the most fast-break points the Knicks have conceded in their last 58 games, including the playoffs.

The Knicks are actually doing a fine job of limiting transition possessions (only the Celtics are operating less in transition as a percentage of their total possessions defended), but when teams have been able to run on them, they have been cut up to the tune of 145.5 points per transition play -- the fourth-worst mark in the league, per Cleaning the Glass.

As Brown noted after the Orlando loss, there hasn't been any one factor on which this 0-3 start can be blamed. The Knicks are playing well in stretches and then letting up in others. They gave up 13 fast-break points against the Magic in the first half, cleaned that up to six in the second half, but then committed 11 turnovers.

Good shooting can cover for a lot of mistakes in basketball, but right now the Knicks are not shooting well and it's exacerbating all these other parts of the game that they are not executing at a consistent enough level. It's not time to panic, but New York definitely needs to get its act together sooner than later. There is no room for error in this Eastern Conference.

Actress Sophia Bush is sharing a candid look inside her personal life with her new memoir, “And I Will Tell You Mine: Notes from a Life in Progress.” However, there’s one topic she decided to leave out—her obsession with interior design.

During the Oct. 22 episode of her podcast, “Work in Progress with Sophia Bush,” the “One Tree Hill” alum dug into the details she left out of her book, with longtime friend Elizabeth Kott.

“Everyone should know that Sophia has an insane eye for interior design, and I don’t know if people really know that about you,” said Kott.

As she read through Bush’s memoir, she found herself hoping the star would divulge some details on her home design, Kott said.

In response to the assertion that Bush, 44, could pursue design as “an alternate career," given all of the “gorgeous” spaces she’s “created,” the “Grey’s Anatomy” cast member agreed it’s her “favorite thing.”

“I would love to have that 'Sliding Doors' moment with my own life and see what it might be if I were doing that for a living,” she said.

Bush went on to speculate that her interior design passion likely came from having a variety of residences throughout her career, which has spanned more than two decades.

“I’ve had so many places and spaces in other states. And when you live across state lines in multiple places at the same time, you kind of lose track,” she said. “I really respond to the place that I live in terms of the way my home is put together. The style of the building, the style of the town, you know, whatever it is.”

While Kott remarked that Bush is a “serious shopper”—a statement made in a “celebratory way”—the actress admitted that she has “hit a point” where she’s “consolidated” more home decor than she knows what to do with.

“Like, what am I going to do with all this sh--t?” she questioned. “One of my friends was like, ‘I think you need a Joanna Gaines-style warehouse, and then people should shop it for their clients.’”

Bush joked that it might not be a bad idea to tap the legendary HGTV star to find a solution for her impressive inventory of items.

“I should just DM Joanna, honestly, and be like, ‘Hey, girl, I’ve got to figure out what to do with all this stuff. I think you might want it,’” she quipped, also adding that a “cool, old estate” in Los Angeles might be a great home for her tchotchkes.

“I always joke, these are my treasures and tchotchkes,” Bush added, noting that her wife, retired Olympic soccer player Ashlyn Harris, 41, calls the collection her “trinkets and trash.”

Although Bush’s affinity for interior design is certainly ripe for material, she explained that it wasn’t a topic that felt like a fit for her memoir.

“I just caught myself cringe, because I’m like, ‘Ooh, I don’t know that I would write about my own good taste,’” she said. “I think that would be hard for me. But maybe I should figure out how. Maybe that’s a problem. Maybe it’s like, I need to learn to accept a compliment.”

The actress went on to explain that her design acumen goes back further than she can remember, and recalled a moment from “a couple of years ago,” when she “burned” her “whole life to the ground” and had spent the summer visiting flea markets with her mother—likely referring to 2023, when she had filed for divorce from her ex-husband, Grant Hughes.

“I was talking about, ‘Oh yeah, this looks cool, but they should move this wall, and windows should go here,’ and somebody was like, ‘How do you know that?’ And my mom goes, ‘Oh, she’s been like this since she was a little kid,’” she shared.

According to Bush, her mom continued the story, sharing that “it used to be so embarrassing” to bring her to visit friends’ homes as a child.

“‘We’d walk into somebody’s house, and here comes my 6-year-old being like, ‘Your door is in the wrong place,’” Bush recalled her mom sharing. “And I’m like, ‘I did? I don’t remember that, but I’m so sorry.’”

Bush also added that she enjoys hosting, noting, “I love to have a house full of people.”

With that said, she's still happy to keep some events private, and recently opened up about her decision to marry Harris in a secret backyard ceremony in upstate New York in summer 2025.

"When you get to this stage in your life, you begin to unpack what's yours and what's always sort of been for everyone else," she told Vogue.

Bush detailed the special occasion, explaining that they celebrated with her now-stepchildren, Sloane, 5, and Ocean, 4, whom Harris shares with her ex-wife and former teammate, Ali Krieger, 42.

"The kids were just running and happy, and we picked flowers from the property and our daughter Sloane was holding them," she said. "It was so fun for them, because it wasn't this pressure-filled day that's too much for young kids. It was so precious, and zero pressure. I will never forget it, and I think wedding days sometimes aren't like that."

The night included a marshmallow roast, and Bush and Harris later shared the news with friends and family in person.

#723. Maxxing named Dictionary.coms 2026 Word of the Year

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apparently, getting better at something now requires adding "maxxing" to its name. There's sleepmaxxing, proteinmaxxing, looksmaxxing, and even cozymaxxing, because a night on the couch can apparently be optimized, too.

As of Oct 26, "maxxing" is Dictionary.com’s 2026 Word of the Year. The publisher describes it as:

“the practice of pursuing an activity to the fullest or optimizing a certain aspect of your life.”

The shorthand for "maximizing" can describe a serious self-improvement effort or a joke about doing very little. Its versatility has helped it spread across beauty, wellness, work, and everyday internet conversation.

Dictionary.com traces the term’s roots through "min-maxing," a gaming strategy that involves developing a character’s strengths until their weaknesses matter less. Online communities eventually gave "maxxing" another "x" and an expanding list of things to work on.

One of its most recognizable forms is "looksmaxxing," which focuses on improving physical appearance. But the template has spread into workplace conversations about "tokenmaxxing," or using as much AI as possible, and lifestyle trends like "frictionmaxxing," which implores you to "rethink your relationship to your device — and your humanity." Increasingly, Dictionary.com says, people also use "maxxing" as a word on its own.

The dictionary recognition has been building, too. In September, Merriam-Webster added "looksmaxxing" among 1,400 new words and definitions, alongside "vibe-coding" and "cuffing season."

The word's history also goes beyond jokes about optimizing your bedtime. Looksmaxxing grew out of incel forums and sits within the broader manosphere, a network of online communities often associated with misogyny and rigid ideas about masculinity. Mashable has explored the subculture through its looksmaxxing glossary and coverage of its racist beauty standards and dehumanizing language about women.

One of looksmaxxing's most recognizable figures is Braden Peters, better known as Clavicular, who shares appearance-focused (and often misogynistic) advice with millions of followers. While looksmaxxing can involve everyday grooming and skincare, its more extreme forms include crash dieting and "bone smashing" — striking facial bones in the unfounded belief that doing so will create a more defined face. There's also "pheromone-maxxing," which involves skipping showers, avoiding deodorant, or wearing unwashed clothes in the hope that a stronger natural scent will attract potential partners.

The concerns extend beyond physical injury. New Zealand's Classification Office, for example, warns that some looksmaxxing content can encourage disordered eating, obsessive scrutiny of appearance, and feelings of hopelessness, while exposing viewers to misogynistic or extremist messaging. That history matters as the language becomes mainstream, even as people adopt "maxxing" for pursuits far removed from those communities.

Dictionary.com’s other finalists were "affordability" "data center," "odyssey," and "unc." Its explanations, respectively, pointed to the cost of living, debates over AI infrastructure, Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, and slang for someone considered older or out of touch.

Together, the choices offer a recognizable portrait of 2026: money worries, AI everywhere, a major movie event, and, yes, slang with roots in incel forums.

If that all sounds exhausting, there's always touching-grass-maxxing.

#5932. The Locals' Guide to the Mexico City Grand Prix Weekend

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Enrique del Bosque has attended his fair share of Formula 1 races around the world. From iconic circuits like the Autodromo Nazionale Monza in Italy—the famed home of Team Ferrari—to newer racetracks in Las Vegas and Lusail City in Qatar, the Mexican content creator with the largest F1-related TikTok following in Latin America has been a guest at plenty of paddocks and VIP parties.

Still, he says, nothing compares to the festive spirit that accompanies the Mexico City Grand Prix.

“Other [destinations] are mostly about the racing,” del Bosque says. “Mexico has its own flavor. The party is bigger, especially with important cultural events like Día de Muertos going on at the same time.” He also references chilango—a term used for Mexico City residents and their bombastic, out-of-pocket antics—that gives the city a uniquely local sporting energy.

Mexico City is no stranger to larger-than-life sporting events. Earlier this summer, the football-crazed capital hosted the 2026 World Cup. Online and elsewhere, fans raved about the experience, highlighting not just the sporting event itself but also Mexico City’s overall appeal as a host city, from its gastronomic attractions to its welcoming atmosphere for out-of-towners.

The Mexico City Grand Prix has become a much-anticipated race on the Formula 1 circuit.

The country’s storied motorsport lineage can be traced to 1963, when Formula 1 originally added a Grand Prix in Mexico’s capital. After an on-again, off-again run, it returned for good in 2015 after a complete rebrand, and is under contract until at least 2028.

Today, the Mexico City GP is recognized as one of the Formula 1 circuit’s intercontinental darlings. In 2025, the race drew 401,326 fans, making it the third most attended among F1’s 24 races. And F1’s overall popularity in Mexico is surging, with an estimated 48 million supporters across the country.

Much of that enthusiasm is fueled by Sergio “Checo” Pérez, the Guadalajara-born driver for the all-new Cadillac team, whose homecoming draws massive support: “Mexican fans are very creative. They’re always coming up with new chants and cheers,” Pérez told Condé Nast Traveler between his races at Bahrain and Singapore in early October. “I think a lot of people saw [the passion of Mexican fans] during the last World Cup. What happens here is difficult to explain until you experience it for yourself.”

This year will mark Pérez’s first time back at Mexico City’s Autódromo Hermanos Rodríguez since 2024, and on Wednesday morning, fans will gather to see him drive through Paseo de la Reforma, a popular thoroughfare that courses out from the gates of Chapultepec Park, in his 1,000 horsepower Cadillac.

Mexico City’s charm, however, extends beyond the racetrack—and for many travelers, the event is just a gateway into exploring the best the city has to offer. Few know that better than local resident Iván Saldaña. As founder of Mexican spirits company Casa Lumbre and co-creator of Almave, the world’s first non-alcoholic agave beverage, along with seven-time World Champion Lewis Hamilton, the tastemaker knows the ins-and-outs of CDMX as intimately as his business partner Hamilton knows the intricacies of a racetrack. “There is an appetite here, and I don’t mean only for food and drink. I mean for life.”

Another local who will attest to Mexico City’s neoteric pleasures is Lilmister P, a muralist and clothing designer from Iztapalapa, a community in the city’s outskirts known for its street culture. Last year, the artist designed sugar skull- or calaverita de azúcar-inspired luchador masks for F1 Williams racers Carlos Sainz and Alex Albon. The year prior, he created an F1-themed wheat paste mural that was featured in ESPN’s coverage of the Grand Prix. The Mexico City-raised artist also explores his hometown’s food scene as part of his “Ando Con Hambre” series on YouTube.

“The fandom keeps growing,” says Lilmister P. “Before it was more niche. But now during F1 race weekend, you will see lots of people all over, outside of the race track, in every neighborhood, riding public transit, wearing the gear. You can tell the fans are out. Everyone is singing, partying, and chanting.”

This year, hometown hero Checo Pérez kicks off race weekend in style, roaring down Paseo de la Reforma in his Cadillac.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The announcement followed the banking regulator finalizing its rules for the GENIUS Act, a stablecoin law to be implemented in January

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) has petitioned community banks to enter the payment stablecoin market as part of the regulator’s rules on the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for US Stablecoins (GENIUS) Act.

In a Monday X post, the OCC said that it was “encouraging the development of payment stablecoin capabilities within the community banking sector,” citing its finalized rules for the GENIUS Act. The payment stablecoin law is set to be enforced starting in January, and the OCC finalized its rules for the bill last week.

“Interested community banks are encouraged to consider applying to become, or for a subsidiary to become, a permitted payment stablecoin issuer, as applicable,” said the US banking regulator.

The plea to community banks came as many banking advocacy groups pushed back against the OCC’s proposed rules on stablecoin yield, with some arguing that they went against the congressional intent behind GENIUS. If enough community banks were to enter the stablecoin market, they could challenge the market share of crypto companies and financial institutions.

Community banks are already engaged in legal action against the OCC over the regulator’s approval for charter applications from crypto companies. Earlier this month, the Independent Community Bankers of America filed a lawsuit alleging that crypto companies had an advantage over community bankers by not being subject to the same regulatory requirements.

#3793. 5 Best Walter Koenig TV Shows That Aren't Star Trek, Ranked

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Walter Koenig has sadly passed away at 90 years old. He was best known for playing the role of Pavel Chekov on "Star Trek: The Original Series" and was brought in during the show's second season (a move, Koenig once said, that was done to appeal to a younger audience). Chekov was a Russian character seen working alongside the USS Enterprise's main crew, and he was cute, a little cocksure, and claimed that Russians invented Western Civilization. Although rarely highlighted on "Star Trek," he was an invaluable part of the series and appeared in several "Star Trek" feature films.

But Koenig was more than "Star Trek." He began accruing credits in the early 1960s, turning up in episodes of the day's hottest TV shows. Koenig started off by scoring a small role on the revolutionary soap opera "General Hospital," and from there, it was off to the races. He soon appeared on shows like "The Alfred Hitchcock Hour" and "Jericho," leading to his time on "Star Trek." Koenig continued to work from there, acting in various projects and taking on numerous voice roles for practically the rest of his days.

Many may not be familiar with Koenig's non-"Trek" career, so we're here to offer some guidance. The following is a list of notable, fun, funny roles that Koenig tackled on TV, including his most famous part outside of the "Star Trek" franchise.

Starting in 1986, young fans of Ivan Reitman's 1984 blockbuster "Ghostbusters" were treated to a creative and exciting animated spin-off TV show called "The Real Ghostbusters." The animated series re-envisioned the four leads from Reitman's film with new designs but very much the same character traits, and it sported some amazing voice performances. Lorenzo Music initially played Peter, Maurice LaMarche played Egon, Frank Welker played Ray, and Arsenio Hall played Winston after Ernie Hudson was rejected from reprising the role (for a very silly reason). Because of its episodic structure, "The Real Ghostbusters" had the leeway to introduce a wide menagerie of ghosts, ghouls, and demons, and it would even occasionally stray into the Lovecraftian.

Case in point: Vladimir Pavel Maximov, the character played by Walter Koenig in the episode "Russian About." Maximov was a scientist who had been laughed out of the scientific community for his zany theories. (The Titanic sank, he said, because it crashed into Elvis.) In the episode, Maximov comes to possess a Necronomicon-like grimoire called the Nameless Book, which has access to incantations that can summon the Old Ones. The Old Ones, as fans of H.P. Lovecraft can tell you, are a gaggle of evil gods that have been biding their time before reclaiming the Earth.

Maximov uses the Nameless Book to summon a god called Yibb-Tstll, much to the annoyance of the Ghostbusters. It's fun to hear Koenig play a goofy cartoon villain like this. Koenig was a talented voice actor, and folks loved his Russian accent. (Koenig, notably, was of Russian descent, although he was born in Chicago.)

The one-season sci-fi TV series "The Starlost" has a great premise. The series is set on board a 200-mile-long starship called Earthship Ark, outfitted with dozens of enormous biospheres, merrily making its way through the cosmos and hoping to relocate humanity to a new world. A century into the voyage, an accident on the ship seals off all the biospheres from the others, and everyone on board has to fend for themselves. The action of "The Starlost" takes place 405 years after said accident, in the year 2790. Keir Dullea plays Devon, an agrarian farmer who manages to flee his biome and discover the now-forgotten mission of Earthship Ark. Devon and a few of his friends spend the series exploring the ship. William Ostler plays the computer who instructs them.

Walter Koenig appeared in two episodes of "The Starlost" as a humanoid alien named Oro, hailing from the planet Exar. A year prior, Oro's ship crashed into the Ark, and he managed to get on board. He has since been tearing apart the Ark, looking for the components he needs to rebuild his ship and employing the aid of Idona (Alexandra Bastedo), who also fled one of the ship's biospheres.

Koenig plays Oro as a cold, calculating character, an opportunist with plans of his own. The character also returns a few episodes later, promising Devon and the crew a place to settle (naturally, he's lying for his own purposes). Koenig's presence on "The Starlost" was like getting a blessing from the sci-fi head honchos.

"The Starlost" was created by the infamously grumpy and undeniably great writer Harlan Ellison. It may be obscure these days, but it's worth looking into. I love the premise.

Gen-Xers like me remember Stretch Armstrong as a mere toy, a smiling superhero figure with goo-filled rubber limbs that could be stretched across a room. But I'll wager that not many of us were aware that in 2017, Netflix adapted Stretch Armstrong into an animated series called "Stretch Armstrong and the Flex Fighters." The new series was about a trio of teens who were accidentally exposed to a miracle chemical called Flexarium that turned them into stretchy, rubbery superheroes. They become Stretch (Scott Menvile), Wingspan (Steven Yuen), and Omni-Mass (Ogie Banks).

"Stretch Armstrong" also attracted a few "Star Trek" alumni. Kate Mulgrew from "Star Trek: Voyager" played a seeming antagonist in the first season of the series, before becoming a mentor figure in the second. Wil Wheaton from "Star Trek: The Next Generation" played Jonathan Rook, the benefactor of the Flex Fighters, but also secretly a were-monster. Keith David, Henry Rollins, Clancy Brown, and many other notable actors had small roles.

In a few episodes, Walter Koenig played a character named Mr. Savic, a mad scientist who created an intelligent plant creature called Anastasia (Tara Strong). He was a biology teacher who, in the show's second season, learns how to turn into a massive superhero monster called Biomass. Koenig brought a certain degree of energy and humanity to the role. He was a nerd with genuine enthusiasm. Audiences also see that Mr. Savic is a lonely man who only wanted to create a family for himself. It's actually a pretty great role.

In the "Futurama" episode "Where No Fan Has Gone Before," Fry (Billy West) learns that, in the 31st century, it is illegal to say the phrase "Star Trek." In the centuries since the present, Trekkies became religious zealots who started wars. Thereafter, Earth took precautions against future conflagrations by ridding the planet of all episodes of the original "Star Trek" series. The cast, reduced to living heads in jars, fled the planet.

Armed with Leonard Nimoy's head, Fry goes looking for the rest of the cast and finds that William Shatner, Walter Koenig, George Takei, DeForest Kelley, and Nichelle Nichols have been resurrected on a distant planet by a weird energy being named Melllvar (Maurice LaMarche), who has fallen in love with "Star Trek." Melllvar's "action figure" collection is complete when Fry brings Nimoy along. James (James Doohan was replaced by a new character named Welshie.)

The entire cast of "Star Trek" has a great sense of humor about their place in popular culture, and they are all game to make fun of themselves a little bit (Doohan notwithstanding). At one point, Fry points out that everyone can achieve their goals in life, pointing out that after "Star Trek," Walter Koenig became an actor.

That's a bit of an insult, but Koenig expands on the joke by saying that he became an actor, but also a person with friends and keys. The plot of the "Futurama" episode involves the cast of "Star Trek" fighting the cast of "Futurama" in hand-to-hand combat, and Koenig points out that he has no experience actually fighting, "except with you guys," he says to the "Star Trek" cast.

Koenig had a sense of humor, and that's important to remember. Shatner, meanwhile, called Fry voice actor Billy West to make sure the episode was on the up and up.

One of Walter Koenig's best roles was Alfred Bester in 12 episodes of "Babylon 5." Bester was a member of the Psi Corps, a cadre of human psychics that are, in the lore of the series, required to become cops or else be injected with psychic-dampening drugs. Because there are no checks to the organization's power, they hastily became somewhat fascistic. Fans of "Babylon 5" might be able to tell you about the show's storied Telepath War.

One of the show's wickedest Psi Cops was Bester, a war criminal who passed through Babylon 5 occasionally. Bester was first introduced investigating another rogue psychic, and presented as a cool, calming, insidious presence that was clearly using his low energy as a manipulation tool. While many admire Koenig for his energetic "Star Trek" performances, his best acting came from "Babylon 5." Bester was an arrogant figure, able to diffuse any threats made in his direction. There is a scene wherein Babylon 5's security chief Garibaldi (Jerry Doyle) says that he'd like to beat Bester like a piñata, and Bester takes it as a compliment: "So, you think of me as something bright and cheerful," he says, "full of toys and candy for young children? Thank you. That makes me feel much better about our relationship."

Unlike Chekov, Bester drove the stories on "Babylon 5," and his character had a grand arc and was given a great deal of background. If anyone only ever saw Koenig as a supporting player or a side character, know that he was capable of evil, pathos, and rich, intriguing characters. Bester might be the actor's best role, period.

Walter Koenig, known as the cheeky and endearing Russian navigator Pavel Chekov in the original Star Trek television series, died on Sunday at 90, The New York Times reports. Koenig played the tart-tongued character for the iconic show’s second and third seasons, and also reprised the role in six Star Trek movies featuring the original cast.

Koenig had a memorable stint in another space-themed show, Babylon 5, playing telepath Alfred Bester. He worked as a screenwriter on shows including Land of the Lost and Family. He voiced Chekov in numerous video games and also wrote several books.

But for many, he’ll forever be remembered as the likable ensign with a Monkees mop-top hairdo and distinct accent. The bestselling author behind “The Let Them Theory” and host of “The Mel Robbins Podcast” spent years raising her family outside of Boston before relocating to Southern Vermont.

Along the way, her homes have accommodated growing children, an expanding business, and renovations that required some difficult decisions.

Her former family home was in Sherborn, MA. From there, they packed up their belongings and relocated to a house on 26 acres in Vermont.

The move also presented Robbins and her husband, Chris, with an existing family connection: The Vermont house was built by his parents.

Robbins has discussed buying and renovating it on her podcast, sharing both her initial reluctance to relocate and the features that eventually helped make it a home.

Her circumstances are a considerable departure from the financial difficulties she has described.

Robbins previously faced $800,000 in debt, alongside problems in her marriage, before establishing her successful publishing and podcasting career.

“I wake up every day and I am profoundly grateful for making a decision to change myself for the better because that’s what started it all. One decision changes your life," she told People.

Robbins’ former Massachusetts residence was a small, older farmhouse where space became an increasingly important consideration as her family and work evolved.

On her podcast, she recalled that the house initially had a dirt basement. Even after the family added a concrete floor, its low ceiling limited how they could use the space.

The narrow layout also made it difficult to accommodate the gatherings she had hoped to host for her children and their friends.

An addition created more room, although it introduced another challenge.

Robbins explained that she and Chris refinanced the property to build a garage with a room above it. She envisioned that upstairs space as a playroom for the children, but an antique pool table from her father took up much of the available area.

Over time, the table served purposes well beyond playing pool. The children used it for Lego bricks, Robbins folded laundry on it, and a plywood covering allowed it to function as a work surface as her business grew.

She ultimately decided to dismantle it so the room could better accommodate the household’s needs.

Leaving the property brought a different kind of challenge.

In a 2022 video titled “We Just Sold Our House. I Was Not Ready To Feel This,” Robbins discussed the emotional experience of parting with the family home after 24 years.

The Vermont purchase emerged as the family considered a change for their son, Oakley, who wanted to attend high school there.

Robbins has said she initially opposed leaving Massachusetts, where her company was based.

Meanwhile, Chris’ mother was considering downsizing and had offered her children the opportunity to buy the house she and her husband had built.

After reconsidering, Robbins asked Chris to arrange an appraisal. They bought the property and renovated it, although she has acknowledged that adjusting to the move was difficult.

According to reports, Robbins and Chris lived in Southern Vermont, while her media company, 143 Studios, remained based in Boston.

The Vermont property gave Robbins an opportunity to address something she had missed in Massachusetts: a dedicated place where her children could spend time with friends.

She described commissioning a small barn outbuilding with durability in mind. Rather than worry about delicate furnishings or the occasional mishap, she wanted a space that could withstand young people gathering, playing pool, and enjoying themselves.

She used the project as an example of a personal goal that required deliberate attention, even when the necessary space was already waiting.

Another family connection appears in the entryway: an antique cabinet inherited from her great-grandmother, which Robbins has described as having a marble top. In everyday use, it has held keys and dog leashes.
