#93. How the streets and social media drove Lula to a historic comeback in Brazil

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A viral WhatsApp channel and student-led protests played a key role in the veteran Brazilian leftist’s election victory

After the first round of the Brazilian presidential election three weeks ago, the leftist incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva reportedly told allies he could not understand how he finished second after a government he considered the best of his three terms.

A mood of dismay swept Brazil’s left, and because 9 million voters for the other candidates, all of them on the right, were expected to mostly transfer to senator Flávio Bolsonaro, analysts widely saw the far-right senator’s victory as certain.

Then, on the night after the election, students from the Fluminense Federal University took to the streets of Niterói, a city of 480,000 across Guanabara Bay from Rio de Janeiro, to protest against the return to power of a member of the Bolsonaro family, whose patriarch, former president Jair Bolsonaro, Flávio’s father, is under house arrest for attempting a coup.

Two days later, another student protest took over downtown Rio and, in the days that followed, similar ones spread across the country in organic demonstrations that analysts and Lula himself agree gave new life to his campaign and played a decisive role in his historic comeback in the runoff.

“I want to thank the generosity of Brazil’s young people who took to the streets to defend democracy,” Lula said in his victory speech on Sunday night after defeating Bolsonaro.

Political scientist Fábio Vasconcellos said Lula’s victory, in which for the first time in Brazil’s democratic history a second-placed candidate overturned a first-round defeat in the runoff, could be explained by a combination of the streets, social media and the machinery of government – in recent weeks, Lula approved a “package of benefits” at the limits of what electoral law allows, including increasing and bringing forward payments from the main cash-transfer programme for poor families.

“The streets and social media almost overlap,” said Vasconcellos, a researcher with the Representation and Democratic Legitimacy group at the Federal University of Paraná.

Two days after the first round, congressman André Janones, a Lula ally, created a WhatsApp channel called “Lula’s Army”, where Janones sent audio messages, videos – often beginning with shouts of “Guys, urgent!” – and dozens of daily instructions on how to make pro-Lula content perform well on social media.

The channel had 60,000 followers by the end of its first day and, by election Sunday, more than 10 million, and Lula’s digital reach overtook Bolsonaro’s.

In his victory speech, Lula called Janones “our general”, who had the role of “raising the morale of our troops every day”. The congressman’s relentless efforts, which included a 24-hour YouTube live stream on the eve of the vote, led to him being admitted to hospital last week – the only day the WhatsApp channel went silent.

Lula’s campaign also overhauled his online communications, and the incumbent, who turns 81 on Tuesday, began recording selfie videos despite not owning a mobile phone. On Sunday night, he said he had been given a phone and that “from now on I’m going to talk a lot to the Brazilian people, many times a day, directly”.

With the streets and social media, “the mood of defeat was gradually replaced by hopes of a comeback over those three weeks,” said Vasconcellos.

Camila Rocha, a political scientist who studies Brazil’s new right, said Lula’s comeback also represented “a broad rejection of the Bolsonarista project for Brazil”.

She has been interviewing undecided voters since May and said “the fear voters had, workers had, of losing rights and also of a possible concentration of power in Bolsonaro’s hands was very clear”, which meant that even people who did not like Lula voted for him this time.

Bolsonaro had won 56.1m votes in the first round and received 3.7 million more in the runoff. Lula, by contrast, went from 53.9m to 64.2m. The more than 10m-vote increase came both from persuading voters to turn out after historically high abstention in the first round and from convincing voters from other candidates, including Bolsonaro.

“Some undecided voters we interviewed said Bolsonaro was a playboy who sided with big business, while at the same time spontaneously saying that, if he won, there would be a dictatorship in which people would lose rights,” said Rocha.

Bolsonaro was also embroiled in several controversies recently, including saying he would use the new, even more conservative congress to change the constitution and “re-democratise” Brazil, as well as the two federal police inquiries in which he is under investigation over alleged corruption and money laundering.

Many analysts have also pointed to the weight of Lula himself. “If it were another candidate, Bolsonaro would already have won in the first round,” said political scientist Helcimara de Souza Telles.

Marco Aurélio de Carvalho, Lula’s campaign manager, answered without hesitation when asked what explained the historic election fightback: “President Lula. We have a great statesman on our team. Vasconcellos believes Lula’s re-election campaign, “from what we saw on the streets, offered the Brazilian left an opportunity for renewal”.

“Lula’s party can learn from what happened and find better ways to connect with young people and urban voters who feel their concerns are not being heard,” he added.

#5447. Germany’s 300-Meter Wind Tower Faces a 199-Meter Competitor

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Germany’s GICON has completed one of the more remarkable wind-turbine construction projects in recent years. Its Schipkau prototype reached a hub height of 300 meters in September, putting the upper blade tip at a reported 365 meters. The lattice structure and telescoping inner tower allow the turbine assembly to be raised into place without a conventional crane lifting it to that extreme height. The engineering achievement is substantial, and the attraction of stronger winds higher above the ground is easy to understand.

But while GICON was developing its tower, conventional wind towers were getting taller too. Max Bögl recently erected a Vestas V172-7.2 MW with a 199-meter hub on a series-production hybrid tower. That changes the commercial comparison considerably. The generation advantage of reaching 300 meters looks impressive against a conventional tower at 100 or 150 meters. It becomes a different proposition when developers can already choose a hub approaching 200 meters.

I modeled two hypothetical wind farms using identical 7.2 MW turbines, rotor diameters and turbine counts, changing only their hub heights from 199 to 300 meters. Under the screening model’s wind assumptions, the taller farm produces 11.4% more electricity annually. That’s valuable additional output from the same generating equipment, although the gain varies considerably with the site’s vertical wind profile. Measurements at Schipkau suggest that some locations could do substantially better.

The construction question is less straightforward. GICON’s approach addresses some of the transport and lifting problems associated with very tall conventional towers, but a giant lattice structure has a different assembly burden. The prototype required thousands of components and extensive work at height. The published construction record also includes an interruption for steel-quality checks. Future towers should benefit from the experience, although repeat construction costs and schedules have yet to be established.

That timing matters. Turbines begin earning as they enter service, while construction costs arrive much earlier. My assessment models both farms’ progressive commissioning, additional electricity, field labor and discounted receipts using a provisional reference-class construction allowance. The results show how strongly the commercial case depends on repeatable installation performance, with important costs still requiring supplier quotations.

There will be sites where reaching stronger winds at 300 meters is attractive, particularly where existing permits, land access or grid connections make additional production valuable. Schipkau has established that an extreme-height tower can be built. Whether the approach becomes commercially competitive depends on what subsequent installations achieve.

The full assessment examines the construction evidence, alternative labor and scheduling assumptions, financial thresholds, and the implications for wildlife and site selection. It’s available in TFIE Strategy Briefing, along with the assumptions supporting the model.

#4434. Pearley: A Mean Jaguar Hot-Rod and the Fight For Humanity

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A project like Mick Jenkins' hot-rod E-Type takes a village.

Mick Jenkins’ personal project is proceeding nicely. Known for his spectacular paintwork on show cars and high-end restorations, Jenkins is building a hot-rod version of a Series I Jaguar E-Type coupe. It is, he told me last year, “The bastard child of a 427 Cobra and an E-Type.” Checking in on its progress a few weeks ago, it’s a wicked mix of Jenkins’ native British pride and the primal hot-rod mania of his adoptive homeland, Southern California.

But while Jenkins’ Jag is wholly his idea, he’s not building it alone. What he’s doing is gathering up the expertise from the fanatics who twist Jaguars in Britain and the craft of insane Americans. Plus, its Tremec five-speed transmission is made in Mexico.

There’s a lot of ego that goes into building cars, whether they’re one-off customs or Hondas being spit out by the hundreds of thousands in Ohio. Enzo Ferrari organized his Scuderia, then leaned into the expertise of Aurelio Lampredi and Giacchino Colombo to design the V-12 engines that powered the cars. Henry Ford established the moving production line to make the Model T, but between 1903 and 1913 relied upon John and Horace Dodge (among others) to supply parts and engines. Every car is the result of a collaboration. No one builds a car alone.

And Road & Track isn’t built by one person either.

It remains a mystery to me why anyone develops an enthusiasm for anything. But identifying some passion is how we start to learn about it. And sooner or later, that means hanging with other people to soak up what they know or what they’ll share. We all start at zero, but only fools believe they can’t learn from others. And bigger fools think they can go it alone from there.

Cars and car culture are human connections. And we’re on the brink of that diminishing. Last year, I needed some advice on changing the headlight buckets on my Tundra. I could have bought a service manual, but that would have taken too much time and effort. So, I looked up a YouTube video. This week, I asked ChatGPT the same question and 29 seconds later, it delivered seven-step directions and a list of the tools needed. Kind of amazing. But I miss seeking out real humans… plus I wound up using zip ties.

To build his monster E, Mick is using a bellhousing from Simply Performance, the 6.8-liter version of the Jag V-12 is built by Chris Tramel (long part of the hive mind at the Ed Pink race shop in SoCal), and the narrowed rear end also comes from Simply Performance in Britain. The big six-piston caliper brakes that fit inside 15-inch wheels are from Fosseway Performance, which is, yup, in England. All stuff one person could develop and build, if that person had several lifetimes in which to pursue it all with fanatic focus.

A couple weeks ago, the R&T crew came together at our annual Performance Car of the Year (PCOTY) fiesta. Like so many enterprises today, our staff is far-flung—some in New York City, our core centered in Michigan, and then a few of us scattered around California. The only time we regularly get together is at PCOTY.

As PCOTY concluded, we all gathered for the traditional concluding dinner at an excellent sushi restaurant in a mall across from our hotel in Windsor, California. Our ages range from 22 to, gack, 65, and our experiences and talents are well varied. Even though, at least right now, all us writers and editors on staff are dudes.

Though, yes, the women who work with us save our asses on the production side practically every day. They’re the ones who keep the business going and the text readable.

We’re the people who hand-build each issue of the magazine and every story on the website. We should spend more time together, but the fact that we do at all is kind of amazing considering the distances involved.

When Mick Jenkins finishes his human-conceived, human-made E-Type, it will weigh far less than 3000 pounds and have more than 500-hp thrumming from its V-12. It will produce sensations that no intelligence could appreciate unless it’s contained inside a human head. My head is ready.

Cars are machines, but our love of them is purely human. By reading this, you’re sharing that love with me and everyone who works on R&T. Over this last 100 years, cars have been regulated, restricted, criticized, and commodified in ways that should mitigate our passion. But, well, we’re still car freaks.

The challenge in the immediate future will be our passion being tracked and churned through computers that produce algorithms leveraging it for commercial advantage. Some good will come of that, but we need to be aware of that manipulation. We must fight to sustain human connections that make this worthwhile.

And Mick’s nasty Jaguar, unattached to any electronic logic circuit, all visceral and mean, and fully plumbed into our car-loving soul, reminds me of that challenge.

John Pearley Huffman has been writing about cars since 1990 and is getting okay at it. Besides Road & Track, his work has appeared in Car and Driver, the New York Times, and more than 100 automotive publications and websites. A graduate of UC Santa Barbara, he still lives near that campus with his wife and two children. He owns a pair of Toyota Tundras and two dogs. He used to have a Nova and a Camaro.
