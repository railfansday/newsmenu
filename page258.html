#4562. "I was a real anglophile": A Canadian's obsession with Viz magazine is why Grand Theft Auto: London's streets are covered in dogs' eggs

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Sadly, there are no vibrating bum-faced goats

The Grand Theft Auto games have a long history of being made by developers who live far from their source material. The original game and its mainline sequels have all been led by Rockstar North, housed in a decidedly not US-based Edinburgh. But, even when it came to a comparatively local GTA mission pack set in London, the team chose instead to ask their newly acquired Canadian team to handle the development work. It didn't matter that only a few people on the team had actually been to London before.

Yet, that distance, as proven across the series grants the developers a more incisive view of a place, one that focuses more on a setting's cultural rendering than its reality.

As evidenced by Grand Theft Auto: London's dog shit.

Ahead of the launch of GTA 6, the London culture-focused publication Shortlist has published a making of Grand Theft Auto: London by Jeremy Peel, that writer what's often spotted hanging around RPS's backdoor looking for a way onto our rarefied pages. It covers the unlikely story of how a newly acquired Canadian studio was renamed Rockstar Canada and put to work turning Rockstar's open world crime 'em up into a game set in London during the swinging sixties.

It's an odd game to look at. Built with the same tools as the original game, the developers managed to roughly recreate the geography of London and make it so cars drove on the left-hand side of the road, with drivers even getting in through the correct side to access a right-hand drive steering wheel, but the streets remain hopelessly American. There are no curves to the roads, instead they are wide lanes with right-angled corners like you'd see in New York and LA.

Still, despite being Canadians working on a British game engine built to make games set in America, there was a cultural export that left its mark on the team: Viz magazine. The long-running parody of The Beano had somehow made its way over to Canada and into the hands of artist Ray Larabie. “I was a real anglophile,” he said. “I knew all about Newcastle upon Tyne and the curry shops and the Kray twins from Viz magazine. I had this twisted view of England. I remember going over there and asking normal people about Viz magazine. And they were like, ‘We’re adults, we don't read that. That is for juvenile young men.’”

I don't know how the creators of the vibrating bum-faced goats, who named their 2012 annual the Cleveland Steamer, or has a long-running character called Johnny Fartpants could be called juvenile. But, then, there's no accounting for taste.

Larabie told Shortlist he managed to get a particular detail from Viz's pages into GTA: London “London was famous for having dog shit everywhere in the 60s. So I put little dogs’ eggs, as they call them in Viz, on the sidewalk. And set it so that if you see a poop on the sidewalk, you can do a handbrake on it and leave a big brown skid mark. That’s my only innovation I ever made in videogames.”

I recommend reading the full making of feature, but it's that globehopping thread of culture that I'm particularly fascinated by. There is certainly an argument that stories should be told by the people closest to them, but something the GTA games have consistently revealed isn't the actuality of the countries they're set in but the cultural lens through which we view them. While also true of the earlier games, from GTA 3 onwards, Rockstar's references to the films they're inspired by become more and more overt. Badfellas posters litter Liberty City in GTA 3, scenes in Vice City are lifted straight from Scarface, and missions in GTA 4 recreate the heists in Heat.

If I had to pick the cultural touchpoints that represent the UK, I wouldn't know where to start and there are so many conflicting choices you could make: Fawlty Towers, Our Friends in the North, Mr Bates vs The Post Office. But an outsider likely has a narrower field to pluck from.

Which makes the fact that the field includes Viz and its long-running joke of dogs' eggs is a real joy.

The Edmunds EV Range Test is designed to offer a more accurate estimate of a vehicle’s range during real-world, everyday driving conditions. It consists of 60% city and 40% highway driving with an average speed of 40 mph.

The vehicle stays within 5 mph of the posted speed limit, and the climate control is set on auto at 72 degrees.

#4401. PlayStation founder Ken Kutaragi says discs became "metaphor for 'owning' games", and that upset at their discontinuation is a "mentality issue"

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ken Kutaragi, the founder of PlayStation and architect of the first three PlayStation consoles, has said that people being upset about Sony's discontinuation of game discs is probably a "mentality issue". He understands it, he noted, but that doesn't make it "economically reasonable" for Sony to keep printing them.

Kutaragi was speaking to Video Games Chronicle at the Game Republic New Horizons event, where he said: "I understand that physical discs have become a metaphor for 'owning' games. I really understand that mentality... But nobody thinks [physical media] is necessary for a networked device like a smartphone, for example, because people feel that they will keep their content. So it's a mentality.

"I really understand [why some people are upset]," he added, "but I don't think it's economically reasonable. Maybe it's a mentality issue."

Kutaragi retired from PlayStation in June 2007, shortly after the PlayStation 3's European release, so he's been out of the company for a while. Two generations of consoles have passed since he left, though I'm sure he has close ties to Sony and people working there still. "I'm not the CEO at PlayStation any more," he noted in the interview, "but we still have some common [view points]."

Maybe Sony is pushing towards an all-digital device, he remarked, in a similar way that we see smartphones and other devices. "Maybe that's how those people are thinking," he said. "But this has caused some friction with game enthusiasts."

Kutaragi, who's currently the CEO of Ascent Robotics and owner of Cyber AI Entertainment, also expressed concern at the price of hardware in today's component shortage-ridden market. He said machines are "super expensive and that's not a healthy situation"; comments backed up by news from the weekend that PlayStation 6's memory and storage alone reportedly cost more than $680.

Kutaragi's advice? Harness AI and cloud computing to their full potential and "we won't need any console". I don't think that'll go down terribly well, Ken.

#4571. "Sexy sorceresses" and "bare-chested fighters": Dragon Age's original artists wanted to base it on Conan the Barbarian, much to the outrage of the designers

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Former lead writer David Gaider on the perils of not having a "vision keeper"

Buried in the vast and sooty-fingered Summer Eternal anthology, there's an interview with former BioWare developer and Dragon Age lead writer David Gaider, who is now creative director at Stray Gods outfit Summerfall Studios. The interview is a podcast-length account of BioWare's transformations over Gaider's career, stretching back to Baldur's Gate 2. Amongst other things, it explains why the Dragon Age games feel so different from sequel to sequel.

Gaider discusses the original trilogy's fortunes under RPG-phobic parent company Electronic Arts, commenting that 2009's Dragon Age: Origins made it to release partly thanks to "sunk cost" thinking after EA bought the studio. He also talks about Dragon Age's lack of a properly defined vision, early on, which led to a lot of overcorrection between releases. As regards Origins, there was something of a faction war initially between departments over what exact flavour of fantasy fiction the new RPG should draw on.

"When we started, Dragon Age's big premise was 'it's D&D, but it's not," Gaider began. "We'll just file off the serial numbers enough to make it our own. That was not great because there are different kinds of fantasy."

Specifically, a rift emerged at BioWare between developers who wanted to channel Tolkien, and those who wanted to invoke Robert E. Howard's Conan the Barbarian stories. "If you look back at the E3 Expo, where we announced the existence of Dragon Age, and you watch the video, you will see," Gaider went on (the screening in question was behind closed doors, sadly, so all I can do is point you towards IGN's coverage at the time). "It was mostly made by our art department. They were part of the Conan group. And if you watch it, you'll see sexy sorceresses, the fighter is this bare-chested barbarian riding around… it looks very Conan.

"And that part of the team was determined that their vision was set out," he said. "The design department was mostly like, 'no, this is Lord of the Rings, man, okay? Fuck Conan.' But Art didn't want to listen to us, so we're like 'If you're going to ignore us, we'll ignore you.' And that's really why the Dragon Age that came to be is the way it is. If you look at Dragon Age: Origins, if I said to you, this is the art style of a Conan world with the writing style of a Lord of the Rings, then you'd be like, 'Oh.'"

When teams are at odds in this way, it's helpful to have a third group intervene to thrash out a compromise. The producers, for example. Unfortunately, Gaider continued, Dragon Age's production team had their own wild ideas about the project's direction - they thought it should be Diablo. This did, in fairness, have some kind of unifying effect. "Both the art department and the design department completely ignored them. Like, no, we're not doing Diablo."

Nonetheless, the producers persisted. "They wanted that if you hit creatures, they'd just explode in blood," Gaider said. "We actually had presentations where the people in charge were like, 'Yeah, Dragon Age, we want that the theme is blood! Dripping with blood!' And I'm like, 'That's not a theme.' They did get their way a little bit, Dragon Age was pretty bloody. People did almost explode in blood. We had to tone it down a bit because that just didn't feel right."

I realise there's a serious argument being made here about the necessity of knowing your game's heart, before you commit to knocking out scripts, models and trailers, but I am also having fun imagining the inter-departmental squabbles. I picture Gaider bumping into one of the Dragon Age artists outside an elevator in Edmonton, sometime in 2004. The artist's eyes narrow. "Tell me, David," they growl. "What is best in life?" Gaider's knuckles whiten. "To take the ring to Mordor, throw it into Mount Doom, and hear the lamentation of Sauron," he hisses. At this point the elevator doors open and a producer appears, apoplectic and roaring: "Ahh, fresh meat!"

According to Gaider – who would eventually become "vision keeper" or "educator" for Dragon Age as a series - BioWare did a much better job of getting the band together with Mass Effect, mostly thanks to project director Casey Hudson.

"Mass Effect went through much the same kind of struggle," he said. "It's science fiction. It's 'their Star Wars,' is what that was supposed to be. But there's so many different kinds of science fiction. And they had the benefit that Casey Hudson was the responsible person, and realised sooner than Dragon Age did that we need a singular vision. And he eventually put his foot down and said, 'This is what it is.'

"I think, honestly, Mass Effect as a series had a much stronger throughline of vision," Gaider concluded. "This is a game that is very clear on what it is and what it isn't. Whereas Dragon Age started off kind of a jumble and we figured it out as we went. With Dragon Age: Origins, versus Dragon Age II, versus Dragon Age: Inquisition, you've clearly got a team that is figuring out the world of the series by the seat of their pants and reacting with each release."

To address the Qunari in the room, here: Gaider left many years before Dragon Age: The Veilguard, whose development was a whole 'nother layer of troubled. EA - who are now owned by a consortium consisting mostly of Saudi Arabia's sovereign investment fund - appear to have paused the series in the wake of Veilguard, though modders are trying to keep the flames alive.

What's your poison, then? Conan, Tolkien or Diablo? While we're talking office politics at giant corporate developers, the Summer Eternal anthology also harbours a lengthy chat with Dragon Age's former executive producer, Mark Darrah. He has words of warning about "over-specialised" studios and $100 million projects that need to sell ridiculous quantities to break even. As far as I can remember, Darrah doesn't spend any of his chinwag lamenting the great Diablo-like that might have been if it weren't for the Gandalf-loving pinkos in the writing team or those Cromulent numpties in art.

#4933. Scientist tattoos himself with disappearing ink

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 19, 2026 3:57 PM EDT

Tattoos are pretty permanent decisions, and laser removal is often described as more painful than actually getting them in the first place. But what about a design that can only be seen when you want to see it? Engineers at the University of Colorado Boulder are working on a new type of tattoo ink to answer that question. Their dye is specifically concocted to physically alter depending on exposure to visible or ultraviolet (UV) light.

The team described their invention in a study published earlier this month in the journal Matter & Light, and they didn’t only demonstrate the capabilities in the lab on silicone and pig skin. One of its creators, Carson Bruns, has already tattooed himself with the ink.

“[It’s] almost a tradition,” the study co-author recently told Nature.

Bruns, a nanoscientist at UC Boulder, previously developed a now-commercialized tattoo product called “Magic Ink” that turns magenta depending on its exposure to UV light. To highlight its efficacy, Bruns gave himself “solar freckles” as a method to track his exposure to sunlight. In similar fashion, he recently completed a similar session on his leg using the disappearing ink.

The formula currently comes in three colors—yellow, blue, and magenta—and essentially relies on the same principles behind transition lenses and some textiles. Each dye is composed of tiny spheres of acrylic glass. These beads contain photochromes, which are chemical compounds engineered to absorb specific light wavelength photons. In this case, the photochomic dye’s spheres begin in their original color, but then flatten into transparency after absorbing corresponding light photons exposed to UV rays. The dye remains this way until it absorbs visible light photons, which destroys the chemical bond and allows the spheres to return to their original shape.

Aside from the lab tests, Bruns tattooed his own leg over about 12 months using various dye combinations, all of which responded as intended to UV and visible light exposure. The ink also remained effective for over two years—but the team’s intention is go beyond a nifty parlor trick. Future ink iterations may be particularly useful in medical procedures, such as temporary markings to help maintain accuracy during direct radiotherapy sessions. For now, however, the photochromic tattoo dyes remain for laboratory (and Bruns’) use only until further safety assessments are completed.

#4025. 10 Hacks Every AllTrails User Should Know

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AllTrails is a popular tool for finding walking, hiking, running, and biking routes around the world. You can use it to locate trails and check user reviews, but the app has plenty of features for customizing activities, tracking conditions, and navigating (safely) in real time. Here are the tips and tricks to get the most out of AllTrails.

The elevation map is useful for visualizing total gain and loss on your route, but you can scrub the chart on mobile to see exactly where climbs or obstacles are on the map. For example, you can see where the grade increases (and what the percentage is) or how far you are from stream crossings or switchbacks. Open the map from the trail page or the Navigate function and drag your finger across the elevation profile to track the location marker on the map.

You may be most likely to search for trails based on length and difficulty as well as location, but AllTrails has a wealth of filters to find routes with specific features, or that are dog- or kid-friendly. You can also use filters to identify trails that are likely to be less crowded (though if a route is on AllTrails, it certainly isn't a secret). In the Search window, tap All to open available filters. Select Not very busy under Visitation and Loop under Route type (out-and-back trails can feel busier). Select a rating and add any other requirements, then hit Show trails.

When you use AllTrails to log an activity or write a review, a map of your track is saved to the trail listing and visible to other users. You can disable this in Settings > Privacy settings > Content—but even if you don't display your activity, you can use those that are public to find custom routes that string multiple trails together or add a ridgeline not included in the official AllTrails track. Scroll down to the reviews section and tap the activities link to pull up maps of completed activities. Note, though, that some variations in routes may not be actual detours, and you should still follow Leave No Trace principles and stay within your skill level when it comes to difficulty.

A free AllTrails account will get you most of the service's basic features, but you have to pay for a Plus subscription—$35.99 per year—for things like offline maps and wrong-turn alerts. If you have an REI membership, you get a 30% discount on paid AllTrails subscriptions, bringing the annual fee down to $24.99 per year. (A Peak subscription that usually costs $79.99 per year drops to $49.99 per year.) In the AllTrails app, go to your profile > Settings > REI Membership and follow the steps to connect your account and claim the discount.

User reviews can give you a good sense of current trail conditions, such as seasonal closures and whether there are muddy or snowy conditions, as well as more global environmental considerations like bugs and river crossings. You should obviously read the most recent reviews for your route, but you can also keyword search on desktop (CTRL/CMD + F) for terms like "mud," "scramble," "snow," "gate," "bugs," or "ticks." A trail may be marked as "easy," but this can reveal issues like snowmelt that have made running water trickier to wade through. You can read the reviews from the same month in previous years too.

When you search trails and trailheads by name on popular mapping apps, you may not always get directions to the exact starting location pinned on AllTrails. Instead, launch directions from the AllTrails listing to use the GPS coordinates, which you can then search in Google Maps or Apple Maps. On the trail page, scroll down to Get directions, then select your preferred map app (or copy the coordinates to the clipboard).

When you record an activity in AllTrails, you have the option to add a waypoint, which is essentially a note pinned to a specific location on the route. This can be useful if you want to remember details, such as tricky turns or hidden features, and you can opt into adding your waypoints to community layers to help other users. Open the Navigator in the mobile app and tap the arrow in the lower-right corner. Swipe up and tap Add Waypoint. Select the waypoint type, add details and select privacy settings, and tap Save.

AllTrails maps have optional layers that contain a lot more data than just the route itself. All users have access to the Wildfire overlay, which shows current wildfire perimeters in the U.S. With a premium subscription, you get additional overlays for precipitation, temperature, ground conditions, snow depth, fall foliage, and community heat maps so you can see real-time environmental conditions at that specific location. A Peak subscription unlocks advanced overlays for slope angle, high-definition terrain, and public and private lands. Open a map and tap the Layers button in the upper-right corner.

Basic trail safety includes letting someone know exactly where you're going and when you plan to return. The AllTrails Live Share feature (available on paid plans) centralizes this information and shows trusted contacts where you are in real time so they can send help if things go wrong. When you share a link with safety contacts, they'll be able to see your real-time location, planned route, recorded mileage and elapsed time, estimated end time, coordinate info, and cell signal strength. They can also opt into SMS alerts. Activate Live Share under the Navigate tab—you'll need to generate a unique link each time you head out.

#4407. Japanese acting union fights back against AI-generated voice use, proposing strict new guidelines, especially regarding the use of a deceased actor's voice

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Technological advancements will not stop, but..."

The Japan Actors Union has issued new rules, guidelines, and restrictions related to AI-generated voice use. This includes an outright ban on the replication of the voices of dead actors, unless the actor had authorised its use during their lifetime.

Per Automaton, these guidelines are meant to protect an industry that has been exposed to exploitation by generative AI. One example includes voice actor Kenjiro Tsuda (Jujutsu Kaisen and Chainsaw Man) taking Tiktok to court in an attempt to remove AI-generated voice imitations from the platform. He would go on to lose that case, but not without setting a precedent that a person's voice is subject to protection under publicity rights laws.

The guidelines cover four major points. The first is focused on consent and establishes that permission must be granted at "every stage" of the generative AI process. This would mean AI companies require permission from actors before models are trained on their performances.

The second point, meanwhile, states that AI replications of an actor's work are to be counted as a new performance under the guidelines. This would mean royalties or compensation must be paid by AI companies to the actors involved.

Next up comes generative AI-powered translations, which takes an actor's performance and automatically changes it to another language. These must maintain the "artistic integrity" of the original performance, the guidelines state, and may not be used without permission in overseas regions.

Lastly, the Japan Actors Union covers generative AI use regarding a dead actor's work. The union's guidelines ban the use of generative AI here, unless that actor had given express permission for the software's use while they were alive. Even with this, an AI company would require permission from the deceased actor's family, the agency or company the actor was affiliated with, and to pay royalties.

"The Japan Actors Union will strive to not just establish these guidelines as industry-wide rules, but also strengthen its efforts to encourage the government to legislate regulations that protect 'voice rights'," wrote The Japan Actors Union (translated by Automaton). "Technological advancements will not stop, but the 'living, breathing human expression' and a 'respect for rights' must always remain at its core."

It is worth noting that these guidelines are not laws and therefore are not binding rules AI companies must follow. Even so, it marks a major push from the Japan Actors Union for this to change. These new guidelines contribute to mounting pressure against the generative AI industry in Japan, as actors continue to push for better protections.

Japan's Voice Actor Union is not the only one pushing back against generative AI. In Western countries, similar collectives have been speaking out about the technologies' impact on the field, with one describing it as "catastrophic".

The Roman empire at its height included over 1.9 million square miles of territory, including much of central Europe and central Asia. Augusta Traiana quickly grew into one of Thrace’s most prominent urban hubs, becoming so prosperous that Rome ultimately granted permission to build its own coin mint during the second and third centuries CE.

Over 1,750 years later, Augusta Traiana’s remnants exist underneath a city once again known as Stara Zagora. Ruins of the Roman empire are regularly unearthed through the town, including the recent discovery of a large cemetery used for nearly three centuries outside Augusta Traiana’s original walls. Among the 135 graves, researchers identified a child buried with a pair of gold earrings and one of Thrace’s bronze coins. Although details about the young individual’s death and identity remain unclear, the coin clearly dates to either 274 or 275 CE. Archaeologists assume the child likely died either around that time period, or soon after.

While researchers may never learn the precise circumstances of the burial, ancient Greek and Roman funerary beliefs suggest the coin served a specific purpose. It’s possible the child’s family intentionally placed the currency with the deceased as payment to Charon, the ferryman who brought souls to the afterlife. Knowing this, it may be that grieving relatives hoped to impart one final gift to their loved one.

#4415. PS5 "bullet ballet" game Saros gets a big update to make it both easier and harder

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bullet-dodging PlayStation shooter Saros has welcomed a sizeable Zenith update today, which has the twin aims of making the game both more accessible for those who haven't played it (or are struggling to get into it) and more replayable for those who have.

The headline additions are a quickfire boss-rush mode, a New Game Plus mode, an entire new layer of modifiers to make runs easier and harder, and - very appealingly - a slow-mo option to slow the game down.

The Slow Game Speed option can be varied depending on preference and can be activated either manually or automatically. You choose how much it slows the game down by; a video of it makes it look a lot like slow-mo in many other games, providing crucial thinking time in Saros you otherwise struggle to get.

Developer Housemarque said in a blog post it was "always wary" about toning down its games. "Although we realise our games can look intimidating at first, we didn’t want to compromise the combat system of Saros by reducing the projectiles that put the 'bullet' in 'bullet ballet'," it said. Hence the new slow game speed.

The new game modifiers allow you to tune the difficulty depending on what kind of challenge you're up for. If you want a bit of help, there are 10 new Protection modifiers you can apply. An example given was of a Corruption Melee Enhancement, which unleashes the game's deadly Corruption that builds up on you for huge melee damage.

Meanwhile, if you want to make the game harder, there are Trial modifiers. An example given was of Hostile Regeneration, which allows crowd-controlled enemies to quickly regain health unless you close to melee and finish them off with a strike.

The New Game Plus mode is called Eclipse Layers, and it activates once you've cleared the game. Each new Eclipse Layer represents a more intense version of the game with altered rules, apparently, and each has various prerequisites required in unlocking it; some require a certain amount of modifiers to be in effect, for example.

In step with this, Arjun now wears a cool sun-like crown during the Eclipse Layers - and his armour glows golden - that grows depending on the modifiers you have in play. The crown is also essential for seeing 12 new story visions Housemarque has added to the game for you to find.

Finally, Overlord Rush is the boss-rush mode that's designed to be played in 10-minute bursts, giving you a 'set' of the game's bosses to fight. A final set - Totality, it's called - has you face all eight Overlords in one continuous gauntlet.

Saros released in April on PS5 and was well reviewed, though it had a sluggish start in terms of sales, especially compared to predecessor of sorts, Returnal. Housemarque doesn't seem concerned, though, saying "this is just the start of the discussion", and that there's plenty to "tweak and add" and work will continue for "a while". This Zenith update is presumably a major part of that work.
