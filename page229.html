#250. In the biotech world, you can’t see everything

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

A reporter’s unsettling takeaway from You Can See Everything.

Earlier this week, I attended a screening of You Can See Everything, the buzzy new documentary about the disgraced former biotech CEO Elizabeth Holmes.

I went in not really knowing what to expect. I’d avoided spoilers (and won’t be giving anything away here, either!). I was wondering what new information this film could reveal, given how familiar many of us are with the Theranos story thanks to years of media coverage, the best-selling book Bad Blood, and the Hulu series The Dropout, starring Amanda Seyfried.

Like many others who have seen the film, I came away feeling unsettled. But not just about Holmes’s fraud and the somewhat disturbing interactions between the subjects. The film reignited a concern I’ve had about covering science for most of my career: Despite our best efforts, we never can actually see everything.

In case you’re rusty on the details, Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford University in 2003 to run Theranos, a diagnostics company she founded, when she was just 19. She went on to claim that her company had invented a way to run over 100 diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood.

Holmes raised hundreds of millions of dollars for Theranos, which was valued at $9 billion in 2014. Forbes named her the youngest self-made female billionaire—she was worth $4.5 billion when she was just 30 years old.

Things started to unravel in 2015, when an investigation by the Wall Street Journal raised concerns by former Theranos employees that the tests didn’t work. Some also said that the company’s device had not been tested properly, and that blood samples had been analyzed on machines from other companies instead. MIT Technology Review named it one of the biggest technology failures of 2015.

Forbes had revised Holmes’s worth to $0 by 2016. That year, a damning government report said that the company’s lab was struggling with basic procedures and gave patients results even when they knew they could be wrong.

In 2018, Theranos, Holmes, and former company president Ramesh Balwani were charged with “massive fraud.” In 2022, she was sentenced to a little over 11 years in prison for defrauding investors. She began serving her sentence in May 2023.

It was around a month before this that filmmakers Nathan Fielder, Lance Oppenheim, and their colleagues began documenting Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, at home with their two young children. I’m not giving anything away when I say it’s an extremely intimate portrayal—at least of how the two adults interact with Fielder, who is also a central character in the film.

As you might know if you’ve seen the trailer, there are plenty of super close-ups. I felt as if I spent most of the film staring straight into the faces of those people. And almost the whole way through, they all seemed to be trying to figure each other out. Holmes is known for her dishonesty, but she invited cameras into her home in an apparent bid to reframe the narrative and position herself as an honest person with an unusual mind. Will anyone believe her?

I couldn’t help feeling some empathy for Holmes, a mother we see preparing to leave her two kids for a lengthy prison sentence. And even though I have long known the facts, Holmes is clearly an effective manipulator. You can see her plant the seeds of doubt in Fielder’s mind. At times, I felt she was convincing me, sitting in my cinema seat. Maybe she really didn’t mean to lie?

But then I remembered how other journalists felt after being taken in by her claims. “I blew it,” Fortune’s Roger Parloff reflected in 2015, a year after profiling Holmes and her company. “I’m pretty sure she lied to me,” STAT’s Matthew Herper wrote recently, having interviewed Holmes in 2015. Even the former employee turned whistleblower Tyler Shultz, who knew the product didn’t work, said that he could “have a five-minute conversation with her and walk away feeling like my work was changing the world,” he said in 2022.

I didn’t cover Theranos in the 2010s. If I’d interviewed Holmes, would I have fallen for it all?

One of the early red flags in the Theranos case was the lack of published, peer-reviewed data. Much of the company’s work was classified as a “trade secret.” Scientists tend to publish their work in journals—a slow process that involves having the work evaluated by often anonymous experts in the field, who provide feedback, highlight questionable conclusions, and often ask for clarification. Peer review is meant to add rigor to the publication process. You should be able to trust published scientific papers.

Scientists aren’t perfect. Some will exaggerate their results or inflate their importance. Some manipulate data to serve a hypothesis. Sometimes these infractions are caught during peer review, but not always. There are plenty of examples of scientific findings that could never be replicated by other labs and ended up collapsing under the weight of real evidence. Many papers ultimately end up with corrections. Some are withdrawn—10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023.

At any rate, there’s been a shift to sharing research in other ways. Today, many scientists opt to publish their work at preprint servers like arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv before they submit them to established journals. Those outlets bypass peer review. So do company press releases, social media posts, and many conference presentations. And I get a lot of my stories from conversations and interviews.

I’ll admit I’ve looked at a scientific finding and thought: A scientist has just said they’ve done that. How can I be sure they really have?

I like to think that I hold myself to high journalistic standards here, as does everyone at MIT Technology Review. I would never publish a story about some new scientific development without doing my own form of peer review, and asking scientists—ideally people I know and trust—what they think of a new claim.

But the film is a stark reminder of how easy it can be to fall for an appealing narrative. While most scientists are, I hope, pretty honest, and Holmes is certainly an outlier, we should all continue to interrogate the claims being fed to us. If you’ve seen the film, let me know what you think!

#4732. At Scotland’s ancient Cladh Hallan, homes were full of human bones

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 8:56 AM EDT

In 2001, archaeologists were painstakingly excavating a dwelling at the Bronze-Age site of Cladh Hallan in  the Outer Hebrides islands off of Scotland’s northwestern coast. The house had been oval in shape, with a low door leading to an interior hearth. Other conveniences included sunken storage areas and stone-lined water storage tanks. Under the floors of these houses, though, and under the feet of the occupants, was a fascinating feature that evokes the opening scenes of a classic horror film.

As the archaeologists slowly dug through the floor of the structure, they came unexpectedly upon a human skeleton, legs tucked up in the fetal position. Another skeleton, similarly arranged, was found beneath a different roundhouse’s floor. These people had clearly been laid to rest with care, with stones laid out around them.

When archaeologists began to study the remains, though, they were staggered to learn that each body’s parts had not all come from the same person. These were composite mummies, assembled with the remains of at least six people who had died over the span of two hundred years. But this wasn’t a jumbled-together macabre collection or evidence of a Bronze-Age serial killer. These bodies had been treated carefully and buried in stone-lined graves. So what were the people of Cladh Hallan doing with the dug-up remains of their ancestors?

More than four thousand years ago, early farmers began to work the soil on the Outer Hebrides islands off of Scotland’s northwestern coast. At the time, the Roman Empire and even ancient Greece were specks on the chronological horizon. Along the Aegean coast, Minoans and Mycenaeans were building palaces and cities. Farther north, Germanic tribes were exporting amber and expertly forging bronze and gold artifacts.

At Cladh Hallan, early farmers grew barley, herded sheep, and foraged the abundant resources on the island’s beaches. Their descendants continued to thrive on the island for centuries, the population grew, and so did the need for land.

Nearly a thousand years into Cladh Hallan’s occupation, with living space on the small island becoming harder to find, people started building over the burial grounds of their ancestors. But the islanders were definitely aware of the graves beneath their feet—in fact, they went out of their way to acknowledge the people buried in them.

Residents of Cladh Hallan dug down into these older burials containing both cremated and unburnt remains, removing some parts and leaving the rest undisturbed. Then they assembled the exhumed remains into the requisite anatomical combinations that modern archaeologists have found centuries later. Once this was done, the composite bodies were once again laid to rest under the foundations of the roundhouses.

The two best-known adult skeletons from Cladh Hallan are the most unusual. Each was found under a roundhouse. One skeleton was identified as male, the other female, based on their pelvic bones. The bodies date to between 1300 and 1500 BCE…mostly.

The skeleton originally identified as a woman had a man’s skull where her own head used to be. Two of the teeth were missing from the skull, and those had been placed in each of the woman’s hands. By radiocarbon dating the bones, researchers found that the skull actually belonged to a man who had died at least a century earlier, perhaps a relative from an older generation.

The body originally identified as male was put together from three different people—all confirmed as male through DNA testing. The burial was made up of a man’s torso, a second man’s skull, and a third man’s jawbone. Each of these men had died at different times over the span of about 150 years. Again, ancestry and family identity seem to play a big part in these re-burials.

It isn’t terribly uncommon for cultures to include their deceased loved ones in celebratory, ritual, or even daily activities. Every few years, families in the Toraja region of Sulawesi, Indonesia exhume the remains of loved ones, clean them, dress them in fresh garments, and honor them with festivals.

South Korean “death beads” are made from the cremated remains of family members and displayed in the home.

Pre-colonial Inca religious tradition included regular ceremonial processions featuring the remains of people “buried” in natural alcoves in the Andes mountains and mummified by the dry, cold mountain air. The Cladh Hallan skeletons had once been mummified as well, though this was not mummification as it was practiced in ancient Egypt, nor like the mountainside Inca tradition.

First, the bodies were tightly wrapped in fabric, which kept the bones and soft tissue flexed into the fetal position. This is why the limbs remained so tightly curled towards the torso, and why the bodies were so well-preserved. Archaeologists were able to identify this type of preservation based on microscopic changes to the surfaces of the bones from the acidic environment of the peat bogs in which the bodies were submerged after wrapping.

Peat is a dense, wet accumulation of centuries of decaying plant matter, and is a feature of lots of landscapes, particularly in the northern latitudes. People have used dried peat as fuel for thousands of years, since it burns well and slowly, and is plentiful in areas where there aren’t a lot of trees for firewood. Peat from the Scottish isles, notably, is responsible for the smoky, earthy taste characteristic of Scotch whiskey.

Peat bogs are so packed with plant material that there is almost no oxygen present, which is great for preservation, as is the typically cool temperature beneath the peat’s surface. The lack of oxygen keeps microbes and other critters from consuming the body’s soft tissues, and the tannic acid from all the decaying plants in the bog turns skin dark and leathery.

By first burying the bodies of family members under layers of peat, the people at Cladh Hallan ensured that the remains would be as intact as possible when later generations exhumed them. This not only means that the Bronze Age inhabitants of the island were well aware of peat’s preservative properties, but that the entire process was a deliberate sequence of events.

Archaeologists interpret the pieced-together reburials as a part of a social or religious tradition that emphasized family lines and reverence for ancestors. Stones clearly marked the graves under the roundhouses, so the homes’ placement was definitely on purpose.

The initial bog burials were deliberately used as a source of family remains, and the parts that composed the buried skeletons seem to have been deliberately chosen, though we may never know why or how. It seems as though ancestor-recycling may have been a regular process at Cladh Hallan in the later part of the Bronze Age, as older generations gave way to new.

Archaeologists also surmise that the exhumed mummified remains were initially kept above ground, maybe in a special place in the house, and included as a part of daily life. These multi-ancestor figures might have been consulted when important decisions were made, or they may have been seen as household protectors.

Archaeology can never really uncover the full story of what people thought and believed thousands of years ago. Thoughts do not fossilize, and decay and weathering affect even the best-preserved archaeological sites.

We will probably never know exactly what it was like to live at Cladh Hallan when it was a busy, thriving settlement. We may never understand how the people that lived there conceived of their world, or how they thought about spirituality. Thanks to the burials at Cladh Hallan, though, we have a tantalizing glimpse of how people may have seen death in their universe as a continuous, pieced-together chain of lives lived over generations.

#5620. The New Energy Stalemate in Ukraine

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

TATIANA MITROVA is a Global Fellow at the Center on Global Energy Policy at Columbia University.

SERGEY VAKULENKO is a Senior Fellow at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin.

Why Russia’s Escalating Attacks Are Unlikely to End the War

In the days since U.S. President Donald Trump’s highly public falling out with Ukraine and growing rapprochement with Russia, Kyiv’s prospects going into the fifth winter of war look grim. The Trump administration has demanded that Ukraine suspend its attacks on Russian refineries, which have been one of Ukraine’s most effective means of putting pressure on Moscow. Meanwhile, Kyiv faces dangerous gaps in its air defenses against Russia’s jet-powered drones and stepped-up use of ballistic missiles. And Trump has entered into a deal with Russian President Vladimir Putin that temporarily waives U.S. sanctions on Russian diesel exports, offering Moscow a diplomatic win. Trump’s envoys have also proposed that Europe increase purchases of Russian gas as part of an effort to gain the Kremlin’s support for peace—further complicating the efforts of Ukraine and its remaining allies to squeeze Russia.

Yet missing from this bleak picture are the ways that Ukraine has already adapted to escalating attacks. Kyiv has developed innovative strategies to mitigate the impact of Russian energy strikes and keep its grid running, even amid intense bombardment. And despite the new U.S. pressure, Ukraine has continued to launch powerful drone strikes against Russia’s energy infrastructure, including successful mid-October attacks on an oil terminal in St. Petersburg and industrial gas processing plants as far away as Surgut and the Novy Urengoy, 1,800 miles from the Ukrainian border. Overall, since mid-2025, Ukrainian drone strikes have reduced Russian refinery throughput by roughly 30 percent; they have also eroded a significant share of Moscow’s excess refining capacity, resulting in a gasoline shortfall of about ten percent.

Still, this is only one part of a rapidly evolving energy war. Moscow has developed its own ways to limit the effects of Ukrainian strikes. And it has intensified its assault on Ukrainian infrastructure, including an October 19 barrage that damaged more than ten energy facilities. DiXi Group, a Ukrainian think tank, has estimated that Russia launched 12,800 missiles and drones at Ukrainian energy facilities in 2025 and early 2026, compared with just 2,800 during the first three years of the war combined. Although these figures count launches rather than successful strikes, they show how massive and relentless the Russian campaign has become.

Moreover, both sides are now able to target the substitutes and reserves that in the past could be used to cushion losses. Following Russian strikes on local power plants in early October, Ukrainians experienced widespread blackouts, even though Ukraine’s national grid had electricity to spare, because Russia continued hitting the lines and substations carrying the reserve power. During the summer, Ukraine hit so many of the refineries and pipelines around Moscow that Russia was forced to bring fuel by rail from as far as Lake Baikal, thousands of miles away.

If U.S. pressure and negotiations cannot stop the energy war, the central question for Ukraine is whether its remarkable capacity to adapt can keep pace with Russian attacks. At the same time, its fortunes this winter will depend on whether it can continue to hit Russian infrastructure in ways that challenge Moscow’s ability to repair and rebuild. Both Russia and Ukraine must now protect and repair their entire national networks around the clock; attackers can concentrate on the most vulnerable sites and strike again as soon as repairs are done. In such a war, defensive ingenuity matters as much as offensive capability. In the end, energy attacks alone are unlikely to force either side to accept the other’s terms, neither side may be able to gain a decisive advantage, even as both incur mounting costs. Recognizing the limits, as well as the power, of these strikes may be crucial not only to ending the present conflict but also to understanding the role that energy infrastructure will likely play in future wars.

For Ukraine, international support has been especially crucial in containing Russia’s energy barrage. As of November 2025, before the worst of last winter’s attacks, Ukraine had suffered $17.1 billion in direct damage to its electricity infrastructure, according to a report by the UN, the World Bank, and the European Commission. Yet Kyiv was able to keep the electricity flowing by drawing on the country’s remaining nuclear plants; repairing thermal generation facilities; increasing electricity, gas, and diesel imports; and compensating for lost power plants with distributed generation—smaller units running on solar, wind, gas, and diesel energy that are connected to the main grid.

Europe has also provided crucial replacement technology. In 2025, the European Commission delivered equipment from a retired Lithuanian thermal power plant to Ukraine in 149 shipments, enabling Ukraine to provide power to one million people. Such transfers have turned foreign utilities and suppliers into part of Ukraine’s rapid repair system. In September, Ukraine and the UN Development Program launched a new coordination center to speed up the installation of donated energy equipment.

Expanded use of batteries and distributed power also play a growing role. In September 2025, the Ukrainian energy company DTEK and the U.S.-based battery maker Fluence installed battery storage at six sites around Kyiv and Dnipro, with enough capacity to supply 600,000 households, roughly half of Kyiv, for two hours. Meanwhile, Kyiv has put in place 150 megawatts of distributed power generation—not enough to cover winter peak demand, but sufficient to provide more than half of the city’s essential services, including water supplies, hospitals, and heating systems. Nonetheless, Ukrainian reports suggest that 2,500 to 3,000 of Kyiv’s 12,000 apartment buildings could enter winter without an alternative heat supply.

That vulnerability is especially dangerous because restoring electricity does not automatically restore heat. Replacement boilers can help, but they must be installed locally, supplied with fuel, and connected to functioning heat networks, often while the same city remains under repeated attack. Last winter, UN monitors recorded at least 74 Russian attacks on heating infrastructure across Ukraine—attacks that left hundreds of thousands of people without heat or hot water for weeks on end in frigid temperatures. Keeping the national electric grid running did not make those homes habitable in freezing weather. Residents can try to solve the problem with electric space heaters, but their widespread use could overload the already fragile grid.

To prepare for even worse conditions this winter, Kyiv has been building up gas reserves. As of August, Ukraine’s gas stocks stood at 13.1 billion cubic meters, compared with 9.6 billion a year earlier. Still, stored gas must reach functioning heat and power plants, and electricity must pass through functioning transmission networks, both of which Russia has been continually attacking. Regardless of how resilient the national system is, Russia’s incessant targeting of particular Ukrainian cities has left them exposed, because local sources of substitute supply are often attacked before damaged facilities can be restored.

Meanwhile, Ukrainians are paying for the survival of their energy system in ways that capacity measures miss. Across the country, businesses have to run expensive generators, cut shifts, or close. Households spend more for fewer hours of reliable service. Declining industrial demand can make the electricity balance sheet look healthier, when the reality is that the economy is deteriorating under the pressure of relentless attacks. Those losses weaken the tax base and increase the need for foreign support. Russia does not have to switch off Ukraine’s power to impose an extraordinary burden.

Russia has also felt the growing effects of the energy war. In theory, Moscow has a much larger cushion: when the war began, its refineries produced more fuel than its domestic market consumed, and many had excess capacity to distill crude than to convert the resulting streams into finished motor fuel. Losing one distillation unit did not necessarily mean losing an equivalent share of gasoline or diesel supply. But Ukraine’s attacks this year have tested the limits of that flexibility.

In June, Ukrainian drones hit one of two processing units at Moscow’s Kapotnya refinery and hit the other six days later. The refinery was shut down after the June attacks and although it subsequently resumed operations, another strike again halted processing in September. Elsewhere, Ukrainian attacks have damaged complex units that are designed to raise fuel yields and improve quality; they can take six to eight months to fix, so they might remain unavailable through the coming winter heating season. Spare primary distillation capacity cannot replace damaged secondary equipment needed to turn intermediate products into finished gasoline and diesel.

To make up for these losses, the Russian government has loosened fuel standards, allowing naphtha blended with octane boosters to enter the gasoline supply, and extended state subsidies to imported gasoline. Russian companies can also send crude to its ally Belarus for processing or import finished products. These measures have helped fill consumers’ gas tanks without restoring the equipment that previously supplied them. But they come at a cost, which consumers, companies, and the state will all ultimately have to absorb.

Not all Ukrainian drone strikes have had such long-term impact. In March 2026, for example, Ukraine attacked the Ust-Luga oil terminal, which is on the Baltic Sea near St. Petersburg, continually for almost two weeks, halting oil shipments from that terminal. But shipments from Ust-Luga resumed a few weeks later, and by late April Russia’s total seaborne crude exports averaged 3.8 million barrels a day—up seven percent from March and 11 percent from a year earlier. This increase helped offset falling exports of refined products, as damage to refineries left more crude available for export.

For Moscow, the most pressing question is how to manage fuel shortages. Through higher prices and rationing, it can ensure that the military remains well supplied even if civilians have to cut back. It can also use subsidies and price controls to provide some relief to consumers. None of these options are costless, but as long as the government is willing to impose lower living standards and absorb the resulting political costs, it retains considerable room to maneuver. Ukrainian strikes can force Russia to spread its air defense thinner, absorb money and labor, and reduce its income from refining. They can somewhat reduce the oil and gas revenue that finances Russia’s war, but none of these costs are likely to make the war prohibitively expensive for the Kremlin.

Paradoxically, the growing success of Ukraine’s strikes against Russian refineries has brought new pressure on Kyiv itself. To buttress its increasingly constrained domestic market, Russia has reduced the amount of diesel it is selling abroad. Normally, higher prices should encourage refiners elsewhere to take up the slack, but the U.S. war against Iran has made that adjustment harder. Combined Russian and Middle Eastern net diesel and gasoil exports in September were 1.3 million barrels a day below their level a year earlier, according to the International Energy Agency. That global supply crunch has also affected the United States, which has now blamed Ukraine for stubbornly high U.S. diesel prices.

Ordinarily, the United States enjoys a substantial diesel surplus and is the world’s largest diesel exporter. Yet in the absence of export controls, quotas, or export tariffs, the U.S. market is exposed to galloping international prices, even when there is no U.S. fuel shortage. It is also far easier for the Trump administration to press Ukraine to stop its attacks on Russian refineries—and to enter a diesel agreement with Russia—than to restore lost Middle Eastern diesel exports.

In reality, Washington’s moves are unlikely to add much to global supply in the near term: redirecting existing exports does not create additional fuel. A larger, lasting increase would require Russian refineries to recover and stay in operation. This is where Washington has an incentive to restrain Ukraine’s attacks—even to the point of withholding, or threatening to withhold, Kyiv’s access to Starlink satellite service or U.S. intelligence, or to block European purchases of U.S. weapons for Ukraine. If Ukraine complies with U.S. demands, one risk is that Moscow could obtain protection for its refineries while continuing its assault on Ukrainian cities.

The United States is not the only external player in this widening energy war. For Europe, Ukraine’s collapse would create a security threat far greater than the cost of replacing transformers or financing fuel imports. Thus, in mid-October, the EU’s ambassador to Ukraine described a $1.1 billion energy support package Europe is preparing for the coming winter, which will enhance Ukraine’s ability to recover. But if some European countries, despite the European Commission’s opposition, accede to the Trump administration’s proposal to expand European purchases of Russian gas in the hope of lowering energy costs for households and industry—that could bolster Moscow’s earnings and its ability to finance the war. Europe’s energy choices matter on both sides of the contest.

China and India may also affect the future direction of the energy war. Thus far, both countries’ purchases of Russian crude have helped sustain Russia’s war machine. Moreover, by refining discounted barrels of Russian crude, both countries can benefit their own industries, and by exporting more refined fuel, they can ease the global crunch that is now driving U.S. pressure on Ukraine. But the ability of these countries to raise their exports depends on available crude and government approval—particularly in China. Over time, Russia’s leverage from the current diesel crunch will shrink as other suppliers recover, trade adjusts, and consumption falls. But that shift is unlikely to happen in time for this winter: the International Energy Agency expects diesel markets to remain tight well into 2027.

For both Russia and Ukraine, the aim of the energy war is clear: to disrupt or break the adversary’s economy to the point of undermining support for the war or making it unsustainable. But there are few indications that either side can achieve such an outcome. U.S. pressure could nevertheless produce a cease-fire or a broader settlement, even on terms deeply unfavorable to Ukraine. A mutual halt to energy strikes would give both sides relief and time to recover.

For now, however, an enormous gap remains between the conditions that Moscow and Kyiv deem minimally acceptable for even a temporary cease-fire. High levels of mutual distrust have increased that gap further. Moreover, a deal to protect only refineries, as the Trump administration has called for, would leave Ukraine’s cities and other infrastructure still exposed to Russian attacks. And Moscow has less incentive to accept any such deal if it can secure Ukrainian restraint through pressure by Washington, without making concessions of its own.

If diplomacy fails, the most likely scenario is another winter of sustained strikes on energy and other infrastructure by both sides. For Russia, the loss of refining capacity has been manageable thus far, but that does not preclude larger problems ahead. With each additional loss of capacity, Russia’s protection of its military energy supply becomes more expensive. Certain regions that have already been squeezed, such as occupied Crimea, could become especially vulnerable. If Moscow is unable to make repairs fast enough or otherwise compensate for further losses of refining capacity, regional fuel shortages could develop into wider disruptions to civilian transport and industry.

For Ukraine, the central risk is a cascading series of Russian attacks on the same city. During prolonged cold, repeated attacks—whether on Kyiv or Kharkiv or Odessa—could exhaust backup supplies faster than crews can restore service. Prolonged pump stoppages could lead to a disastrous local scenario of ruptured water supply, district heating, and sewage pipes, even if electricity remains available elsewhere in the country. A milder winter and timely equipment deliveries could keep Ukraine ahead of such a crisis, but delays in air defense deliveries or the loss of a critical heating facility could overwhelm a city whose electricity balance looked adequate. An indication of danger will be local power outages that continue to grow despite repairs, showing that recovery is unable to keep pace with attacks.

As it prepares for what will likely be its harshest winter yet, Kyiv faces a dilemma: if it reduces or suspends its attacks on Russian refineries to preserve U.S. military support, it will give Moscow a repair window while the Ukrainian grid remains exposed. If Kyiv refuses, it could continue its strikes but face intelligence or Starlink cutoffs that could make its infrastructure harder to defend. An additional influx of European funds and technology could soften the consequences—and perhaps provide some alternatives to U.S. communication networks—but will not fully replace U.S. intelligence and air-defense capabilities. That dilemma will not go away as long as Ukraine’s own infrastructure is exposed to attack.

As it approaches its fifth anniversary, the conflict in Ukraine has transformed energy warfare in ways that have few precedents. The advent of cheap long-range weapons has allowed each side almost unlimited access to the energy infrastructure on which the other depends. Yet at the same time, innovations in distributed power generation, batteries, and mobile equipment have given defenders novel ways to preserve essential services and continually recover, even in the face of large-scale attacks. Russia’s deep industrial reserves and Ukraine’s impressive adaptation have shown that complex energy systems can withstand enormous damage. That endurance has been far greater than a tally of destroyed assets on either side would suggest.

But this same resilience can make wars longer and more devastating. As Ukraine and Russia have shown, each side can gain confidence from its ability to sustain repeated attacks while also persuading itself that an even more intense campaign against its adversary might finally succeed. In this dynamic, each adjustment and workaround by one side invites a fresh attack by the other, and each new strike accelerates the search for new ways to endure. This adaptation spiral can continue even as economic and human losses mount. Only a true cease-fire can break the cycle. And that will depend on political choices that energy attacks alone cannot determine.

#675. Scientists discover a hidden messaging system deep inside the brain

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Deep within the brainstem is a tiny group of nerve cells that plays an outsized role in how the brain functions. This region, called the locus coeruleus (LC), gets its name from the Latin words for "blue place." Although it contains only a small fraction of the brain's neurons, it produces norepinephrine (NE), a chemical messenger that helps regulate attention, learning, heart rate, and the body's reactions to stress and excitement.

Scientists have long assumed that the LC operates like a loudspeaker, sending essentially the same norepinephrine signal across the brain. However, a new study published in Nature challenges that understanding. Using brain imaging, recordings of neural activity, genetic techniques, and behavioral experiments in mice, researchers at the Allen Institute discovered that the LC communicates through several distinct pathways. Rather than broadcasting one general message, it delivers signals to specific destinations, with different groups of neurons responding to different experiences.

A Hidden Brain Network That Sends Precise Messages

By tracing the connections of LC neurons, researchers found that different groups of cells communicate with specific regions of the nervous system. These pathways determine where signals trigger the release of norepinephrine, creating a much more organized communication network than previously recognized.

Neurons in the upper (dorsal) portion of the LC primarily connect to the cerebral cortex and forebrain. Meanwhile, cells in the lower (ventral) portion send their signals mainly to the brainstem and spinal cord. This arrangement allows different parts of the LC to influence separate brain functions.

"The findings suggest the brain's norepinephrine system is far more like a targeted postal network than a foghorn," said Karel Svoboda, director of Neural Dynamics at the Allen Institute and study co-author.

Why the Discovery Could Matter for Brain Disorders

Understanding how this signaling network is organized could eventually change how certain neurological and mental health conditions are treated.

Norepinephrine plays a central role in regulating attention and mood, making it an important target for medications used to treat depression, anxiety, and ADHD. Neurons in the LC are also among the earliest brain cells to deteriorate in Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that the new findings could have implications for understanding and treating these conditions.

Current treatments that influence norepinephrine can affect multiple brain circuits at once, sometimes producing beneficial effects in one region while causing unwanted effects elsewhere. A clearer map of the LC's specialized pathways could help scientists develop therapies that act more selectively on the circuits involved in particular symptoms.

The research received support from the National Institutes of Health's Brain Research Through Advancing Innovative Neurotechnologies® Initiative, also known as The BRAIN Initiative®.

Scientists Identify Brain Cells Involved in Learning

The researchers also uncovered an important distinction between the functions of different LC neurons.

Cells in the dorsal LC that connect to the cortex were particularly active during learning. When mice performed decision-making tasks and adjusted their choices after an unfavorable result, these neurons increased their activity.

Neurons in the ventral LC behaved differently. Their activity rose shortly before mice ignored signals indicating that a reward might be available. This suggests that these cells help regulate whether animals respond to opportunities in their surroundings.

"What emerged was a clear map: neurons in the dorsal LC that send signals upward to the cortex are involved in learning, whereas neurons in the ventral region projecting downward to the brainstem and spinal cord govern whether animals engage with their environment at all," said Svoboda. "We also showed that these anatomical differences are mirrored by distinct gene expression patterns."

These findings indicate that the differences between the two groups extend beyond their locations and connections. They also have distinct patterns of gene activity that reflect their specialized roles.

Researchers Discover a Record-Breaking Neuron

The study involved an extensive examination of the mouse brain. Scientists used whole-brain imaging to study nearly 35,000 neurons, analyzed the genetic profiles of almost 400,000 cells, and reconstructed the complete structures of several exceptionally complex nerve cells.

During this work, they encountered another remarkable finding: LC neurons possess extraordinarily long axons. These projections carry signals from nerve cells to other cells, allowing information to travel across the nervous system.

The researchers found that LC axons measured approximately 35 centimeters long on average. One particularly extraordinary axon had a total reconstructed length of 70.32 centimeters, making it part of the longest single neuron ever measured in a mouse.

"This neuron, like many others that we studied, supplies NE to a very large volume of the cerebral cortex. For the brain, this is highly unusual. Most neurons are more specific in their targets," said Jeremiah Cohen, scientist at the Allen Institute and study co-author. "But this neuron doesn't release NE everywhere. It ignores the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord."

Despite its remarkable length and extensive reach, the neuron does not distribute norepinephrine throughout the entire brain. Instead, its connections remain selective, reinforcing the discovery that the LC operates through carefully organized signaling pathways.

A New Understanding of How the Brain Learns

The researchers also identified intriguing similarities between the norepinephrine system and the brain's dopamine signaling system.

Dopamine is another major chemical messenger that sends signals to the basal ganglia, a brain region involved in forming habits. Scientists suspect that dopamine and norepinephrine work together to support learning, potentially allowing the brain to acquire several complex and abstract concepts at the same time.

By revealing the intricate connections, specialized functions, and unusual physical structure of neurons in the locus coeruleus, the research provides a more detailed picture of how the animal brain communicates and learns.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice, they could eventually help scientists better understand human brain function and design treatments that target specific neural circuits rather than affecting entire signaling systems.

#306. India slashes cost of cancer drugs after court rules mark-ups are ‘extortion’

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Case filed in 2022 by patient unable to afford medicine leads to cap on drug prices for millions who face ruin if diagnosed

The Indian government has moved to reduce the price of cancer drugs after a retired bank clerk took her case to the supreme court.

From next month, 137 cancer drugs in India will be cheaper as the government tries to tackle the tragedy of poorer patients either forgoing treatment, selling land or falling into debt to meet the high cost of some medicines, which are making large profits for hospitals and pharmacists.

Sarojam Radhakrishnan, a former bank employee in Kerala, was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2021. Doctors prescribed life-saving targeted treatments but the monthly cost, of more than 63,000 rupees (£500), was far beyond her family’s means.

Realising that thousands of families were being ruined by the cost of such drugs, Radhakrishnan filed a petition to the Kerala high court in 2022 demanding government intervention to lower the costs.

When the case reached the supreme court, the judges looked at hospitals’ and retailers’ mark-ups on cancer drugs and called the situation “carnage” and “extortion”.

During a hearing on 29 September, justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta noted how a life-saving cancer drug supplied to hospitals at 2,700 rupees carried a vastly marked-up price of 27,000 rupees.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

As a former member of the council of the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre, Kalra knows the importance of early diagnosis, but he said very few people attended. “When I ask why, they say they’re too scared – if something serious like cancer is detected, they haven’t got the money to pay for the treatment so they prefer to remain in the dark,” he said.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

“Some families start the treatment, hoping against hope to manage the cost. They start but they don’t come back for chemotherapy or radiation. They can’t sustain the cost,” he said. “The decision is a welcome step if the outcome is that more patients can access the drugs they need.”

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

As the supreme court delves deeper into the subject, what was clear, said Jyotsna Singh, co-convener of the Working Group on Access to Medicines and Treatments, a civil society network, was that a “total overhaul” of the healthcare system was needed.

“Right from the cost of manufacturing a drug to the point of sale, we need to make the system transparent and regulate the entire supply chain. Only then will these drugs become available to those who need them,” she said.

Singh said companies and hospitals were likely to try to find legal loopholes to wriggle out of the 30% cap, suggesting that hospitals may offset the lower price charged for a drug by raising charges on other items.

The supreme court judges have also asked the government’s lawyers why the cap should apply only to cancer drugs and not the entire range of non-patented drugs. The court has given the government until 27 October to respond.

Unfortunately, the court’s ruling came too late for Radhakrishnan, who died in September 2022 while her case was still pending in Kerala.

#579. Scientists propose a way to detect dangerous “mirror life” before it exists

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Scientists have proposed what they believe is the first way to detect "mirror life," a hypothetical form of life built from molecules that are reversed versions of those found in living organisms on Earth.

Researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University have developed a proposed detection technique that uses molecules capable of emitting light with distinct left and right-handed properties. By analyzing these light signals, scientists may eventually be able to distinguish conventional organisms from their mirror-image counterparts.

Although mirror life does not currently exist, some experts consider its potential creation a serious biological safety concern. These artificial organisms could behave in unpredictable ways when interacting with Earth's ecosystems and might evade certain natural defenses that protect people, animals, and plants.

The likelihood of mirror life emerging is considered low, but its possible consequences could be severe enough to justify developing detection methods in advance.

Why Mirror Life Could Pose a Biological Threat

Concerns about mirror life have prompted scientists to recommend restrictions on research intended to produce organisms capable of reproducing using mirror-image biological molecules.

The potential dangers have also attracted attention from governments and international organizations, including the UK Government Office for Science and the United Nations Secretary General's Scientific Advisory Board.

The underlying science involves a property of molecules known as chirality, often described as molecular "handedness." Just as a person's left and right hands are mirror images that cannot be perfectly aligned, some molecules can exist in two forms that have identical chemical components but different three-dimensional arrangements.

These subtle structural differences can have major biological consequences. Two mirror-image versions of a molecule contain the same atoms connected in the same order, yet the human body may respond to them very differently. For example, one form of a medication could successfully treat a disease, while its mirror-image counterpart might be ineffective or even harmful.

Scientists are concerned that organisms assembled from reversed biological building blocks could interact with existing life in unexpected ways.

Importantly, the researchers emphasize that mirror life remains entirely hypothetical. Their proposed detection method is intended as a precaution, not a response to an existing biological threat.

The proposed approach relies on a phenomenon called circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), which can reveal the molecular handedness, or chirality, of certain substances and biological structures.

Light can exhibit different polarization patterns, including forms that rotate in opposite directions. By studying these patterns, researchers can obtain information about the three-dimensional arrangement of molecules.

The underlying concept has a surprisingly familiar application: the polarized light technology used in modern 3D movie glasses to create the illusion of depth.

The research, published in Royal Society Open Science under the title 'Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) as a Contingency Assay for "Mirror Life" Bio-Hazards', was led by Dr. Lewis MacKenzie of the University of Strathclyde's Department of Pure and Applied Chemistry, working with Professor Robert Pal of Durham University.

Dr. MacKenzie said: "We don't expect mirror life to appear tomorrow, but if it did arise, the consequences could be severe, and it makes sense to develop detection methods before they are needed. Professor Pal and I had been working on CPL technologies before recent concerns became prominent, and guarding against mirror life was a clear fit for our research."

A Molecular Fingerprint for Detecting Mirror Life

To identify a possible mirror organism, the researchers suggest using specialized fluorescent probes that attach to particular structures inside cells.

Once bound to their targets, these probes would generate characteristic CPL signals. Scientists could analyze the resulting patterns to determine whether a biological sample contains the molecular handedness associated with ordinary terrestrial life or its reversed equivalent.

In effect, the light signals could serve as a molecular fingerprint, revealing differences that might otherwise be difficult to identify.

However, relying on a single fluorescent probe could produce misleading results. To improve reliability and reduce the risk of false readings, the team proposes combining several probes designed to recognize different cellular structures.

By examining multiple signals together, researchers hope to develop a more dependable way to distinguish conventional organisms from hypothetical mirror life.

Testing the Technology Without Creating Mirror Organisms

One important advantage of the proposed technique is that scientists could begin investigating and refining it using ordinary biological samples. There would be no need to manufacture mirror organisms simply to explore whether the underlying detection principles work.

The researchers also believe the method could eventually be incorporated into several types of scientific equipment, potentially allowing mirror-life screening in different environments.

Professor Pal has already demonstrated laboratory-scale CPL detection technologies involving microscopes, cameras, and biological cell plate readers, which are instruments commonly used to examine large numbers of cell samples.

However, the complete detection method specifically designed to identify mirror life has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Further research will be necessary to determine whether the proposed approach can reliably distinguish between different forms of molecular handedness in biological samples.

Professor Pal added: "CPL detection relies on the same fundamental concept that allows modern 3D cinema glasses to create a stereoscopic image.

"This CPL detection approach can be adapted to many types of systems for analysis of cells, including biological plate readers, microscopes, and cameras. This means our mirror life assay approach could be generalized to many environments depending on end user needs."

Unlike issued tokens that can have a counterparty and can be frozen, etc., XRP is not a claim on anyone.

Vet also responded to the popular question of why the price of XRP doesn't necessarily correlate with the increased utility of the XRP Ledger.

The expert explained that it is not necessary for the price of XRP to rise instantly whenever there is new tokenization or institutional integration.

Instead, the expert believes that the focus should be on increasing the adoption of the network first.

Then, as a secondary effect, one can expect that the price may potentially increase.

"A valuable network is a good place to house money," he said.

"It's order of operations. Let the price of the native asset be a knock-on effect of large-scale usage and acceptance of the XRP Ledger."
