Hinge is for daters in their 20s and 30s who are over swipe culture and want a real, meaningful relationship. It's the app I recommend to friends when they're ready to get serious and stop wasting time.

I picked Hinge as the best dating app overall because its "Designed to be deleted" slogan isn't just marketing. Instead of just swiping on photos, you can like a specific photo or prompt answer (like "My simple pleasures") and add a comment. You don't have to write anything, but making the first move about something on their profile gives you a better starting point than just "hey."

“Comment on something in the person’s profile, like an activity they’re doing in their photos or an interest listed in their bio,” Weiss says. “This will make your message stand out amid the generic ones.”

Hinge has also been rolling out useful, non-gimmicky features to help with the parts of dating people tend to overthink. The AI "Prompt Feedback" tool gives you advice on your prompt responses without writing them for you. Prompt Feedback is optional and private, so you can ignore the advice if your answer already sounds like you.

"Match Note” lets you share something privately with new matches before chatting, while “Convo Starters” gives you ideas for an opener based on their profile. (Again, you still have to write the message yourself.)

If the problem is figuring out what to share in the first place, Hinge also has a collection of 10 profile prompts developed with psychotherapist Esther Perel. Called "Your World," it includes prompts like "I'm in my element when..." and "I could stay up all night talking about..."

To help you get offline faster, Hinge offers "Date Ideas," a feature that lets you add up to three date ideas to your profile. Potential matches can pick one and select a window of availability, like "this weekend," from a drop-down menu. It doesn't schedule the date for you, but it helps you show you're both interested in meeting — and what you'd want to do.

Hinge uses a mix of machine learning and human moderators to enforce its "ACE" (Authenticity, Courage, and Empathy) values. "Are You Sure?" nudges you before you send potentially harmful language, and "Hidden Words" lets you filter unwanted words and emoji in comments attached to incoming likes.

In October, Hinge also introduced an educational walkthrough for some minor harassment violations in chats. Human moderators assess the context and severity; users who receive a warning have to complete the walkthrough before continuing to use the app. Severe or repeated violations can still get someone banned. The idea is to teach better messaging etiquette rather than immediately remove everyone who crosses a line — not to give everyone a free pass for harassment.

Hinge's verification requirements vary by region; its age-check guidance explains what applies where you live. Regardless of the verification method, a badge isn't a background check.

If you’re at a point in your life where you're over the games and just want to find a real, serious partner, I'd send you to Match. It's my go-to for daters in their 30s, 40s, and 50s who are established, know what they want, and are genuinely ready to find someone.

While the younger crowd is busy swiping on Tinder and Hinge, Match has carved out its niche as an option for a more mature demographic. The 2026 SSRS Opinion Panel Omnibus found that 33 percent of U.S. adults aged 50 and older who have ever used a dating site or app have tried Match.

In my experience, this means the crowd on Match is generally more invested (both emotionally and financially) in finding a real connection. That doesn't mean everyone is looking for marriage, or that having a subscription makes someone a better prospect. But if you're tired of trying to figure out whether someone wants a relationship at all, Match is one of the places I'd start.

The profiles give you room to explain your interests, lifestyle, and what you're looking for in a partner. That extra context is useful when you're trying to work out whether someone fits into your everyday life — not just whether you'd swipe right on their photo.

Match offers anonymous reporting and says its team reviews every issue members report. You can report a concern from someone's profile or a message, including harassment, requests for money, or inappropriate behavior during or after an in-person meeting. Its reporting process is anonymous, and they've partnered with RAINN (the nation's largest anti-sexual violence organization) to improve their reporting, safety, and messaging features.

Match also warns against sending money to someone you've only met online — even if you've spent weeks talking and their emergency sounds convincing. Its safety guidance flags pressure to move off the app without meeting or video calling as another reason to be cautious.

OkCupid (also part of the Match Group) is my top pick for the open-minded dater who wants a feature-rich experience without having to shell out cash. It's one of the most inclusive apps out there, with over 60 ways to identify across its gender and orientation options, so it's great for the LGBTQ community. But the experience doesn't always live up to the promise.

In this Reddit discussion, some users describe the app as a "disaster," complaining about suspected bots and "passport" profiles from thousands of miles away. Of course, these complaints aren't definitive proof of what you'll encounter on the app, but they're worth reading through.

I picked OkCupid as the best app for daters with dealbreakers because it’s the gold standard for comparing what matters to you before starting a conversation. While Hinge is my favorite free app overall, OkCupid’s free version is pretty impressive.

Its matching system is built on an in-depth questionnaire. You answer questions on everything from your political views to your messy habits, and the app gives you a compatibility score for other users. It's a way to spot potential dealbreakers before you even say "hello."

And those dealbreakers aren't just about politics or whether someone wants kids. In February 2026, OkCupid launched "Love & Money," a series exploring how finances shape modern romance. Among the Gen Z and millennial daters it surveyed, 63 percent considered being open about money a green flag, while 68 percent said financial responsibility was sexier than generosity. Apparently, sticking to a budget has its appeal.

OkCupid's safety policy is built on one simple rule: "don't be a jerk." The app uses a combination of "Safe Message Filters" (automated tools) and trained human agents to scan interactions and enforce its guidelines. Its rules prohibit nudity, pornography, and sexually explicit profiles, including kink/fetish profiles or descriptions of specific sex acts. If you're looking for a kink-positive space, I’d recommend Pure or Feeld, which has a "Reflections" quiz to help users explore their interests and desires.

It's a not-so-fun reminder of the data risks involved with dating apps.

In September 2026, Tinder took the friend-group approach further with Group Hangouts, which launched in the U.S. and select markets. One person creates a group, picks an activity, and invites at least two friends. Once you have three members, you can browse other groups, match, and chat about meeting up. Everyone needs their own Tinder profile, so this isn't one person playing matchmaker from a shared account. If a one-on-one first date sounds exhausting, bringing your friends is at least a different way to start.

The "Are You Sure?" and "Does This Bother You?" tools are designed to flag potentially harmful messages. Tinder announced AI upgrades in March 2026, including better detection before sending and automatic blurring of potentially disrespectful messages.

It's like a weird, morphed lovechild of Tinder and Hinge: You can fill out the basics and get going, or spend more time on prompts, interests, and what you’re looking for. Dating intentions include “Committed relationship,” “Something casual,” and “Physical connection,” so it isn’t exclusively a relationship app or a hookup app.

To be sure, China’s EV industry has benefited immensely from Beijing’s subsidies, easy credit, and tax relief. That explanation, however, is far too narrow and risks severely underestimating the scale of the challenge ahead.

The more consequential story—that will likely make Chinese EVs even more competitive in the near future—is Beijing’s evolving approach to overseas industrial policy. At the core of this approach is an increasingly coordinated model of public- and private-sector activity.

State-owned enterprises (SOE) lay the groundwork for favorable investment environments abroad by undertaking massive infrastructure projects and securing access to strategic resources. Boosted by Beijing’s diplomatic ties and international clout, private companies leverage these state-backed platforms as a springboard for trade by bringing in investment, production capacity, and industry-specific expertise.

The advantages of this approach for Beijing—and the challenge that it poses to foreign companies—are already on full display in Morocco, where years of existing strategic investment, infrastructure development, and geopolitical maneuvering are pushing China closer to vertically integrating EV supply chains, bypassing tariffs, and further reshaping Europe’s auto industry.

It is only in recent years that Morocco has emerged as a key node in China’s EV empire.

For decades, Beijing maintained much closer ties with Morocco’s neighbor and rival, Algeria, but that began to change in the early 2010s as the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) took off. In 2016, Morocco joined the growing ranks of countries signing “strategic partnerships” with China, making mutual commitments to expand economic cooperation and recognize each other’s core interests.

The next year, Morocco officially entered the BRI, and China has since poured millions of dollars into Morocco’s energy and transportation sectors. Almost a decade later, that economic relationship has expanded dramatically: In March, China’s committed investment in the country exceeded $10 billion, concentrated mostly in the EV and energy sectors.

China’s EV manufacturing capacity in Morocco builds on years of large-scale, state-backed investment in transportation infrastructure and natural resources. Over the past decade, Chinese SOEs have collaborated on major infrastructure and development projects in Morocco, including bridges, highways, and smart cities. Some of these are directly relevant to the EV industry, such as the chemical processing unit in Khouribga, home to one of the world’s biggest reserves of phosphate, which is critical for EV battery production.

This SOE-led scramble for resources and construction-heavy projects paved the way for private Chinese companies in the EV sector to establish footholds in Morocco and ship to overseas markets. Tanger Tech City, China’s SOE-led flagship collaboration with Morocco, is located less than 30 miles from Tanger Med, the largest container port in Africa. Taking advantage of no-fee business registration and tax-free dividends, private Chinese companies—including the global battery giant BTR New Material Group as well as tire manufacturers Sentury Tire and Guizhou Tyre—have established or announced plans to establish production bases in the industrial zone.

Elsewhere in the country, major private investments include battery manufacturer Gotion’s $1.3 billion factory and a $2 billion joint-venture battery materials factory between China’s CNGR Advanced Material and the Moroccan private equity firm Al Mada.

This isn’t to deny the role of subsidies and preferential financing, which Beijing sees as key tools for supporting companies that are expanding internationally. A 2024 directive, for instance, called on Chinese financial institutions to improve credit approval and lending procedures and to enhance cross-border settlement to “better support companies expanding into international markets.” Closer collaboration among Chinese banks, semi-official trade organizations, and Moroccan banks has also helped to streamline financing and channel investment into the African market.

But these policies are useful to Chinese firms because they are operating in an industrial landscape that SOEs have already shaped, with relatively easy access to necessary resources and infrastructure.

China’s success with this approach has only been possible because it also advances Rabat’s desire to expand domestic manufacturing capability and position Morocco as Africa’s next industrial powerhouse. Chinese investment is expected to generate sorely needed jobs; Morocco’s unemployment rate has hardly dipped below 10 percent for the past decade, and youth unemployment in cities stands at a staggering 47 percent. Officials claim that the Gotion factory alone will create 17,000 local jobs, including 2,300 high-skilled positions.

Beijing has coupled this economic engagement with a subtle diplomatic shift in favor of Rabat’s claims over Western Sahara. For decades, China has avoided explicitly backing either Morocco or Western Sahara, though its rhetoric often leaned toward the latter—and thus to the latter’s main sponsor, Algiers. With one exception in 2018, it consistently voted in favor of United Nations Security Council resolutions extending the peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara and affirming support for the Sahrawi people’s right to self-determination.

However, in recent years, China has done an about-face. In 2024, its Ministry of Foreign Affairs implicitly acknowledged the importance of the Western Sahara question for Rabat, stating, “China supports Morocco in maintaining national security and stability and is ready to work with Morocco to continue firmly supporting each other on issues concerning their respective core interests.” Then, last fall, Beijing abstained on the most recent U.N. resolution on the matter, which for the first time endorsed Rabat’s proposal to formally establish Western Sahara as an autonomous region under Moroccan sovereignty.

The full payoff of China’s Moroccan gambit will materialize if and when localized EV manufacturing matures, which could take years. That is still a tall order, as evidenced by the recent shelving of a BYD assembly plant project that had been stalled since 2017 due to budget issues and limited local charging infrastructure, according to a company executive.

Though Chinese automakers are not yet building assembly plants in Morocco, the country matters for everything “upstream” of assembly. It is becoming an integrated ecosystem for the production of batteries, tires, and other components, which reduces Chinese EV companies’ reliance on direct shipments from China and lowers costs for Chinese assembly plants in Europe and Africa.

Morocco also offers Chinese firms convenient access to the European market through the Tanger Med port and—more importantly—a rules-based pathway through Brussels’s tightening trade defenses. Currently, the EU imposes duties of up to 35 percent on EVs manufactured in China. However, vehicles that qualify as Moroccan in origin can enter the European market duty-free. That includes vehicles assembled in Morocco, so long as foreign parts make up less than 45 percent of the finished vehicle’s ex-works price.

In short, if Chinese EV companies succeed in establishing vehicle assembly factories and fully integrating supply chains in Morocco, they will be able to sell vehicles to Europe under a “Made in Morocco” label, avoiding both the 10 percent EU standard automobile tariff and the additional punitive tariff on Chinese-made EVs—reducing the total tariff effect from as much as 45 percent to zero. This would pose an unprecedented threat to what remains one of the EU’s most important industries.

China is rolling out this coordinated approach to overseas industrial policy elsewhere to varying degrees.

In Thailand, for example, China is a key investor in the Eastern Economic Corridor, a flagship regional development initiative anchored by a high-speed railway that will connect several Thai cities and seaports to China. The corridor has, in turn, provided an attractive environment for private Chinese investment. China is now the corridor’s largest source of foreign direct investment, primarily in the EV sector and related industries. Like Tanger Tech City, the corridor offers private Chinese companies major tax incentives and more convenient access to the Association of Southeast Asian Nations’ market.

The United Arab Emirates offers another example. In 2017, Chinese SOE Jiangsu Provincial Overseas Cooperation and Investment Company Limited (JOCIC) partnered with Abu Dhabi Ports to develop an industrial and special economic zone inside the Khalifa Port’s free trade zone. The park’s location provides Chinese companies with a convenient base for expansion into the Persian Gulf and wider Middle Eastern markets, along with exemptions from value-added tax and other financial incentives. As of 2025, more than 40 Chinese companies had collectively committed more than $700 million to the park, with a focus on EVs and renewable energy.

Beijing has long seen its SOEs as tools for advancing its broader economic interests. As China in recent years has emphasized “small and beautiful” projects, the SOE-led megaprojects are often viewed as commercially disappointing holdovers from an earlier and more profligate era of BRI. Nevertheless, under this coordinated strategy, Beijing has been able to capitalize on the advantages secured by past infrastructure projects and deepen its presence in the overseas market.

As governments in industrialized economies respond to China’s surge of high-value manufactured exports, they must recognize how the sources of Chinese firms’ advantages are changing. Today, those advantages come not just from subsidies and cheap credit at home but also from integrated ecosystems abroad, built through coordination between public and private enterprise.

This means that Brussels’s retaliatory tariffs and the recently announced limits on hybrid EVs imported from China, on their own, are unlikely to stem the tide of EVs made by Chinese firms operating around the world. China’s rivals—even in industrialized economies—will struggle to compete unless they can coordinate across borders and build supply chains and infrastructure that leverage or, far less plausibly, completely sidestep what China has already put in place.

Electricity prices have gained political salience as U.S. households feel the financial strain ahead of next month’s midterms. Senate authors of the landmark permitting overhaul bill argue it would lower costs through quicker approvals for new transmission lines to get more clean power flowing to homes and businesses more efficiently.

Set for a key procedural vote on the legislative vehicle when senators return on Nov. 9, the measure would make several politically dicey changes to bedrock environmental laws intended to make it easier to get more infrastructure projects built faster — from electric grid upgrades to oil drilling rigs.

That’s created a powerful alliance of clean energy and fossil fuel industry backers, a rarity in Washington that’s put some major lobbying muscle behind the yearslong effort culminating in last month’s deal between ordinarily strange political bedfellows.

The vast majority of power-generating facilities waiting years to be connected to the grid are wind and solar farms and battery storage systems, about 8,200 projects capable of over 2,000 gigawatts worth of generation and storage, according to the Energy Department’s Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.

Senate Energy and Natural Resources ranking member Martin Heinrich, D-N.M., one of the key negotiators, often cites the SunZia Wind and Transmission Project in his home state as motivation for the bill. The project, with 916 wind turbines and 550 miles of transmission lines, took 17 years to complete.

Such facilities are typically built in remote areas away from population centers, requiring more land and a need to navigate a substantial state-federal permitting bureaucracy. By contrast, traditional coal- and natural gas-fired power plants were mainly built in the 20th century with decades of transmission infrastructure in place around them.

Even some environmental critics of the broader bill support the measure’s transmission title. One is the Natural Resources Defense Council, which said that part of the bill would “produce substantial and durable benefits, updating an aging power grid by allowing new investments.”

Large electric utilities, however, aren’t so sure about upending a regulatory process for new transmission lines that’s mostly been handled at the state and local level where they’ve enjoyed more sway. But the industry appears divided ahead of next month’s vote, which gives the bill’s supporters an opening.

Pressure to act is also coming from the midterm campaign trail. A Hart Research survey of battleground House districts in early October, commissioned by environmental groups, found that heating and electric bills were the third-most important issue for voters after food costs and gas prices.

The political impetus is evident from Energy Information Administration forecasts that wholesale electricity prices will average $52 per megawatt hour this year, up 11 percent from 2025.

In territory governed by PJM Interconnection, the regional transmission organization responsible for 13 states — including those with key congressional seats in play, like Michigan, Ohio and Pennsylvania — prices are up 41 percent on average.

Reasons include utilities passing through the costs of upgrades to aging grid infrastructure; surging demand from data centers powering artificial intelligence; increased electric vehicle usage; extreme weather events and hotter temperatures driving consumers to turn up the air conditioning.

Whatever the causes, supplies aren’t coming online fast enough to meet demand for some 166 additional gigawatts of power needed by 2030, according to estimates by Grid Strategies, a consulting firm.

That’s mostly going to come from solar, wind and battery storage, with some natural gas as well — provided there’s enough power lines for delivery, said Ray Long, president of the American Council on Renewable Energy.

In the past, a sticking point for GOP lawmakers on permitting overhaul efforts has been trying to ensure their constituents don’t get saddled with higher bills to cover costs associated with transmission buildout if those communities aren’t benefiting from new renewable power sources.

The Senate bill tries to fix that problem by requiring the added costs to be shouldered only by those consumers getting their electricity from renewables, with payments “roughly” proportionate to the benefits received.

The bill “is a win-win for the issues that both Republicans and Democrats are trying to address in our power and electricity needs,” Long said.

But some utility industry players remain skeptical of shifting authority over the approval process from states to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, where they typically have less direct influence.

A leaked document from Oklahoma Gas and Electric Co., obtained by the nonprofit environmental group Energy and Policy Institute, lays out “significant concerns” with the bill as currently drafted.

Under the bill, FERC would be required to issue permits for high-voltage interstate transmission lines of 230 kilovolts or greater when a state lacks authority or does not act on an application within a year. Developers for larger projects of 345 kilovolts or more could apply simultaneously to FERC and a state to allow reviews to run on parallel timelines.

The bill also includes provisions to address transmission planning, a practice that has not been codified and is largely based on FERC regulatory orders.

The bill would codify a FERC order that requires local planning commissions to create a regional planning process, and for neighboring regions to create an interregional planning process. Projects approved in an interregional plan could then be taken directly to FERC for permitting, effectively cutting out state and local approvals.

It would also allow states to refer local utilities’ transmission planning practices to FERC for investigation. FERC would be able to cut a utility’s allowed profit or impose fines of up to $5 million if it finds an action was unjustified. This is intended to address concerns that utilities may overbuild infrastructure through inefficient spending that increases customer costs.

There’s also a mandate to use certain advanced transmission technologies FERC identifies as more cost-effective.

Finally, the measure would codify a separate FERC order barring incumbent utilities’ “right of first refusal,” or ability to build their own transmission lines in their service territory free of competition from independent developers. States could still enact their own rights of first refusal, but codifying the federal ban is viewed as a step backward.

OG&E’s position paper takes issue with the above provisions, arguing that “permitting reform should not expand federal authority at the expense of the states, displace or weaken state authority, eliminate [right of first refusal] protections, or expand a competitive transmission process that has delayed projects and increased customers overall costs.”

OG&E spokesperson Christi Woodworth confirmed the document was authentic. “OG&E believes there is a need to build necessary transmission faster and at the lowest reasonable cost while preserving the existing roles of the states and accountability to customers who ultimately bear those costs,” Woodworth said.

Big investor-owned utilities like Duke Energy Corp. and Southern Co. helped scotch a 2024 permitting package negotiated by then-Senate Energy and Natural Resources Chairman Joe Manchin III, I-W.Va., and then-ranking member John Barrasso, R-Wyo.

This year’s package is considered even more aggressive when it comes to overhauling the process of greenlighting transmission lines. So far, only two major utilities — Berkshire Hathaway Energy and American Electric Power Co. — have expressed support.

“This legislation will help accelerate the process to bring critical infrastructure projects online, supporting the investments required to enhance reliability for our customers, reduce costs, and create new economic opportunities in the states we serve,” said AEP CEO Bill Fehrman, whose company serves 11 states including Ohio and Michigan.

Other big utilities have kept their powder dry so far. The same goes for the main industry trade group, the Edison Electric Institute, which hasn’t taken a position.

“We are reviewing the transmission provisions and how they may affect efforts to deliver reliable, affordable electricity for our 250 million customers and their communities,” EEI President Drew Maloney said in a statement.

House Energy and Commerce Chairman Brett Guthrie, R-Ky., has said he wants changes to the Senate bill before he’d back taking it up in his chamber. A Guthrie spokesperson didn’t elaborate further on what he’s looking for.

Guthrie is the No. 1 congressional recipient of campaign donations this cycle from the utility sector, according to an analysis of Federal Election Commission filings by the Energy and Policy Institute, which tracks the industry’s lobbying and political activities.

Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., has endorsed the overall thrust of the Senate bill, though it could still look different if and when it reaches that chamber during the lame-duck session. Senate sponsors say they are soliciting feedback for a manager’s amendment they’ll offer if their chamber votes to get on the bill after the elections.

David Pomerantz, executive director of the Energy and Policy Institute, said it is likely that many utilities will oppose “virtually all the transmission reforms in the bill” and may characterize some of the provisions expanding FERC authority as a “federal power grab.”

“It’s very easy for me to imagine utility lobbying against the transmission reforms, either sinking the bill or, in a scenario where the bill passes, just severely weakening the parts of it that have the most buy-in,” said Pomerantz. “I think that’s a real threat to the bill’s efficacy, at least for the things that a lot of people seem to value in it.”

#578. Webb spotted a subtle difference in the interstellar comets ice

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Astronomers caught something unusual about the ice of a comet that came from another star system.

A Southwest Research Institute team pointed NASA's James Webb Space Telescope at interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS three times between Aug. 2025 and April 2026, catching it before and after its closest pass by the sun.

The space telescope spreads a comet's light into a rainbow, including infrared light that human eyes can't see. Water ice soaks up light at a few specific spots in the rainbow. That pattern makes it possible to detect water ice the way a fingerprint can identify a person.

Ice normally leaves one deep mark and two faint ones, and most comets around our sun show all three. But in 3I/ATLAS, the two faint marks barely registered, if they showed up at all. Researchers ran computer simulations and found that light scatters off comet grains in a similar way if tiny bits of ice are blended with more dust. Because the dust blocks light before it penetrates far through the ice, the lighter two marks tend to vanish.

"At first glance, 3I/ATLAS looks different from the icy comets we know," said Silvia Protopapa, lead author of a new paper in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, in a statement. "But when we placed it alongside distant icy worlds in our own solar system, it no longer looked out of place."

While the alien comet's dusty ice looked strange compared to the form usually seen in solar system comets, the team noticed that its makeup actually lines up with ice on some frozen worlds far past Neptune. That suggests ice may form in similar ways around different stars, as tiny grains that range from nearly pure to heavily dusty. If so, the raw materials for building planets may look much alike from one star to the next.

Comets like this one start out in planet-forming disks — the flat, spinning clouds of gas and dust that surround young stars. As worlds assemble, gravity can hurl leftover icy chunks clear out of a system. That may explain how some interstellar objects have lost their way from home.

Astronomers have caught just three passing through our solar system. Telescopes never detected a glowing halo of ice turning into gas, known as a coma, around the first, 'Oumuamua, causing many to later doubt that it was a comet. But both 2I/Borisov and 3I/ATLAS showed signs of a coma.

The team compared 3I/ATLAS's halo to existing Webb data on Trans-Neptunian objects, icy bodies that circle the sun beyond Neptune. Many scientists view these distant worlds as time capsules from our solar system's infancy.

Several groups of them share the comet's pattern: a strong water-ice signature, with the two faint ones barely detected or missing altogether. The best matches also carry carbon dioxide ice and carbon-rich compounds.

"The icy building blocks of planets, here and around other stars, may share ice in the form of tiny, micron-scale grains, ranging from relatively pure ice to ice mixed with dust," Protopapa said, "with 3I/ATLAS toward the dusty end."

If more evidence backs up this notion, a young star's disk may decide early on what form its ice will take, the researchers said. Comets and other icy bodies that grow there would carry at least some of that original material with them.

By April, as 3I/ATLAS raced away from the sun, Webb's readings pointed to a second crop of ice-rich grains. They ran slightly larger than the earlier ones but still measured roughly a micron — one-millionth of a meter and far thinner than a human hair.

"We can't yet tell whether the changes over time are the result of the sun heating the grains differently at each observation, or of new material exposed as the comet’s surface eroded near the sun," said Michael Kelley, a co-author of the paper, in a statement. "Both are possible, and telling them apart is one of the open questions we aim to address."
