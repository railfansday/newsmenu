#3760. Kim Kardashian Weighs In on Potential Run for Political Office: 'We Have Time'

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Kim Kardashian cannot ignore the chatter from some people who speculate she may one day run for political office.

“People ask me all the time,” Kardashian, 46, told journalist Jonathan Van Meter in his new book Citizen Kim: The Women Who Created the Future. “I mean, the amount of people who wrote to me after [Joe] Biden’s disastrous debate? The fact that you guys think I’m capable of this? I am so flattered.”

Before she asks for anyone’s vote, however, Kardashian said she has some things on her to-do list that need to be accomplished first.

“I think that, No. 1, I have to pass the bar and become a lawyer first,” she continued. “And if you do run for office, you really can’t own and run a company. And I love Skims and what we’ve built. That is my focus.”

Skims was cofounded by Kardashian in June 2019 and has grown into a top-selling shapewear and clothing brand. The company is valued at approximately $5 billion and now includes shoes, slippers, bags, hair accessories and more.

“I definitely need some time,” Kardashian added. “But we have time.”

Perhaps one of those friends who wouldn’t be surprised if The Kardashians star entered political office is Van Jones.

A mentor and friend throughout Kardashian’s journey to becoming a lawyer, Jones, 58, predicts the businesswoman will one day open a law firm with a criminal justice reform pro bono aspect.

“And I’m sure it will be one of the most sought out and successful law firms in the country because she’s Kim Kardashian. She’s not going to not pass the bar ultimately. She’s not going to not become an attorney,” Jones said in Citizen Kim. “And then at some point, when you’re from the most famous family in the country, when you have the most famous name in the world and you’ve done great work, and now you’re not 41, but 51, or 61, at some point, somebody says, ‘How come you’re not a senator? How come you’re not the governor of California?’”

Kardashian and her inner circle were interviewed by Van Meter over the course of nearly 10 years for an in-depth biography called Citizen Kim.

When Kardashian asked Van Meter about the title of the book, he explained the significance.

“In this story, you are the mogul and the muse; you are the billionaire and the pretty young thing,” he wrote in the book’s prologue. “You don’t need a rich guy, because you’re the rich guy — Citizen Kim.”

Citizen Kim: The Woman Who Created the Future is available in stores Tuesday, October 27.

Busy Philipps‘ new show, Cupertino, is staging a reunion with Renée Elise Goldsberry two years after Girls5Eva was canceled — and Us has the first look.

In an exclusive sneak peek at the Thursday, October 29, episode, Natalia (Philipps) and Nichole (Goldsberry) spend quality time at the firm while Michael (Mike Colter), Olivia (Rachel Keller) and Christy (Ella Stiller) are away.

Natalia, who is boxing, discusses Bo (Rob McClure), who Nichole claims is the property of the company she works for.

“If you lost track of your IP it is your fault, not ours,” Natalia replies before Nichole suggests they “move this conversation to court.”

According to the official episode synopsis, Michael and Olivia “defend the young founders of a prediction market site, one of whom has been arrested while the other flees on his private jet with Christy in tow after a night out together.”

Philipps, 47, and Goldsberry, 55, previously shared the screen on Girls5eva, which aired on Peacock from 2021 to 2022. The musical comedy then moved to Netflix and released a third season before the show officially came to an end.

Cupertino, meanwhile, marks Philipps’ return to TV after she underwent surgery for a brain tumor in early March. The show stars Colter as a Silicon Valley attorney who, after being fired by his employer, forms a firm with another laid off lawyer (Keller) to represent people harmed by the technology industry.

For Philipps, the show’s focus on questioning the use of AI hit close to home.

“I’m not an AI user. We don’t use that in my home. I actually am obsessed with my daughter’s eighth grade — and with the entire school. Corporate Ethereum [ETH] accumulation is a double-edged sword.

The logic is simple: With DATs aligned to the broader market, there is a direct impact on their actual ETH exposure if there is either a risk on/risk off trigger. With risk-off sentiment forming around the FOMC decision and earnings reports, DATS may come under pressure as capital flows across traditional risk assets shift.

In this context, the upcoming 48 hours are potentially very important for Ethereum. BitMine (NASDAQ: BMNR) has just announced it bought 10,452 ETH worth about $24 million in a post on X. This brings its 30-day accumulation of Ethereum up to about 68,000 ETH. Its total holdings are now 6.05 million ETH.

The interesting part? BMNR also bought back $45 million worth of its own shares.

From a strategic standpoint, BMNR continues to add more ETH while buying back company stock.

This is particularly noteworthy as BMNR has fallen 20%+ this month, making this its worst monthly run since June and wiping out over 60% of its Q3 gains, after almost hitting $30. The move suggests BMNR is using the price weakness to buy back shares while continuing to build its Ethereum treasury.

This brings the focus to the upcoming macro week.

Despite lower oil prices helping the U.S. equities rebound, the BMNR has fallen over 6% this week, and its $20 support level is under threat. If there is a greater risk-off mood, this can cause further downside pressure on BMNR’s stock price as it continues to buy back shares at a discount.

This raises a key question: Can BitMine sustain its Ethereum accumulation strategy amid broader market weakness?

As the largest Ethereum treasury, BMNR’s recent move could trigger a ripple effect across other DATs.

However, the on-chain data suggests otherwise. From a macro perspective, November starts with some serious volatility, with the market focusing on interest rates. If the Fed maintains a neutral stance this time, but gives a slightly more hawkish outlook on the future policy, then the market could still see a risk-off move as capital flows out of risk assets.

Against this backdrop, the slowdown in corporate ETH accumulation comes under scrutiny. Following BMNR, the next biggest holder of ETH is SharpLink Gaming (NASDAQ: SBET) which currently holds over 880k ETH in its DAT. However, the company has only added less than 1,100 ETH over the past 30 days.

Technically, this comes despite more than 68k ETH flowing into BitMine’s Ethereum treasury, highlighting how corporate accumulation remains heavily concentrated rather than being spread across other holders.

More importantly, as shown above, SBET has dropped by more than 22% so far this month. This means that if the market weakness continues, these treasuries may not have the ability to add as much ETH as before, and BMNR could take on a greater portion of the corporate Ethereum demand.

So, while BMNR’s latest 10k ETH purchase is a bullish signal, it may not be enough to offset the broader slowdown in corporate ETH accumulation. In this context, Ethereum might remain under pressure ahead of key macro events.

Many animated films are innovative, beautifully drawn or designed, and full of rich storytelling. They’re true works of art. However, I think as adults we often skip the genre, especially if we don’t have children. There is an assumption that these films will only appeal to kids. Wildwood goes against that argument.

It begs to be seen by adults. This dark fairy tale is visually stunning and has a storyline that may make Wildwood one of the best fantasy movies of all time. I saw the film at an early fan screening. That show included an introduction by Wildwood director Travis Knight. He explained the film’s process and gave a shocking detail. It stuck with me and inspired me.

Films often take years to film, edit, and reshoot, but it’s rare that a film takes almost two decades to complete and release. NASA's Perseverance Mars rover left a mark on the Martian landscape when it passed a historic milestone driving across the Red Planet.

On Oct. 11, Perseverance passed 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers) driven on Mars. This is farther than anything has ever traveled on the surface of another world, earning the rover the interplanetary driving record.

Previously, this record was set by NASA's Opportunity rover, and it took Opportunity roughly 14 years to drive this far. Perseverance traversed this distance in just over 5 years.

In this snapshot captured by Perseverance, the rover looked back at its progress, capturing its own tracks pressed into the planet's dusty surface. This moment of reflection captures years of achievement on the Red Planet.

Perseverance's driving record comes after many years of different rovers pushing autonomous driving on other worlds to new levels. As Vandi Verma, Chief Engineer for Robotic Operations at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, shared in a recent mission update, the rover team continues to push the envelope, exploring new and better ways to drive the rovers remotely from another planet.

While Perseverance has traveled nearly 30 miles across the surface of another planet, the rover has accomplished so much more than just this distance record on its enduring mission.

Since landing on Mars back in 2021, Perseverance has observed, sampled and studied a wide variety of rocks, boulder fields, Martian slopes and more. Recently, observations made using Perseverance provided the strongest evidence yet of possible biosignatures on the planet.

While we have not yet found conclusive evidence that life once existed on Mars, as the rover has made its way across the planet's surface, it has gotten us closer to that answer than we have ever been before.

#3707. The Child-Actor Conundrum That’s Plagued Hollywood Forever—and How to Fix It (Maybe)

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“No matter how good the union safeguards get, no matter the policing of grooming behavior, none of it will change the fact that acting while so young strips us of our childhoods and leaves us to suffer the consequences—alone.”

Alison Pill had wanted to express this message for decades. But she didn’t publicly articulate it until this summer—less than two weeks after Hayden Panettiere, who shot her first commercial before she celebrated her first birthday, died of an accidental overdose at 36. Months after Daveigh Chase, star of The Ring, died at 35 from AIDS complications following a long period of homelessness. A year and a half after Harriet the Spy star Michelle Trachtenberg died at 39 due to diabetes complications, though the full story may be more complicated.

Pill laid out her blunt assessment in a New York Times op-ed headlined “I Was a Child Actor. No One Deserves That Fate.” The conclusion she reached was stark: “The greatest storytellers should find a way to capture the world we live in without child actors, for all our sakes.”

“It took me a while to figure out what, exactly, I wanted to say,” Pill tells Vanity Fair. Once she did, though, she made her point simply and elegantly: that it’s basically impossible to pay a child to act without exploiting them in some way, even unintentionally. That kids who are set up to succeed—kids with loving parents, financial security, on-set support systems, and a burning and internally motivated desire to perform—still grow up to become adults who feel they were taken advantage of and not protected in the way they should have been.

Pill counts herself among that group. “My parents are wonderful people. I didn’t have my money stolen,” she says. Yet in the years since her own child-acting days—when she played a young Lorna Luft in a celebrated Judy Garland miniseries, and Katie Holmes’s little sister in Pieces of April, and Lindsay Lohan’s best friend in Confessions of a Teenage Drama Queen—she’s grown increasingly uncomfortable with the realities of what it means to put children onscreen. Especially now that she’s both a mother in real life and often playing one in her work. “If we can’t protect the most innocent of innocents in any meaningful way, or even attempt to,” says Pill, “what are we doing?”

I spoke with five other former child actors for this story—and while not all of them take the same hard-line position that Pill does, everyone in the group tends to agree on two things.

The first is that acting in a professional setting inherently hurts kids. “There will 100% be consequences to a child in an adultified, high-stress, and high-stakes industry—both in terms of the potential harm to that kid and just the developmental loss,” says Alex Winter, who made his Broadway debut at the age of 12. “That’s just a given. You cannot develop normally as a child if you are a working child.” Mara Wilson, of Mrs. Doubtfire and Matilda fame, puts a finer point on it: “I think that you could probably be a somewhat healthy child actor. I do not think you could be a healthy child star.”

The second is that there’s more to be done to keep those kids safe—simple, concrete changes productions could enact now. “I think we could protect children without necessarily taking away their opportunities,” says Who’s the Boss? alum Alyssa Milano.

“There’s this mysticism surrounding what there is to be done, and there’s a lot of theories and a lot of outside opinion,” former Disney star Christy Carlson Romano adds. “And I think this is an industry issue that needs to be handled effectively inside.”

“I have been asked many times which of my pictures I liked best,” Charlie Chaplin wrote in the pages of this magazine in 1921. “Perhaps the one I most enjoyed making is my last picture, The Kid, written expressly to bring out the qualities of another actor, a five-year-old actor this time, Jack Coogan.” Chaplin said he was charmed by Coogan from the moment they met: “He was with his parents in a Los Angeles hotel, sleeping, as a child will, in a chair. He was roused in order to meet me. He rubbed his eyes, jumped up, made his politest bow, and promptly went back to sleep.”

So Chaplin built an entire feature around that sleepy little boy, thus launching the career of the kid generally agreed to be Hollywood’s very first bona fide child star. Coogan would inadvertently set the template for those following in his wake.

Long before adolescence, Coogan earned millions from his films and the merch that bore his face. But after aging out of cute-kid roles and flunking out of college, he learned that his profligate parents had squandered his fortune. His legacy is a litany of stories with headlines like “The Very Sad Story of Jackie Coogan”—and California’s Coogan Law, which requires employers to deposit 15% of a minor’s gross wages into a trust account that only the child can access when they turn 18. Coogan also played Uncle Fester in the original Addams Family, making a triumphant return to the screen as an adult—older and wiser.

Child actors aren’t a monolith. Some, like Coogan, end up traumatized by their time in the industry. Others have overwhelmingly positive experiences before growing into highly functioning adults. Many fall somewhere in the middle. Armchair critics have a proclivity to focus on the negative outcomes, which “tends to stigmatize the business in a way that isn’t particularly helpful for anyone,” says Winter, who’s spoken openly about the sexual abuse he survived as a child actor. As an adult, says Wilson, “I see a lot of people making the assumption that any child who was in the industry must have been abused on sets.”

Since his Bill & Ted days, Winter has largely traded acting for directing, working frequently on TV series for kids. And according to him, those sets are generally much healthier places to spend time than they once were. “Despite the MAGA discourse that they’re all pedophiles—which is really, really not true—most people who go into that environment are family-oriented by nature,” he says. “The days of the really crass, bullish, patriarchal, male-run sets of the ’70s and ’80s into the early ’90s—that is really a bygone era. You would not get away with being that person today.”

Alyssa Milano and Tony Danza, Who's the Boss?, 1985.

In some cases, growing up on a set might actually be better for a kid’s well-being than the alternative. “I was dyslexic,” Milano says. “I don’t know that I would’ve been okay in a normal classroom, under normal social structures.” She points out that performing is one of the few ways that a talented but underprivileged kid can earn enough money to change their circumstances.

Yet for every kid actor who becomes a successful and seemingly well-adjusted adult, every Daniel Radcliffe or Drew Barrymore, there seems to be a River Phoenix: a bright light snuffed out too soon, a cautionary tale. And even the Radcliffes and Barrymores hardly seem to emerge into adulthood unscathed.

So yes, it’s hard to know definitively whether child actors have worse adult outcomes than any other group. “There’s this spectrum of experience,” says Romano, “so there’s no clarity.” But what we know for sure is that, especially in the post-MeToo era, former child actors who did have negative experiences have been speaking more publicly about them: in pieces like Pill’s, in documentaries like ID’s Quiet on Set and Winter’s own Showbiz Kids, in blockbuster books like Jennette McCurdy’s I’m Glad My Mom Died and Alyson Stoner’s Semi-Well-Adjusted Despite Literally Everything. (Via reps, both McCurdy and Stoner politely declined interview requests.) Romano recently released her own memoir about her Disney days, titled Once Upon a Trainwreck: The Fall and Rise of a Child Star.

These onetime kid actors have spoken movingly about facing isolation, external pressure, intense public scrutiny, and the psychic pain of constant rejection on the audition circuit. In an article Romano published after Panettiere’s death, she compared early fame to CTE: “Not as a diagnosis,” she wrote. “As a way of thinking about repeated impact.”

Alex Winter and Keanu Reeves in Bill & Ted's Excellent Adventure, 1989.

It seems high time, then, that the entertainment industry followed the NFL’s lead by enacting stricter metaphorical concussion protocols. Nobody I spoke to believes that banning child actors altogether is realistic; everyone similarly agrees that, say, replacing actual kids with AI-generated ones isn’t the answer. “I know that’ll be the argument: What if instead of adding staff to a production and considering the human element of any of this, we just AI them? I think it’s depraved,” says Pill.

Even Bryn Mooser, the cofounder of Asteria—a company that brands itself as Hollywood’s ethical AI studio—is with them. “Replacing kids might help alleviate having less kids on set,” he says, “but it’s certainly not going to solve another massive problem, which is that people don’t want virtual actors in a movie.” And though Mooser allows that de-aging technology has gotten vastly more sophisticated than it once ws, he maintains that “it’s so much easier to have a child playing a child. You know what I’m saying?”

This informal conclave suggests more practical solutions. Wilson proposes taking the Coogan Law further—ensuring that more than 15% of a child’s earnings will be secured for the child—and making sure it’s enacted nationally. Romano wants to see more investment in the Entertainment Community Fund’s Looking Ahead program—which offers trainings, resources, and support to young actors—as well as a formal study that can actually determine how many former child actors are struggling, and in which ways. Milano would like to see action from SAG-AFTRA: “I think the unions could do a better job of training our young performers about what it means to have self-care and to protect themselves.” More than one source notes that the union might have a vested interest in this issue particularly because its current president, Sean Astin, is a former child actor himself.

Pill suggests what might be the most radical idea: that sets where kids are working should hire designated child welfare advocates. They would operate sort of like intimacy coordinators do, acting as a buffer between the child actor, the rest of the production, and, if necessary, the child’s parent or guardian—who “must be present at all times while a minor is working,” according to SAG-AFTRA policy.

Christy Carlson Romano attending The Kid premier in 2000.

It’s true that film staffers have unofficially served functions like this one in the past. “On the film sets I was on, I had studio teachers who basically acted like that,” says Wilson—referring to the educators who are required to be employed on any set where a minor is working for at least three consecutive days, according to SAG-AFTRA policy. But there’s a difference between ad hoc advocacy and the kind that’s mandatory. Especially when it comes to studio teachers, who “are easily corrupted, in my opinion,” says Romano, “because they are the liaison between production and the child.”

Few people understand this better than Maggie Blake. She too was once a child actor, appearing in ’90s and ’00s-era Canadian productions like Emily of New Moon. She now works as a designated child safety and well-being consultant on film and TV sets. “When I was a kid, I had a therapist on set—but I didn’t know who she was,” says Blake. “She was a random lady in the corner. And I don’t want to be that random lady.”

Her ethos is straightforward: “I want to prepare the production for the child, rather than expecting the child to adapt to an adult system,” Blake says. “So I’m there talking to ADs and producers and directors, saying, ‘How are we going to support this child?’ I want to meet them before they come to set and understand their developmental needs. I want to talk to their parents and caregivers and understand where they’re at, then build a relationship.”

Blake helps prepare kids emotionally to perform violent or mature material. She speaks out when her charges are too tired during a night shoot to do their best work. She counsels young performers who are despondent after losing out on a part. She works with their parents too, urging them to release the pressure valve.

For an industry that’s already forced to navigate competing union interests, corporate headwinds, and endless miles of red tape, adding another layer of compliance may seem impossible. To the people who think that way, “I would say, ‘Risk mitigation, risk mitigation, risk mitigation,’” says Blake. “We have intimacy coordinators. We have stunt directors for adults. And yet the most vulnerable population that is working on sets has no one.” Intimacy and stunt coordinators aren’t infallible, of course—but most would agree they’re better than nothing.

Mara Wilson and Robin Williams, Mrs. Doubtfire, 1993.

Like almost all of the former child actors in this story, Blake is a parent herself. Like most of them, she will not let her own kids act professionally. “I think that it’s telling that there aren’t very many second-generation child actors,” says Wilson—who doesn’t have kids, but wouldn’t want them to act in a professional capacity if she did. Most child actors whose parents were also child actors, she points out, “tend to be in projects with their families.”

Milano’s daughter, 12-year-old Elizabella, is one of them. This summer, the two of them shot a film together in Canada.

“Before she was born, I was like, Oh, what am I going to do if I want a kid in this business? But then after she was born, I realized she came out who she is,” Milano says. Former child actors can recognize the type. “There are kids who really want to be onstage, performing and taking dancing and singing lessons, and that’s who they are,” says Winter. “That’s absolutely who I was, and my parents kind of had to get out of the way.”

But Milano didn’t. She was close by as her daughter filmed on what sounds like a particularly nurturing set. “Bella had a social worker…and there was someone there making sure she had her breaks, and was comfortable, and didn’t work longer than the hours that she was supposed to work,” she says.

When asked how it felt to work with Bella, her mother’s voice turns dreamy. “It was amazing,” Milano says. “My most memorable moment on set—I think ever in my entire career—was watching them shoot her close up from the monitor. It was really beautiful.”

As announced late last week, Arc will officially sunset on Dec. 22, 2026, giving users two months to transition their data to an alternative.

One option is to move to the company's new AI-focused Dia browser, though there are other (potentially better) choices depending on what you're looking for. Most data from Arc—including Spaces, Favorites, pinned tabs, and folders as well as passwords, bookmarks, extensions, and history—will transition automatically over to Dia, but if you're switching to a different platform entirely, you'll need to take what you can with you before Dec. 22.

Officially, Arc supports exporting Spaces and tabs only, and the resulting Export.md plain-text file is simply a list of links that you can open and bookmark in your new browser. MacOS users can initiate this process via Arc > Settings > Advanced > Export Data and saving the file to a preferred location. To simply copy and paste every link from one folder, right-click the folder, select Copy All Links, and paste them wherever you'd like (select Copy All Links as Markdown to keep link titles on macOS). You can also save Easels as images by tapping the share icon > Share Via... and selecting PNG.

There's no formal process to export other Arc data. If you saved logins in Arc, now is a good time to start moving credentials over to a different platform. (Our top picks for password managers aren't tied to a single browser.)

There are a few alternatives to Arc's official export process, which is highly manual and doesn't keep your folder structure intact. The extension is available for Chrome, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, and Firefox. CF Benchmarks, whose pricing indices underpin BlackRock's bitcoin exchange-traded fund (ETF) and CME Group's crypto futures, is bringing its market data to blockchain networks as more financial firms look to offer traditional investment products through decentralized finance (DeFi).

The company, which is owned by Kraken parent Payward, has introduced CFB Oracle, a service that gives blockchain applications access to its benchmarks for cryptocurrencies, stocks, bonds, commodities and currencies.

The idea is to make the same pricing infrastructure used by major financial institutions available to developers building investment products on blockchains.

For example, a blockchain-based fund holding tokenized stocks would need reliable prices to calculate the value of its portfolio. A lending platform accepting tokenized bonds as collateral would need that data to determine how much users can borrow and when positions should be liquidated.

CF Benchmarks already provides this type of pricing data to traditional financial markets. Its indices are used by products representing more than $120 billion in assets, according to the company.

With CFB Oracle, developers can use those benchmarks to build products such as onchain index funds, tokenized portfolios and derivatives without having to develop their own pricing systems. The service is available on Ethereum ETH$2,490.55, HyperEVM and Robinhood Chain, with plans to support other compatible networks.

CF Benchmarks says the data is digitally signed, allowing applications to check that prices came from the company and haven't been altered. It also publishes each index at the same frequency for all customers, rather than charging for faster access.

The service has a planned use within Payward. The Kraken parent intends to use CFB Oracle to supply pricing data for an upcoming U.S. perpetual futures offering powered by Hyperliquid (HYPE). That product is still awaiting regulatory approval.

The launch puts CF Benchmarks in competition with oracle providers such as Chainlink LINK$12.64 and Pyth, which already supply market data to decentralized trading and lending platforms.

CFB Oracle won't issue funds or handle trades itself. Its role is to supply the reference prices those products need to operate.
