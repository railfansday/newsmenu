#5581. Democrats still poised to make gains, but poll shows warning signs for just how many

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A member of the League of Women Voters of Greater Cleveland helps hold the flag during the national anthem before a basketball game on Oct. 26, 2026 in Cleveland, Ohio. Jason Miller/Getty Images hide caption

📲 Get the latest on the midterms & more in the NPR App

Democrats retain significant midterm electoral advantages, with key data pointing to them being the favorites to win control of the U.S. House. But, there are indications of why a potential wave could be limited, a new NPR/PBS News/Marist poll finds.

The poll also sheds light on the issues driving voters across the ideological spectrum. The cost of living continues to be top of mind overall with a third of people saying they've experienced financial hardship in the past year and 4 in 10 saying their personal finances have gotten worse.

"The economy is at the top of my list right now," said Shun Maurice, a 47-year-old from Arizona who participated in the poll. He's an independent who leans toward Democrats and said high food and gas prices are a strain.

"They're too expensive for everybody, no matter who you voted for," he said. "They're just too expensive for the American people in general, and I don't like that."

Preserving democracy also continues to resonate with Democrats and independents, while immigration is the biggest concern for self-described MAGA supporters.

The poll of 1,289 registered voters was conducted Oct. 19-20. When registered voters are mentioned, the margin of error is +/- 3.7 percentage points. When likely voters are mentioned, it is +/- 3.8 points.

This was the first survey this cycle that Marist applied a likely voter screen, and on multiple questions, likely voters were — within the margin of error — slightly less likely than registered voters to disapprove of the president. Likely voters overall were also slightly less likely to want Democrats to run Congress.

Likely voters are something of a special sauce in polling. Survey researchers apply various metrics — like levels of enthusiasm, past voting history and how important voting is to different respondents — to try to identify the subsample of people who are most likely to vote.

In this survey, just 38% of registered voters approved of the job President Trump is doing, with 61% disapproving (-23). Likely voters gave him a 40% to 59% rating (-19), still not very good, but even a slight margin can make a difference in how significant the gains either party might make in the election are.

That said, an equal share — 53% — of registered and likely voters said they "strongly disapprove" of the job Trump is doing, tied with last month as the highest ever in the poll.

Trump's favorability rating is even lower — just 37% of likely voters have a favorable impression of Trump, while 59% have a negative one.

Democrats also had a strong advantage on which party's candidate people said they are more likely to vote for in their district if the election were held today, known as the generic congressional ballot. Among registered voters, it was a 10-point advantage. With likely voters, it was 8 points, still indicative of Democratic gains.

When Democrats have had that kind of lead on the congressional ballot, they have historically done very well. On average, it was +7 in 2018 and +12 in 2006. In both years, Democrats won the House with at least 30 seats flipped.

But that was before the 2020 Census and ensuing regular redistricting — and the rare, mid-decade redistricting in the last year and a half after Trump asked Republican states to redraw their maps to potentially give Republicans more seats. Though Democrats followed suit, both redistricting periods have tilted congressional seats in Republicans' direction and given them a relative advantage nationally.

Perhaps because of some of those structural disadvantages and past performance, there is a notable degree of nervousness among Democrats.

While 98% of Democratic likely voters said they would vote for a Democrat, just 81% thought the Democrat would win, a 17-point difference. Republicans were only 10 points less likely to believe their candidate would win. Notably, among independents, a higher share thought the Democratic candidate would win.

Working in Democrats' favor — and against incumbents: 55% of likely voters said it's more important in this election to vote for candidates with new ideas who will bring about change. (It was a tick higher with registered voters at 57%.)

Chris Agan, a 43-year-old Democrat in Tyler, Texas, who participated in the survey, never saw himself as an impassioned midterm voter, until this year.

"This year is different. This year, it feels like every opportunity to vote is a referendum against the party in power," said Agan, who's backing Democrat James Talarico over Republican Ken Paxton in the race for U.S. Senate.

"We've been seeing a very slow decline in the standard of living in this state, and it's becoming apparently evident to more people at this time," he added. "I think Talarico embodies that realization that there needs to be change."

Double the percentage of likely voters also said their vote would be one against Trump (44%) as opposed to one for him (21%). In the 2024 presidential election, 55% of those who voted for Trump said their vote was for their candidate, according to exit polls.

Trump is not on the ballot, but he has asked voters to "pretend" he is.

Though Trump's unpopularity poses challenges for Republicans, it may not directly translate to more votes for Democrats. Some swing voters don't like the lurch in some corners of the Democratic Party toward democratic socialism.

Matthew Schroder is a 38-year-old independent living in Charlotte, N.C., who was part of the poll. He backed Joe Biden in 2020 and Trump in 2024. Now, he says that while he's unhappy with Trump's job performance, it isn't what's driving his vote this year.

"What I have seen on the left-hand side is a little bit more of an embrace of democratic socialism, which I just oppose completely," said Schroder, who's supporting Republican Michael Whatley over Democrat Roy Cooper in the state's closely watched race for U.S. Senate.

"I know Roy Cooper is considered a moderate, but the fact that the Democratic Party is even letting some of these voices be heard a little bit more … is not something I'm interested in in the least," he added. "It's not a vote of 'yes for Trump' as much as a vote of, Democrats need to completely reject [democratic socialism] and go a completely different direction."

Democratic likely voters are 10 points more likely to say they are "very enthusiastic" about voting (71% vs. 61%). That's actually wider than the difference between Democratic and Republican registered voters in the poll.

The caution for Democrats, though, is that several of their key pillar groups are not very enthusiastic to vote, and Republicans have closed the gap on who is following the election "very closely."

Just 42% of likely voters under 45, 53% of non-white voters and 56% of white women with college degrees said they are "very enthusiastic" to vote. All of those groups disapproved of the job Trump is doing.

Compare that to pro-Trump groups — MAGA voters (68%), white women without degrees (66%) and white evangelical voters (61%).

For independents, 48% report being very enthusiastic to vote.

Traditionally, younger, as well as Black and Latino voters turn out at disproportionately lower rates in midterm elections. But with the realignment under Trump, more white, college-educated voters are voting Democratic and, as a group, they turn out at the highest rates.

When it comes to paying close attention, last month just 34% of registered Republicans said they're following the election very closely, compared to 42% of Democrats.

Now, they're even – 46% of Democrats and 45% of Republicans said they're paying very close attention. When it comes to likely voters, there's no difference either – 51% of Democrats to 50% of Republicans.

On the positive side for Democrats: those who voted for Democrat Kamala Harris in 2024 are 15 points more likely than Trump 2024 voters to say they are very enthusiastic. And Democrats overall have an 8-point advantage over Republicans when it comes to who cares a "great deal" about who controls Congress.

Three-quarters of likely voters are confident that their state and local governments will run a fair and accurate election.

That includes solid majorities of Democrats (88%), Republicans (68%) and independents (75%), but Republicans were 20 points less likely than Democrats to think they would be run well. MAGA supporters were almost 30 points less likely.

Large majorities also said their preferred candidate should accept the results of the election. That included 70% of Republicans, but again, they were 20 points less likely than Democrats to say they should accept the results. A quarter of Republicans said their candidate should probably or definitely challenge the results.

There was also a sharp divide between those who identified as democratic socialists and MAGA supporters — 86% of democratic socialists but only 65% of MAGA supporters said their preferred candidate should accept the result. MAGA supporters were twice as likely to say they should challenge.

Methodology: This NPR/PBS News/Marist Poll was conducted from Oct. 19-20 and surveyed a national sample of 1,289 registered voters. They were reached through a multi-mode design: by phone, text or online. To create a likely voter sample, Marist applied a likely voter weight to all registered voters. When registered voters are mentioned, the margin of error is +/- 3.7 percentage points. When likely voters are mentioned, it is +/- 3.8 points. That means results fall within a range of roughly eight points – about four points lower or higher.

#4738. How to Winterize a Garden: Ready Your Plants to Weather the Cold With These Expert Tips

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

As fall winds down, you’ll pull sweaters out of storage and crank the thermostat, but what about your outdoor plants? Knowing how to winterize a garden is key to welcoming spring with an abundance of thriving greenery—as opposed to a sad brown patch of lawn. If frost, snow, rain, and freezing temperatures are in the forecast, know that your outdoor spaces don’t have to face the elements without a little assistance.

To prepare your garden for winter, you’ll need burlap; straw; garden tools like pruning shears and a pruning saw or lopper; a compost pile or ready source of nutrient-rich matter; and, ideally, some winter-ready plants that can take the place of flowering annuals and edible plants.

One more item to add to your list (in potentially large quantities): mulch. “One of the biggest things we do is mulch, and then mulch some more,” says Christina Stembel, founder and CEO of D2C e-commerce flower company Farmgirl Flowers. “We bring in what feels like an absurd amount of mulch every fall and use it just about everywhere. It helps insulate roots, suppress weeds, protect the soil through rain [and snow], and eventually adds organic matter back into the ground. We mulch our garden roses, peonies, perennial beds, [and more].”

Amy Enfield, who has more than 30 years of experience in the lawn- and garden-care industry and is currently a senior horticulturalist at Miracle-Gro, offers thoughts on a mindful first step ahead of digging in: “Before pulling out tools or buying materials, walk your garden and take stock of what you have. Note which plants are annuals that will not return, which are perennials you want to protect, where the soil looks tired or compacted, and which areas might benefit from a cover crop over winter. This assessment keeps you from doing more work than necessary or missing something important.”

Burlap adds a layer of protection against the elements for this outdoor plant in the wintertime.

“Do not begin with applying mulch,” says Jose Lopez, the founder and owner of Williams Landscaping Inc. “First, walk through your fall garden to pull up dead foliage. Pull any foliage that shows signs of disease as well. Next, apply appropriate care to the plants that will remain. Finally, cover your soil. If you do not complete these first three steps, you’re simply burying the problems of the growing season.” To cover that soil, there are three primary materials, each of which plays a different role, Lopez says. “Compost supplies nutrients to the soil. Mulch serves as insulation [and you can] use the leaves that are readily available, shredding them for mulch. Straw has no place in most flowerbeds but is suitable for vegetable gardening.” Just be mindful of where you are placing covering materials, and “do not layer large amounts of wet leaves over a newly planted shrub because the water can cause root rot at its base,” Lopez notes.

Raised garden beds tend to be used for annual flowers and edible plants, and these typically need to be removed, says Dr. Enfield. “Leaving their debris in the garden over winter creates a cozy habitat for overwintering pests and fungal disease.” Pull any spent annuals and finished vegetable plants, disposing of or composting them. “Diseased plant material should go in the trash rather than the compost pile,” Dr. Enfield says.

Once the beds are cleared, Dr. Enfield says to “amend the soil before it freezes. Working organic matter, like compost, composted manure, or a bagged soil into garden beds in fall gives it the entire winter to work into the soil structure, so it is ready to support planting in spring. Spread three inches over cleared beds and work it into the top six inches of native soil before winter sets in.”

Some plants are tough enough to make it through the winter without much support, but others can benefit from some TLC in the autumn. For those plants that aren’t hardy enough to withstand winter in your climate, you’ll need to break out some of the aforementioned materials: burlap, straw, frost cloth, and horticultural fleece. Amanda Webb, head gardener at Windy Hill Farm and Preserve, notes that “burlap is particularly useful for protecting exposed evergreens from winter wind, while straw can provide insulation around the crowns of marginally hardy perennials. I prefer breathable materials over plastic, which can trap moisture and create problems when temperatures fluctuate.”

Shrubs and other borderline hardy plants also need to take cover from the cold. “Some plants, particularly broadleaf evergreens like rhododendrons, hollies, and boxwoods, as well as any shrubs or perennials that are on the edge of their cold hardiness zone, benefit from physical protection over winter,” says Dr. Enfield. Burlap is the most commonly used material for this purpose; it can be wrapped loosely around individual plants or used to construct a simple windscreen that protects evergreens from the desiccating winter winds and sun, which can cause browning and leaf scorch even in plants that are otherwise cold-hardy. “Avoid wrapping plants in plastic, which traps moisture and heat and can do more harm than good. Burlap breathes, which is what makes it the material of choice.” For young or newly planted trees, wrapping the trunk with tree wrap or burlap protects against sun scald, “which is the cracking of bark caused by rapid temperature changes on the south and southwest sides of the trunk during winter thaws.”

Frost protection will help shield these potted plants from freezing temperatures.

Gardening doesn’t have to stop in winter; you just have to focus on plants that thrive in the cold. “I can’t stand seeing a vegetable bed sitting bare all winter,” says Mirela Bajic, senior garden designer at House Designer. “If you’re not growing anything in it, put something in for the soil’s sake. Winter rye is brilliant for that, [as] the roots hold everything together through the bad weather and then you just cut it back come spring.” She adds that many plants are more than rugged enough to make it through frosty, even snowy seasons. “Kale, cabbage, leeks, chard, [and more] all keep going through the cold if you let them.” Winter-ready crops like these help hold the soil together, provide some lovely greenery amid the gray backdrop of wintertime, and can even be used in hearty cold-weather meals.

It’s imperative that you tend to your property’s sprinkler systems and hoses, says Webb. “Drain irrigation systems and hoses if freezing temperatures are expected,” she says, or you risk catastrophic damage once water in the lines freezes and expands.

First, you’ll need to shut off the water supply to your sprinklers and hoses, removing the latter and putting them away for winter. Then, blow out sprinkler lines using compressed air to expel any residual water from the pipes. You should use a compressor set to 80 PSI or lower to avoid damaging sprinkler heads in the process. Once the lines are clear, leave the water shut off at the source inside your home (or garage or greenhouse or wherever the main water connection point is found) while leaving external valves partially open so last drips can fall and trapped water has a place to expand if it freezes.

Just because it’s too cold outside to grow colorful flowers or tasty tomatoes, peppers, or herbs doesn’t mean you can’t keep the growing going on indoors, and no dedicated greenhouse is needed, either. With a self-watering hydroponic growing system like a Gardyn Studio 2, you can grow full-sized plants indoors, including the same flowers and edible plants you’d place in a raised garden bed outdoors. If indoor space is at a premium, you can also use a smaller countertop system like a LetPot that can be used with a smaller number of flowers or food plants.

All those leaves you painstakingly rake or blow into a pile need not be bagged up and sent off to the dump; you can recycle it all to make your own mulch, which can preserve and nurture your garden throughout the winter. It’s best to use a leaf shredder to break down fallen leaves into smaller bits, rather than simply spreading collected leaves out atop your beds. You can also use a lawn mower to break down leaves, though this will dull the blade with extended use. If you have a wood chipper, you can use branches and brambles to make mulch as well. Even grass clippings can make great mulch, provided you dry it in the sun first and mix it with some thicker material, like those shredded leaves or chipped branches.

You don’t have to remove every dead flower, be it a truly finished annual or a dormant perennial, says Stembel, of Farmgirl Flowers. “One thing I’m trying to get better at is not cleaning everything up just because it looks messy. There are flowers that went from stunning to eyesores everywhere, seed heads, and so many perennials that are better off being left alone for a while so the foliage can die back naturally and the plants can store energy for next year. A lot of those seed heads and stems also feed birds and give beneficial insects places to overwinter.” Sometimes, the best thing you can do to winterize your garden is to leave it alone.

You should plant bulbs in the autumn when soil temperatures are consistently holding at around 55 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, and about six weeks before you expect the first frost. “[It’s] my absolute favorite fall ‘chore,’ planting the bulbs,” says Stembel. “This fall we’re planting thousands of daffodils, tulips, and drumstick alliums around the farm. I know thousands sounds excessive [but] bulbs might be my favorite kind of delayed gratification. By spring, I’ll mostly have forgotten that we trudged outside on cold, rainy fall days and dug what felt like endless trenches to plant all of them until the little green sprouts start popping through the soil. Those first flowers bring us so much joy right when we need it most [after] months of cold, gray weather. In fall, I wonder why we planted so many, but by spring I’ll wonder why we didn’t plant more.”

CONSIDERING I RAN a 100-mile ultramarathon, my body held up surprisingly well. I didn't get a single blister, which says a lot about the socks and trail shoes I was wearing and about staying on top of foot care throughout the race.

My biggest issue was my right knee. I started experiencing pain around Hope Pass, somewhere around mile 45. Luckily, I had packed a knee sleeve in my drop bag at the next major aid station around mile 50, and getting that on made a huge difference.

Outside of the knee pain, my body stayed tough through all 100 miles. I definitely hurt, but I never reached a point where I felt like I was completely breaking down. I’m gonna give a rundown of how I kept fresh on race day as well as how I’ve been recovering since I crossed that finish line of the Leadville 100 back in August.

I used a handful of different tools at aid stations throughout the race, but the ones I relied on most were a knee sleeve, a Hyperice massage gun, anti-chafe ointment and spray, and Biofreeze.

The leg sleeve became especially important because I had been dealing with some lingering pain in my right knee from a previous 50-miler. I used the Flytex leg compression sleeve. The compression helped the knee feel more secure and manageable as the miles added up.

Anti-chafe ointment was another must. I used Skin Slick and Body Glide. I stayed on top of lubricating my feet and toes throughout the race, and somehow made it through all 100 miles without a single blister—the Hoka Tecton X 4 had a big part to do with that as well.

Finally, Biofreeze became one of my go-to tools once my knee started bothering me around mile 42. I knew that if I wanted to make it another 58 miles, I needed to stay ahead of the discomfort rather than wait for it to become unbearable.

I was pretty banged up the day I finished, but actually not as destroyed as I expected to be. I could still walk, which definitely wasn't the case for everyone crossing the finish line.

My quads, hips, and feet were incredibly tight, but my feet probably hurt the most. Being on them for more than 28 hours takes a serious toll.

My parents and crew were amazing. They got me whatever I needed, kept me hydrated, and basically made sure I didn't have to do anything except recover.

The rest of the day was pretty simple: I stayed in bed with my Hyperice compression boots on, drank multiple Strives to replenish fluids and electrolytes, and eventually crushed an entire large pizza. After consuming gels, waffles, and race “food” for more than a day, getting a big meal with plenty of carbohydrates and calories back into my body felt incredible.

I also took magnesium before bed. Sleep can be surprisingly difficult after an ultra because your body is still so wired from the effort. Even though you're completely exhausted, it can take time for your system to settle back down. I experienced everything from chills to sweating after the race, so that first day was really about letting my body come back to normal.

It took me about four weeks before I truly felt fully recovered from Leadville.

My biggest priorities were sleep, nutrition, and giving my body time to recover. I aimed for at least eight hours of sleep every night and tried to sauna around five days a week because I found the heat relaxing and helpful for how my muscles felt.

I also used my Hyperice compression boots constantly. They became one of my favorite recovery tools throughout the entire training process, and I continued using them after the race to help my quads, hamstrings, and calves feel less stiff.

My feet needed a lot of attention too. I rolled them out every morning and again before bed. Nutrition was another major focus: lots of whole foods, enough calories, and plenty of protein to support recovery.

The biggest lesson was that just because your legs start feeling normal again doesn't necessarily mean you're fully recovered. My muscles bounced back relatively quickly, but my overall energy and sleep took much longer.

My coach told me to take two full weeks off from running. Naturally, I made it about six days.

I ran roughly six miles the Friday after Leadville, and shockingly, my legs felt great. Muscularly, I felt almost back to normal, which was wild considering I had just run 100 miles less than a week earlier.

But that was a little misleading. While my legs felt good, my sleep was still off and my overall energy levels were way down. Those didn't fully return to normal for about four weeks.

Then, three weeks after Leadville, I ran 30 miles around New York City for my 30th birthday. It was an incredible day with friends, and I wouldn't trade the experience for anything—but in hindsight, my coach was probably right about taking recovery more seriously. I aggravated my right knee during that run and have been dealing with it ever since. I've now had to take the past couple of weeks off from running.

If anything, it taught me that there's a major difference between feeling recovered and actually being recovered after a 100-mile race.

Recovery was a huge part of my Leadville training, especially as the mileage increased. The two things I consistently came back to were sauna and compression boots.

I found sauna sessions especially helpful for relaxing after long training days and improving my sleep. When you're stacking big mileage week after week, getting quality sleep becomes just as important as the training itself.

The Hyperice compression boots were probably my most used recovery tool. I used them roughly every other day during heavier training blocks to help my legs feel fresher between sessions. When you're running high mileage while still trying to strength train, anything that helps you recover for the next day's work becomes incredibly valuable.

#763. Honey bees are bullying other bees, scientists discover

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Honey bees are proving to be formidable competitors for food in the UK, and scientists have discovered that they frequently use aggression to drive other pollinators away from flowers. New research has documented how honey bees chase, displace, and intimidate rival insects, providing the first detailed evidence of how extensively they interfere with other species' access to food.

The study, conducted by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) and published in Ecological Entomology, followed pollinator activity over two years at five locations in and around Cambridge, England. Each site was situated within 300 meters of an established honey bee colony.

Researchers observed insects visiting bramble (Rubus fruticosus) and ivy (Hedera helix), two flowering plants that provide important supplies of nectar and pollen. Both attract large numbers of insects during their flowering periods, making them useful places to investigate competition for food.

Honey Bees Dominate the Competition for Flowers

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

#870. Scientists discover ghostly, eyeless creature hidden in Chinese cave

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A newly identified freshwater cave snail in China is offering scientists a rare glimpse into the little-known aquatic ecosystems hidden beneath East Asia. Published in the journal Subterranean Biology, the discovery represents an important step forward in understanding the region's underground wildlife. Until now, no freshwater cave snails had been documented in China, and the country's subterranean freshwater mollusks had received relatively little scientific attention.

The newly discovered species, Erhaia dijiang, has evolved remarkable features that allow it to live in complete darkness. As a true cave-dwelling animal, it has entirely lost its eyes, with the structures normally associated with vision having fully degenerated. The tiny snail also has a cone-shaped, nearly transparent shell, a delicate body without pigmentation, and long, slender tentacles.

Its unusual appearance inspired a name drawn from ancient Chinese mythology. Researchers chose dijiang in honor of "Di Jiang," a mountain patron god portrayed as a faceless beast in the ancient text Classic of Mountains and Seas (山海经). The connection is particularly fitting because the snail is extremely sensitive to light. During laboratory observations, individuals consistently retreated into dark spaces beneath rocks whenever they were exposed to illumination.

The location of the discovery is just as remarkable as the animal itself. Scientists found Erhaia dijiang at an elevation of 2,062 meters, making it the highest-altitude cave snail yet recorded within the family Erhaiidae. The finding demonstrates that freshwater cave snails can inhabit limestone cave systems at elevations above 2,000 meters.

Life in the Dark Waters of a Mountain Cave

Within its underground habitat, Erhaia dijiang lives among submerged gravel and broken rock in water less than one meter deep. The cave's water temperature stays close to 20°C, providing a relatively stable environment for the tiny mollusks.

Even when heavy seasonal rains increase the flow of water and occasionally wash a few snails out through the cave opening, they have not been observed in nearby streams. Researchers found that the animals remain confined to the cave environment, staying within two meters of its entrance.

Laboratory observations also provided clues about what these elusive creatures eat. The snails were seen feeding on diatoms, microscopic algae that grow on rock surfaces, and they also consumed fish food supplied by the researchers.

Only Three Living Snails Found, Raising Conservation Concerns

Although the discovery sheds new light on China's largely unexplored underground aquatic biodiversity, researchers are concerned about the species' future. They describe its conservation outlook as "far from optimistic."

During the first field investigation in August 2025, the team encountered an exceptionally small number of snails. Despite searching the shallow cave waters, researchers located only three living adults and one empty shell. They found no juveniles, suggesting that the known population is extremely sparse.

The scientists are now urging more comprehensive investigations of cave ecosystems throughout China to uncover additional species and better understand these fragile underground habitats. They also emphasize the need to develop conservation protections quickly for these vulnerable mollusks, which are native to highly restricted environments and could face serious threats before their populations are fully understood.

#5266. Scientists discover a way to kill dangerous gut bacteria without killing the good

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A dangerous intestinal infection may soon have a more targeted treatment option. Scientists have identified a natural compound that can kill the bacteria responsible for severe gut infections while leaving much of the beneficial gut microbiome unharmed.

The bacterium Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is naturally found in the intestines of many people and generally causes no problems when present in small amounts. However, antibiotics that target a wide range of bacteria can upset the delicate balance of microorganisms in the gut. This disruption allows C. difficile to multiply rapidly, potentially causing severe intestinal inflammation that can become chronic.

C. difficile infections (CDI) are among the leading causes of diarrhea associated with antibiotic treatment. The bacteria produce toxins that can trigger dangerous and sometimes life-threatening inflammation in the intestines. Because these infections frequently return after treatment, scientists are searching for more effective ways to eliminate the pathogen without causing additional damage to the gut microbiome.

A team led by Prof. Rolf Müller, department head and scientific director at HIPS, has now identified a promising drug candidate that specifically targets C. difficile while preserving much of the gut's beneficial microbial community. HIPS is a site of the Helmholtz Centre for Infection Research (HZI) that operates in collaboration with Saarland University.

Natural Compound Targets Dangerous Gut Bacteria

The researchers found that argyrin B, a naturally occurring antibacterial substance, was highly effective against C. difficile in laboratory tests and experiments involving infected mice. Importantly, the compound showed little activity against the beneficial microorganisms that help maintain a healthy digestive system.

"We were able to show that the natural compound argyrin B is highly effective against CDI: both in laboratory experiments and in a mouse model of the infection, where it significantly reduces the bacterial load," says Dr. Jennifer Herrmann, senior scientist in the department "Microbial Natural Products" at HIPS. "An antibacterial target of the argyrins had previously been identified and characterized in the notorious hospital-acquired pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Now we have been able to show that argyrin B also acts on this target in C. difficile and exhibits particularly high efficacy in doing so. In contrast, the argyrins have virtually no effect on the beneficial microorganisms in our digestive tract. The gut microbiome could therefore be spared during treatment with argyrin and restored to a healthy state more quickly."

A Bacteria-Killing Substance Found in Soil

Argyrins were originally discovered in myxobacteria, microorganisms that inhabit soil. These bacteria produce natural substances that help them attack other microbes and use them as a source of food.

Although argyrin B has demonstrated promising antibacterial activity, researchers must also determine whether its pharmaceutical properties make it suitable for use as a medicine. That includes understanding how the compound behaves inside the body and whether it could cause unwanted effects.

One particularly encouraging finding emerged when the team examined what happens after mice receive argyrin B orally. Very little of the compound entered their bloodstream. Instead, most of it remained in the large intestine, where C. difficile infections occur.

By concentrating in the gut rather than circulating throughout the body, argyrin B could potentially reduce the risk of side effects affecting other organs. Together with its selective activity against C. difficile, this characteristic makes the compound a promising candidate for a treatment that may be better tolerated.

Could It Help Prevent Recurring Gut Infections?

One of the biggest challenges in treating C. difficile is its tendency to return. Patients who experience multiple relapses are often treated using fecal microbiota transfer (commonly known as a stool transplant). This procedure aims to restore a healthier balance of gut microorganisms, but it can be complicated to perform and unpleasant for patients.

A medication developed from argyrin could eventually provide another treatment option. By eliminating the harmful bacteria without extensively disrupting the rest of the microbiome, such a therapy might reduce the likelihood of further infections and relapses. That could also help some patients avoid the need for stool transplants.

More research is needed before argyrin B can be considered for clinical use. The scientists plan to investigate which dosage produces the strongest antibacterial effect and use additional experimental models to determine whether the compound can also combat recurring C. difficile infections.
