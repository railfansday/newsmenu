Michael Saylor, the executive chairman of the world's largest Bitcoin corporate holder, Strategy, has countered a viral argument against buying Bitcoin in a post issued today.

#2572. Sports club members becoming less tolerant of homosexuality

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

See more DutchNews articles in your Google search results

See more DutchNews articles in your Google search results

Acceptance of homosexuality in Dutch sports clubs has declined significantly in the last generation, according to a survey of attitudes by the Mulier Institute.

The organisation found that 66% of people had no objection to a fellow member of a sports club being gay, while in 2008 the figure was 85%.

Just over half (55%) of respondents said they had no problem sharing a shower with a gay team-mate of the same sex, which also represented a drop from 73% in 2008.

The Mulier Institute said the findings reflected a social trend that has also been seen in schools, where the proportion of boys aged between 12 and 16 with a positive view of homosexuality has dropped from 40% to 22% since 2021.

The Dutch Olympic Committee NOC*NSF said the findings were “highly regrettable” and pledged to step up its efforts to make sport more inclusive.

But the John Blankenstein Foundation, which campaigns for LGBT emancipation in sport, said it had noticed declining enthusiasm for its workshops and growing numbers of parents who voiced objections.

Karin Blankenstein, who set up the organisation in 2008, told NOS: “I get the sensitivity around transgender people in sport at the top level, but this is about amateur sport.”

#5370. Korean police book 80 Tesla drivers over illegal FSD unlock devices

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Seoul police have booked 80 people for using aftermarket devices to switch on Tesla’s “Full Self-Driving” in cars that aren’t allowed to run it in South Korea. Officers also seized 21 of the devices.

The Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency announced the case on Tuesday. It’s the largest enforcement action yet against the FSD “jailbreak” hardware that has spread through China, Europe, and Korea over the past year.

Police say the suspects either imported the unlock modules from Europe and China or made their own. One plugged a European-made unit into his own Tesla. Another, an IT worker, built his device with information he found on GitHub.

Suspects also swapped advice online on how to unlock FSD and how to stop the car from keeping any record that it had been activated.

They’re charged under Korea’s Motor Vehicle Management Act, which bans unauthorized software changes that can affect a vehicle’s safety. The penalty is up to two years in prison or a fine of up to 20 million won (about $15,000).

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport asked police to look into it back in April after finding signs of unauthorized activation. A police official told Korea JoongAng Daily that “safety risks were confirmed,” including the possibility of FSD misreading driving conditions. The investigation is ongoing, with police consulting the ministry and the Korea Transportation Safety Authority on the technical side.

Tesla launched FSD (Supervised) in South Korea in late 2025, but only on US-built vehicles: Model S, Model X, and Cybertruck. Those cars are exempt from Korean safety certification under the Korea-US free trade agreement. The Model 3 and Model Y, which Tesla ships to Korea from Giga Shanghai, haven’t cleared Korea’s safety standards for the feature.

And those two models are the bulk of the fleet. Citing Carisyou Data Lab, Korea JoongAng Daily reports that 47,458 of the 229,311 Teslas on Korean roads as of August, or 20.7%, can legally use FSD.

That number doesn’t match the government’s own count from a few months earlier. In May, data released by lawmaker Park Yong-gap showed just 4,292 of 180,684 registered Teslas (2.4%) were eligible: 2,708 Model X, 1,193 Model S, and 391 Cybertruck. Either way, the vast majority of Korean Tesla owners can’t legally use FSD, and some of them paid over $6,000 for it anyway.

The hardware is the same, which is why the hack works. The modules plug into the car’s CAN bus and get around the regional software lock.

Tesla has been fighting the devices itself. In April, it started remotely detecting them and stripping FSD from affected cars, warned owners they’d be fully liable for any crash and could lose warranty coverage, and permanently banned some Chinese owners from FSD.

It’s easy to see why people do this. Picture a Korean owner of a Shanghai-built Model Y who paid for FSD, watching the Model X next door run the same software on the same computer. That’s frustrating.

Tesla sold FSD to owners of cars it had no regulatory path to enable in Korea, the same way it sold “Full Self-Driving” in Europe for years while approval kept slipping. It only dropped the name there this month after Germany pushed back. Sell a feature people can’t legally use and you create the market for the jailbreak.

Korea has now shown it will actually prosecute. So will Tesla refund the Korean owners who bought a feature their cars may never be allowed to run?

#4485. Why are bogs so good at preserving dead bodies?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 3:43 PM EDT

In August of 1984, commercial peat cutters working near Wilmslow, about a half-hour drive south of Manchester, England, discovered a dead body in the moss. The corpse was determined to be a 25-year-old man with a closely shorn beard and well-manicured nails. He had suffered two or three heavy blows to the head, and had sustained a cut throat and a fractured rib. But this wasn’t a fresh corpse: His body had been laying in that peat bog for nearly 2,000 years.

The “Lindow Man” is just one of at least 2,000 bog bodies recovered from Europe’s peatlands. They date from the Mesolithic period, which ended in Europe between 8,500 to 5,500 years ago, all the way up through World War II.

“Bog bodies” are essentially human corpses that peat bogs have naturally preserved and mummified. Quite often, their skin, hair, nails, and internal organs are remarkably intact for thousands of years. Their bones, however, are a whole other story.

To find out more about why bogs are so good at preserving bodies, Popular Science turned to author Anna North, a journalist whose fascination with peat bogs and preserved “bog bodies” inspired her national bestseller Bog Queen. This eco-historical mystery novel details what happens when a forensic anthropologist starts investigating a well-preserved Iron Age bog body, and required extensive research on North’s part. The author first got the idea for her book after coming across the Lindow Man at London’s British Museum, where he’s on permanent display.

“He doesn’t look great,” she says, “but he’s just so recognizably still a human.” The fact that North was sharing a room with this person who ate, drank, and walked the earth 2,000 years ago left an indelible mark on her. She wanted to learn more. “One of the first things I realized is that I didn’t have a good sense of what a peat bog would even look like.”

A bog or “peat bog” is an acidic, nutrient-poor wetland ecosystem. It’s made up of peat, which is ground that’s soft and spongy and consists of partially decayed plant material that builds up over hundreds or thousands of years.

“Peat is really any kind of organic matter that builds up faster than it decays,” says North. It can be grasses, reeds, roots, and shrubs. But peat mostly consists of Sphagnum moss, a large group of shaggy-looking, moisture-loving mosses.

“Sphagnum moss has the capacity to take in a lot of water and store it, so it becomes really spongy and waterlogged.” This creates an excellent environment for preservation, says North, “because there’s not much air.”

While bogs exist across the globe, you’ll find them mostly in cool, northern climates with lots of rain. For years, these mysterious landscapes have appeared as ominous settings in literature. They’re especially popular in Victorian detective fiction and gothic novels like Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s The Hounds of the Baskervilles and Daphne du Maurier’s Jamaica Inn. And for good reason. Their mist-shrouded surfaces (a result of the cool, damp air that sits above their spongy terrain), unstable footing, and stagnant waters make them natural backdrops for the unknown. Of course, there are also the bodies.

The preservation of bog bodies results from a highly-detailed balance of water source, chemistry, and vegetation.

Bogs get almost all of their water from rain and snow, which makes them low in minerals and nutrients. The water also doesn’t move thanks to the bog’s sponge-like moss.

Thick layers of decomposed organic material choke off the air supply to the decaying plant matter underneath. Without oxygen, the environment becomes highly acidic and aerobic bacteria and fungi are unable to thrive. This means they can’t break down that dead plant (and human) material the way they normally would.

Instead, the bog’s high acidity essentially “pickles” human corpses, many of which were left behind as part of a ritual sacrifice or the result of executions. The environment’s low pH (the result of its high acidity) acts like a pickle juice brine, stopping harmful bacteria from growing and preserving bodies almost like “living time capsules.” Sphagnum moss also releases a complex carbohydrate that binds to the proteins in human skin and tissue, called sphagnan. This carbohydrate turns the skin leathery and a dark-brown “tea” color while protecting it against decay. But while this unique environment slows a body’s decomposition, the bog’s intense acid leaches the calcium right out of human bones.

“If you look at X-rays of bog bodies, they’re not going to look like an X-ray of a living person,” says North. Mostly because the bones are softened, warped, or have dissolved completely.

Raised bogs, which are deep, dome-shaped peatlands that are both wetter and higher than their surrounding lands, are best at preserving bodies. That’s because they’re both highly acidic and entirely void of oxygen.

The shallower peat found in blanket bogs means that bodies decompose faster (though bog bodies are still found here). Fens, which are peat-accumulating wetlands that both  surface water and mineral-rich groundwater replenish, are less acidic. This means that a body’s soft-tissue will typically decay.

One way that forensic anthropologists can typically tell the difference between a recently decreased person and a bog body is through their teeth. While researching for her book, North discovered that people who lived and died before there was refined flour would have really different patterns of tooth decay than modern-day eaters.

Back then, she says, “They might actually grind wheat or corn with stone, and little bits of stone would end up in their flour. Those people would then chew on that stone and it would grind down their teeth.”

North also learned that identifying a bog body is a collaborative effort. A bioarchaeologist, who studies human biological remains at archaeological sites, might be brought in to determine the corpse’s age and sex, while a pathologist would examine the cause of death.

“It turns out that the same process that makes bogs really good at preserving a body also means that they’re storing this enormous amount of carbon,” says North.

Since there’s no oxygen to break down a bog’s organic plant matter, it just piles up layer by layer forming thick, carbon-rich peat. In fact, peatlands (including bogs) only cover roughly three-perfect of the planet’s land surface, though they store twice as much carbon as all the world’s forests combined. “They’re just a really powerful resource.”

Bogs are generally safe to walk across as well, despite their reputation for being “graveyards” of sorts. “There are cases of people appearing to have accidentally died on bogs,” says North. “Still, if you’re an average person visiting a bog today and you stay on the marked trail, I am not concerned for your safety.”

War in Sudan has led to the worst humanitarian crisis with more than 33 million people in need of assistance according to the WHO

The US and 30 other Western states have warned of an "alarming deterioration" in food security in Sudan, urging the warring sides in the civil war to remove obstacles to humanitarian aid to prevent famine.

In a joint statement, the signatories said conflict was the main driver of Sudan's hunger crisis, damaging critical infrastructure and disrupting food production.

"There is very little to survive on," Yusuf Roble, regional director for Islamic Relief East Africa, told BBC Newsday, describing how people returning to Omdurman were finding their homes destroyed or looted.

The war, which began in April 2023, has displaced 14 million people.

The statement, external was signed by EU states, the UK, Canada, Switzerland and Norway, as well as the US, where it was published by the state department on Monday after first appearing on the European Civil Protection and Humanitarian Aid Operations website.

It estimates that 19.5 million people or 41% of Sudan's population are experiencing high levels of acute food insecurity, including five million facing emergency levels of hunger.

"I met a mother of seven children who doesn't really know the whereabouts of her husband for the last three and a half years and he was the breadwinner, the sole breadwinner for the family," Roble said.

While the war in Sudan has led to what the World Health Organization has described as the world's worst humanitarian crisis, Roble said only 40% of Sudan's 2026 humanitarian needs plan had been funded as of September.

"Their meals come from the community kitchen, which doesn't really even have very long-term support because of funding cuts," Roble added.

The joint statement warns that "the number of children suffering from severe acute malnutrition could exceed the projected 825,000" without immediate action.

The crisis is being exacerbated by what the signatories describe as systematic obstruction of aid and the effects of El NiÃ±o, which has reduced rainfall and increased drought across more than half of Sudan.

"The parties to the conflict bear primary responsibility for alleviating the suffering of the Sudanese people," the statement says, urging them to lift all impediments to humanitarian assistance and allow full, safe, rapid and unimpeded access.

In June, the UN-backed Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, a measure of the severity and magnitude of food crises, estimated that 5.1 million people in Sudan faced severe hunger.

The civil war in Sudan began in April 2023 following a power struggle between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces.
