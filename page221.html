Brown, when introduced by the 76ers in August, expressed disappointment with how the Celtics handled the trade and said "not really" when asked whether he and Tatum had spoken since the deal.

"We won a championship together," Brown said. "Anybody that you win a championship with, you would think, like, that's what a brotherhood or a lifelong legacy would entail. #4784. Terry Bradshaw Will Never Tell You He’s Retiring

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The 78-year-old NFL legend opens up about depression, cancer, and why he has no interest in a farewell tribute.

TERRY BRADSHAW IS feeling achy today. But it’s not because of his 14-year NFL career, his 40-plus-year career as a broadcaster, or the daily work of manning his Texas ranch. No, what’s really been wearing him out is all the autographs he’s been signing.

“I signed 4,300 stickers in two weeks. I've got 2,500 more sent to me, and I've signed 750 since last night and this morning,” Bradshaw tells Men’s Health. “My finger on my index hand is turning to the left.”

Unfortunately for Bradshaw, people want your signature when you’re a Hall of Famer, stage performer, Fox Sports analyst, and—once again—author. Looking for Terry, Bradshaw’s second memoir, arrived earlier this month, 25 years after his last book. And the 78-year-old has experienced plenty in that span.

He got married (for a fourth time) to his longtime partner Tammy in 2014, survived two cancer diagnoses and even launched his own bourbon. Bradshaw’s book also gets into his struggles with depression and his “dumb” public image—which has followed him since his playing days.

“If you think you know me, you really don’t,” he says. But as the curtain begins to close on his broadcasting career, Bradshaw isn’t interested in taking a victory lap.

The Steelers legend told us that he recently signed a two-year extension with Fox, but expects to be replaced after the network shows the Super Bowl in 2029. He’s hoping that there’s no fancy send-off—not that he expects that wish to be honored.

“I don’t go for tributes,” he says. “I don’t need that in my life.”

For now, Bradshaw remains in the public eye, and he was happy to talk through his new book after a much-needed dose of rainfall to his farm.

MEN’S HEALTH: This is your first memoir since 2001. Why was now the time to put out another one?

TERRY BRADSHAW: I enjoy doing books, unlike probably a lot of people. The first couple of books were fine. The last three I've really enjoyed doing [with coauthor David Fisher].

You kind of park the vanity a little bit, and you say to yourself, "You know what? I’ve got a few things that have happened to me in the last 20-plus years. I'd like to share it with people.” And I'm thinking those that buy the book will learn from the book. Those dealing with depression, those who have had failed marriages… As my wife says, I'm an open book.

MH: A main theme of the book is opening up, and how there’s been this perception of you that isn’t accurate. What’s the biggest way people misunderstand you?

TB: That's my fault. Look, first of all, I know what this question is [laughs]. It's my fault, because I play into the dumb image. You want to have a shocker here? I enjoy it! It's fun for me.

I choose to do hardline football analysis, but with a smile on my face, and I try to entertain. And maybe sometimes I try too hard to entertain. Sometimes you can go too far, you can definitely be too much, and people get tired of that. So I measure my steps, I kind of let things come to me.

MH: You wrote about endearing yourself to people with humor, and how you want them to have good interactions with you. How much of a personal responsibility do you feel to make other people happy?

TB: It's of paramount importance. I hate to use the word, Jake, celebrities. I enjoy people. I enjoy making them smile. I enjoy making their day, because a lot of times, giving an autograph and taking a picture in an airport makes their day.

To tell you the truth, too many celebrities value themselves a little bit too highly. When we're all stripped of our titles and the movies and the songs we've written, the Super Bowls we won—we’re all just people.

MH: You’ve also talked at length about your experience with depression. How important was it for you to be known as someone who spoke about mental health?

TB: The depression [diagnosis] explained all the dark, dark, dark times, even now still, that I go through and experience. And you go through all the times, what started it, what kicked it in. Divorce will kick it in, injury will kick it in, things that are important to you'll kick it. Plus, let's be honest, I take medication, and even with the medication, I still will [sometimes] start sinking.

You have no idea how big a difference Tammy has made. Because if I didn't have her, I'd be by myself. I'd drink too much, I'd get drunk, or I’d get sad, and I'd buy a horse, or I'd purchase something. I was always looking for some fix, and now that's not the case. But to answer your question, I'm not afraid to share my illness or my weaknesses—as some people would want to say it is, but it isn’t a weakness, it's an illness—with other people.

I know I’ve made a difference in men's lives because I have them come up to me. They come up quietly. It's mainly in a grocery store, and I'll be going down the aisle shopping, and they'll slip over to me [quietly], "Hey man, I know about your depression. It got me to a doctor and my wife, and I really want to thank you for sharing all that.”

MH: How difficult was it to accept help for it?

TB: Well, like I told you earlier, events in your life will trigger it. I had gone through a divorce, and I had two little girls. That sent me in a hole because I felt like I lost my kids.

I was in a hole for almost two years. The emotional drag that it had on my life got to a point where it was unbearable, and that's when I decided, I said I gotta figure out what's going on here.

MH: Regarding your cancer diagnoses (which came in 2021 and 2022), you wrote that you weren’t actually sure if surviving skin and bladder cancer made you appreciate life more. Has your stance changed on that?

TB: Getting a report you had cancer was like: “Alright, there's a challenge.” But it didn't scare me. Of course, I thought about it. I didn't understand how serious bladder cancer was. My approach to it was always this way: as a Christian, if I die, I'm going to heaven. So, I'm good. I don't want to die, and I don't know how good I'm going to be at dying.

Let me tell you this: the hardest thing about cancer for me is going back for the checkups and waiting for the call that says you're okay. Now you’ve got another six months before you do it again. But you're okay. And everybody I talk to that are cancer-free, they all say the same thing. The scariest thing now is, “Oh man, going back, what's the report gonna be?”

MH: There’s a feeling you describe in the book of having to constantly prove yourself on the field. Do you feel more confident now as a broadcaster than you did at the end of your playing career?

TB: Oh, I am totally confident on the air, absolutely. Supremely confident. I think I'm going through now what everybody that's in their 70s goes through. I'm 78. I just signed a two-year extension, and I'm pretty sure no matter how well I perform over the next two years, they're going to want to replace me.

I’m not going to announce my retirement, because in two years when we do the Super Bowl, after my contract is expiring, I don't want anybody to do a tribute. So I'm never going to say I'm going to retire. Because, look, they may say, "Hey, you're still doing a good job. We'll sign you for another year.” That could easily happen. They're pissing into the wind if they think I’m gonna tell them I’m retiring.

TB: What if I say, “I don't know why you're doing that for, I'm not retiring.” But I mean, I'm not going anywhere either. What am I going to do, at 80? Go to ESPN? [laughs].

MH: When you reflect on your post-playing career, is there anything you regret having not done?

TB: I’m afraid of heights. I used to be afraid of flying. I’m not a daredevil. I will not paraglide, parasail, whatever you call it. I'm not jumping out of a plane. I'm not snorkeling. I'm not going underwater with a tank of air. Sharks are down there, they will bite you. I don't go in the water [laughs]. I live a really… it's not an uneventful life, certainly, nowhere near. But everything in the entertainment world, I have done.

MH: What does a successful last couple of years at Fox look like to you? Just continuing to make people smile?

TB: Absolutely. Keep entertaining, give strong opinions when I feel it.

I have another book in me, and I'll say more in the next book. I'm already planning it. It won't be a tell-all book. But it's a strong opinion book.

#4696. The 5-Second Rule is scientifically false

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 4:07 PM EDT

Since elementary school, we’ve all been taught the “5-Second Rule.” The idea is simple: Food dropped on the floor is still perfectly edible if retrieved in under five seconds. The logic behind the theory, such as it is, is that germs and dirt and pet dander are somehow caught by surprise by the sudden arrival of food and need a few seconds to get their bearings. If you snatch the food up quick enough, they won’t have time to get it “dirty.”

Even as kids, we were skeptical but admit it–you still roll the dice on it every now and again. It’s like shouting “shotgun” before getting into the passenger seat even if it’s only you and the driver. It’s instinctual. But is it true?

“Let’s go with ‘No,’ there is no truth to the ‘5-second rule’,” says Gillson Sciences Director of Operations Erin Thane. “But I would argue that specifics matter.”

As Thane explains, variables such as comparative wetness of said food and type of flooring can swing things one way or another. “Food dropped on a carpet will likely pick up less contamination than food dropped on a smooth surface with the same microbial loads. Sticky or wet foods will likely pick up more contamination than dry foods dropped on the same contaminated surfaces.”

And that’s not all. “There are actually a lot of nuances to this question,” says Thane. “Is it the floor of Windsor Castle or a public bathroom? Was sticky gum dropped and then put back in the mouth, or was it a goldfish cracker? Am I a healthy adult eating something off the ground or an immunocompromised individual?”

Perhaps more surprising, according to Thane, is that the floors in your home and floors in public spaces actually don’t vary in cleanliness as much as you’d like to think. As Thane puts it, “We all wear the same shoes–or socks hopefully in the home–across the floors we encounter daily, like the kitchen and bathroom, to other areas or spaces. We are constantly picking up hitchhiking germs and depositing them from one floor to the next.”

Specifically skin-inhabiting bacteria such as Staphylococcus (which puts the “Staph” in “Staph Infection”) and Corynebacterium (which can cause diphtheria) to gut bacteria such as Bacteroides. Shoes, clothing, and bags can also carry Bacillus or mold or yeast that can cause nausea and vomiting.

While there are mitigating factors, it’s safe to say that in terms of grossness, the “5-Second Rule” is pretty high up there.

“I would certainly stay away from any wet, moist or sticky foods which act like a sponge and will immediately cling to any and all dirt, debris and contamination that it comes into contact with,” advises Thane. “However, after everything we have discussed so far, I refer back to the best practice or rule of thumb which is to just not eat any food off the floor.”

#4805. Behind the Black Box Summit: The Event That Foreshadowed CrossFit’s Explosive Future

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This exclusive excerpt from The Glass Box documents the early days of CrossFit—and its conflicts.

The following is an excerpt from The Glass Box: The Shocking True Story of How CrossFit Broke the Rules, Made Millions, and Changed Fitness Forever, a new book about CrossFit’s history that features reporting and interviews with the people involved, including Greg Glassman.

AT 1:45 PM on Saturday, November 21, 2009, a few dozen trim, healthy-looking men and women in their twenties and thirties were gradually returning to the aluminum folding chairs that had been arranged in the middle of CrossFit Central, having just finished a light lunch provided by the local Austin health-food chain My Fit Foods. A roughly five-hundred square-foot gym housed in a former body shop on Burnet Road in Brentwood, a flat, oak-lined neighborhood a few miles north-northwest of the Texas capital, CrossFit Central was founded in 2006 by Carey Kepler and Jeremy Thiel, siblings and former collegiate athletes who had been running boot camps in parks around the city before opening a place of their own. The air was mild but the slate-gray sky looked ominous—like a storm was coming.

Most of the people present were themselves gym owners operating CrossFit gyms—known in the business as “boxes”—in cities all around the country. Impressively muscled and swathed in athleisure, they had come to Austin at Thiel’s invitation to attend what had been dubbed the Black Box Summit—a three-day gathering of CrossFitters intended “for affiliates, by affiliates,” in the program’s official description. It was like an Apple keynote with an audience of Arnold Schwarzeneggers.

Thiel had put together the weekend with Dutch Lowy, a fellow Texan who owned a CrossFit box across from Texas A&M University in College Station. The event was inspired by CrossFit’s weekend seminars—conducted by CrossFit founder Greg Glassman and held at the original CrossFit box in Santa Cruz, California—which certified coaches in the methodology. The idea was to embrace the brand’s “open source” philosophy by pooling resources and sharing information, like a fitness-based meeting of the minds. “It was all centered around the CrossFit mentality of finding the best people, bringing them together, and figuring out what we can do to make this better,” Lowy explained. “That was the way CrossFit was initially developed: Take from the best in the business, develop a program that’s superior in every way, and always be changing.”

The desire to gather experts and showcase their unique expertise was one motivation for organizing the summit. The other was that CrossFit gym owners desperately needed help. Glassman was a dyed-in-the-wool libertarian with ties to the Cato Institute, the free-market think tank founded by Charles Koch. In business, he favored the “least rents” model in which a company would claim “a shrinking slice of the pie in a rapidly growing ecosystem.” CrossFit, Inc., charged prospective gym owners only a nominal fee for the rights to use the CrossFit name—just $500 in the early years—which Glassman boasted was a fraction of the capital required to open a Planet Fitness. But in exchange for this miniscule payment, box owners got nothing: no merchandising, no marketing materials, no business plan. Other than having a link on the CrossFit website, these budding entrepreneurs were on their own.

Granted, most people involved in CrossFit in those days appreciated the hands-off approach, at least conceptually. The price was so low that it was easy for boxes to justify, and the informality only added to the brand’s laid-back, renegade air. “There were these affiliates popping up all over the place, and there was really no regulation on what was going on,” said Michael Rutherford, a strength coach from Kansas City who was one of the summit’s plenary speakers. “It was like the old mining days in Leadville, Colorado. People were going out and sticking a flag in the ground and—boom— they were CrossFitting.” At the same time, the total absence of support meant that box owners—many of them former coaches without any real experience running a business—were struggling with standard operating procedures and best practices. It was a crisis that the Black Box Summit was designed to solve. “The original agenda was let’s help people get the answers they’re not getting as a CrossFit affiliate,” Rutherford said. If CrossFit wasn’t interested in providing this kind of information, they would have to provide it to themselves.

It was shortly before the summit, in mid-November, that Thiel arranged a call with the weekend’s speakers, including Rutherford and Lowy, and the box owners James FitzGerald, Greg Everett, Aimee Anaya, and Robb Wolf. He let them know that he’d called Glassman as a courtesy, to extend an invitation and to reassure him that they weren’t trying to steal any of CrossFit’s thunder. Glassman hadn’t seemed to have taken the news so well. “We’ll be there, because we have to keep an eye on what’s going on, not because this is fantastic and we want to be involved” was the gist of the response, in Lowy’s recollection. “It was very standoffish. Us against them,” Lowy said. Or as Rutherford put it, “CrossFit was clearly offended that this was going on. As if we were going to hijack the whole company and take everybody away.”

Everett was not surprised that Glassman was distressed, because this attitude was exactly what he had come to expect of him. Co-owner of the fourth-ever CrossFit affiliate, which he opened with Robb Wolf and his wife, Nicki Violetti, in Northern California, Everett often spent his weekends in Santa Cruz helping facilitate the certification courses. Though he was never as close with Glassman as Wolf, who thought of Glassman as a father figure, Everett had seen enough over the years to develop a clear impression of CrossFit’s mercurial founder: “It was very apparent to me that he was simultaneously this really intelligent, really charismatic guy, but extremely erratic, unreliable, and unpredictable,” he said. “We would joke constantly about how little we wanted to be legally associated with the CrossFit name, because we never knew what he was going to do. Was he going to bomb a building at some point? That was a running joke.”

To the people on the call with Thiel, word that Glassman knew about the summit was alarming. It wasn’t only Everett who had experience with his volatile temperament: Everyone involved in CrossFit knew that Glassman could be both charming and capricious, capable of both extravagant displays of generosity and sudden flashes of venom. “Greg is going to lose his mind,” one of the speakers on the call wagered. “He’s going to freak the fuck out. He’s going to think this is some kind of insurrection.” Thiel was adamant that it would all be okay. “No, no,” he assured them with characteristic brightness. “It’ll be fine. Everything is cool.”

Nevertheless, when Everett stood before the crowd as guests returned from lunch and took their seats at CrossFit Central that Saturday afternoon, he began to feel some serious trepidation.

It started the previous morning, when Everett walked into CrossFit Central right after dawn for the event’s 7 a.m. registration and found a familiar face already there waiting for him: Tony Budding, CrossFit’s longtime head of media and Glassman’s de facto right-hand man. Glassman had warned Thiel that he might send an emissary to attend the summit on behalf of the brand, someone who could represent the company’s interests and report back on whatever matters were discussed over the course of the weekend. But Everett had not been given a heads-up about this particular attendee, and to him it seemed like an ambush. “I was kind of like, what’s the story here?” Everett said. “This is not supposed to be anything to do with CrossFit HQ. Like, is he here to babysit? Is he here to report back to Glassman? It was a little weird.”

Both Budding and Dave Castro, a former Navy SEAL who had quickly become one of Glassman’s top lieutenants, had flown in from California for this event, though Budding maintained that they were welcome guests, or in any case, “certainly didn’t crash it.” Budding shared Glassman’s enthusiasm for debate and vigorous autodidacticism, and to his mind, the entire appeal of CrossFit was the intellectual stimulation its ideas could provoke. “You have to have a pretty involved background in the core questions of science, which is almost indistinguishable from epistemology, in order to understand why things were conveyed in a certain way rather than in other ways,” Budding explained. “I’ve always loved passionate debates. You get the best results from that. And if you’re a skilled debater, you make it about the ideas, not the person.”

The debates were harmless enough, in the beginning. At 11:15 a.m. on the first day of the event, Nicki Violetti gave a talk under the title “Introducing New Clients to a Program and Dedicated Points of Entry,” which touched on “On-Ramp,” a brief introductory session for new members designed to teach them the basic principles of CrossFit and ease them into the program’s intense regime. The idea, Violetti explained, was to mitigate the risk of injury and reduce the likelihood that someone might be overwhelmed: Don’t throw newcomers into the deep end, she advised; instead, let them slowly wade in. But this was starkly opposed to how the CrossFit methodology was taught at its own seminars. “The needs of Olympians and the needs of our grandparents differ by degree, not kind,” Glassman often declared, touting the accessibility of the program. From his seat in the crowd, Budding raised the issue and fervently disputed the point: Throw them in the deep end, he argued. How could anyone who coaches CrossFit not understand?

But it was during Wolf’s talk on “Nutrition in a Box” on Saturday morning that the mood in the room took a notable shift. A successful nutritionist and an early proponent of what he referred to as “the Paleo solution,” Wolf had frequently found himself in opposition to Glassman and CrossFit’s most loyal adherents, butting heads with his friend and mentor over the issue of the ideal diet for someone practicing high-intensity exercise. Glassman had long advocated for a low-carbohydrate, high-fat diet, and at the time had partnered with the dietician Barry Sears to promote his faddish Zone diet as part of CrossFit’s program. Though both diets involved cutting out grains and sugar, Paleo emphasized whole, unprocessed foods, akin to what might have been eaten by our ancestors millions of years ago, while the Zone focused on food categories—ratios of carbohydrates, fat, and protein—and was ambivalent about food quality. There were those within CrossFit who believed that it didn’t matter which specific diet someone followed so long as they were making predominantly healthy choices. But many at the top—including Castro—saw this quibble as a seismic fracture.

Though Castro had only been involved in CrossFit for a couple of years, he was rapidly becoming one of the most prominent figures in the growing organization, in large part because of his role as the frontman of the CrossFit Games, the now annual competition the brand had organized three years running on Castro’s family ranch. But among those present at the Black Box Summit, Castro had a different reputation: He was regarded as Glassman’s heavy. “He was a racehorse that was beat down and groomed and shown what to believe and how to talk. He was honestly Greg’s enforcer,” Lowy said. “Greg needed somebody like that, who could go in and be an asshole so that he didn’t have to. He would step into places where Greg didn’t want to be and he would cause a disruption.” If the speakers were apprehensive about Budding’s presence at the summit, Castro’s presence was enough to make them fear the worst. Wolf in particular was all but certain of it: Glassman had sent Castro to cause a scene.

#5513. XPENG L03 With VLA 2.0 Smoothly Takes On Paris Roads

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On my recent trip to Paris, I had the opportunity to experience NGP with VLA 2.0 in an XPENG L03. Paris roads and drivers are a bit different than my past experiences. Adapting to the traffic poses a unique challenge. I had previously sampled XPENG’s intelligent driving system in Guangzhou, Beijing, Munich, and Amsterdam, and you can read more details about the system in those articles.

Overall, XPENG has the smoothest and most human-like intelligent driving system that I have experienced. However, I wanted to hear a different perspective. So, when I heard Joel from the Tech Jamo YouTube channel was trying to schedule a ride along, I gave him shotgun on my time slot. We soon took off along with Tanya from XPENG and the test driver.

As the system is not officially approved for Europe yet, we were not able to take the wheel. The French test driver had only used the system a few times before we took our lap. Soon after taking off, I used the voice controls to turn the front seat massager on for Joel. He was instantly a fan, as I was on my past test ride.

While I had previously ridden in the back of the L03 for a short time in Munich, the Paris trip was a bit longer and the roads were much rougher. Overall, the L03 offers a surprising amount of room in back for its size and feels very premium for its class. It felt far more spacious with better build quality and materials compared to the similarly sized and shaped Audi Q4 Sportback e-tron that I sat in at the Paris Motor Show. While riding, it was comfortable and quiet over rough parts of the road. The smoothness of the intelligent driving system also did not create the slightest sense of motion sickness, which I can sometimes be vulnerable to when sitting in the back.

Sitting in the back seat, I was also able to see the HUD over the driver’s shoulder. While it is always difficult to capture on video, the HUD was clear, with necessary information and navigation. I kept thinking that it would be great to have a dark mode, where only the HUD stays on with voice controls handling other functions. You would never need to take your eyes off the road and could activate the screen with a touch if you needed it. Joel was also a fan of the idea, having owned a Honda E with a similar capability. That function is not yet available on the L03, but XPENG has a history of listening to feedback.

Joel also has a Tesla Model 3 with HW3, so he has some frame of reference. He seemed impressed with the smoothness and overall quality of the XPENG. After the drive, I sat down with Joel to get his thoughts.

At the Paris Motor Show, multiple companies were invited to demonstrate their intelligent driving systems. Only XPENG and Tesla had the courage to face the challenge, although I unfortunately didn’t get a chance in my schedule to sample Tesla FSD on this trip. Paris can be a difficult driving environment. You have to anticipate other drivers rather than expecting them to follow the rules. Lane markings, when present, are often not followed.

On these roads, the L03 did seem to accelerate and brake more aggressively than in Amsterdam. To navigate Paris streets, you need to be more assertive, much like on my experience in Beijing. However, it seemed to navigate traffic very smoothly. One issue is that drivers in Paris often drive far beyond the speed limit. However, the XPENG sticks to the speed limit by default, as required under upcoming UN DCAS regulations. Tesla FSD can currently speed, but that will need to change if they are to comply with the new regulations.

The car seems to be adapting rapidly to different road conditions. A Dutch or German driver may feel out of place on Paris roads, but the system seems to be adapting to different roads rapidly. Joel has yet to drive on Paris roads, but I would like to hear his perspective when he tries it. If anything, the intelligent driving system could be helpful with unfamiliar roads and traffic patterns, as well as the unfamiliar customs of other drivers, cyclists and pedestrians.

The L03 anticipated what was going to happen surprisingly well. It thought ahead, rather than reacting. In one instance, it saw someone crossing the road, judged her speed and trajectory, and slowed down just enough so that it would get to her position as she was stepping off the road. No slower or faster than needed. It didn’t accelerate and then brake hard like many drivers. It navigated the system just as fast, but the smoother driving would have prevented accordion effect from the cars behind it. And the lack of unnecessary acceleration and braking would undoubtedly reduce energy consumption.

Overall, the car drove very smoothly. So smoothly that what might have been a tricky situation was a non-issue, and you could forget it was driving. You almost take for granted how good it is until you get into a Paris Uber driven by a human being. While people can nitpick little things from the test video, it performed noticeably better than the typical human driver in Paris. Not only was it smoother, but it seemed to be safer and follow the rules better. None of the colorful hand signals and yelling that I experienced on more than one occasion from human drivers.

While NGP with VLA 2.0 is still a Level 2 system, it already felt safe. A human using it would almost undoubtedly be safer than if driving on their own. Especially on unfamiliar roads. NGP with VLA 2.0 may not be ready to fully replace human drivers yet, but it doesn’t feel far off.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The new EV will follow the EX60 as the second vehicle based on Volvo’s SPA3 platform, positioned as a smaller, more affordable option.

As part of Volvo’s largest product offensive, the company plans to launch 13 new cars through the end of 2030.

All new vehicles will be tailored to specific markets, including the US, Europe, and China. Volvo announced during its third-quarter earnings on Friday that it will reveal three of the new vehicles in 2027.

One of the new vehicles will be for Europe and the other two are destined for China (more on the US later).

Following the EX60, which Volvo said has been “a big success,” racking up over 50,000 orders in Europe so far, Volvo will launch the second electric car based on its SPA3 platform.

According to Volvo the new model is a “global BEV car, which targets a large, lower priced market segment” in Europe. The first customer deliveries are set to begin after next summer.

While the company didn’t reveal any further details, the new vehicle is suspected to be the successor to the current EX40.

The SPA3 platform supports everything from sedans and compact SUVs like the EX30 to the three-row EX90. As a mass-market vehicle, the new SPA3-based EV will likely be an SUV.

Will Volvo bring the lower-priced EV to the US? It said it was a “global BEV car,” but also that it was aimed at Europe’s lower-priced segment.

Luis Rezende, president of Volvo Cars America, told reporters during a media roundtable (via TheVerge) earlier this year that the company is preparing to launch a new EV in 2027 that will replace the EX30 as its new entry-level electric car.

Volvo initially said the lower-priced Single Motor EX30 would go on sale in the US in late 2025 starting at $34,950, but later backtracked and discontinued the electric SUV from its lineup altogether.

Although it may not be as cheap as the entry-level $35,000 EX30, Renzende said prices would be “very similar.”

According to an Automotive News report, Volvo will launch the new electric crossover SUV by the end of 2027, starting at under $50,000.

The new entry-level model is expected to go on sale as the EX50, a taller and longer electric SUV than the current EX40.

Volvo filed a trademark with the European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO) for EX50 in July. Although the filing didn’t reveal many details, it did confirm it will be electric.

In the meantime, Volvo is fast-tracking production of its first long-range plug-in hybrid versions of the XC90 and XC60. The first customer deliveries are set to begin this year in Europe, followed by the US shortly after.

In Q1 2027, Volvo will begin production of the XC60 in Charleston. By the end of 2030, it plans to begin production of a “second, all-new car with multiple powertrain options,” the company said.
