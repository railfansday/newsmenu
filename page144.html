#5275. 7 insights from F1 Capital Factory: Builders take deep tech startup infrastructure from prototype to scale

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Across energy, defense, space and applied AI, deep tech startup infrastructure is moving out of the lab and onto the factory floor. The startups building it are no longer trying to prove that the physics works. They are proving that it can be manufactured, shipped and financed at scale.

The next phase will be defined by who controls the bottlenecks. In AI data centers, that means power and cooling, while munitions and robots depend on factory capacity. Companies applying AI to mortgages and insurance depend on domain expertise. As AI infrastructure bottlenecks take focus, investors and governments are backing builders that vertically integrate, put their own capital at risk and turn one-off projects into repeatable products.

“If you’re trying to deploy many gigawatts per year of nuclear, you’ve got to turn it from being a project into being a product,” said Matt Loszak (pictured), co-founder and chief executive officer of Aalo Atomics Inc. “That’s really what we’re doing for the first time for nuclear.”

Loszak and other industry experts spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier at theCUBE + NYSE Wired: F1 Capital Factory event in Austin, Texas. The interviews covered deep tech startup infrastructure related to two-phase cooling, mass-manufactured nuclear reactors, solid rocket motors, satellite communications, defense investing and AI agents in lending and insurance.

Here are seven standout insights from the event:

Large nuclear plants take 10 to 20 years to build, which is far too slow for the data-center buildout. Aalo instead designs around one constraint: the most powerful reactor that fits on the back of a truck. Its reactors use liquid sodium as the coolant and off-the-shelf fuel, so they need no backup power to stay cool during a shutdown, Loszak noted. The company turned on a full-scale prototype reactor two and a half years after its founding, after spending about $100 million. It is now moving into a 1 million-square-foot factory designed to produce 100 reactors a year. Each commercial pod of five reactors and three turbines delivers about 50 megawatts, and the near-term goal is reaching full power for a Crusoe data center.

Accelsius LLC uses two-phase cooling, in which a fluid turns to vapor at the chip, to remove heat that single-phase water systems increasingly cannot handle. Racks that drew 10 to 30 kilowatts a few years ago now run at 600 kilowatts and are headed to a megawatt, according to John Hewitt, CEO of Accelsius, and Josh Claman, executive chairman and founding CEO. The approach takes water out of the data center and frees power for compute. Claman estimated that this is worth about $60 million in added revenue for every 100 megawatts. Sovereign AI systems and inference pods placed in corporate basements also need self-contained, low-maintenance cooling, and the company has built a modular product line for each of those uses.

Conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East have shown that Western estimates of the missiles and munitions needed for a war were off by orders of magnitude. That has pushed the industry from performance at any cost toward affordable mass. X-Bow Systems Inc. builds the solid rocket motors that power those munitions, and rocket-assisted takeoff motors for Group 3 and Group 4 drones are now its hottest product, explained Jason Hundley, founder and CEO of X-Bow. The company has more than 500 employees and opened its first factory this year, which Hundley described as the first new technology factory at scale in its industrial base. It is on a path to more than $1 billion in annual revenue within two years. Its next systems will be built autonomy-first, shrinking a seven- or eight-step supply chain to one or two steps.

Marlinspike Partners LLC invests in dual-use defense technology companies that sell to both commercial and government customers, betting that the winners of physical AI will be the builders. Startups can build prototypes in California, but scaling up requires the land, opportunity and supportive government found in Texas, emphasized Jude Akpunku, senior associate at Marlinspike. Portfolio companies tackle that manufacturing bottleneck directly. Foundry Robotics Inc. is designing a factory floor where robots build other robots, and Layup Parts Inc. makes composite parts on an automated floor. The firm also backs JetZero Inc., a blended-wing-body aircraft developer with Air Force contracts that expects to cut fuel costs by 50% and fly in the fourth quarter of 2027.

Borrowers who submit a lead online can wait 21 to 40 hours for a lender to reach out, even though the first lender to respond usually wins the loan. AIVI LLC connects those leads faster and then studies loan officers’ calls to learn what the top 20% of closers do differently, so agents can automate those habits. That was the approach highlighted by Giorgio Mihaila, co-founder and CEO of AIVI, and Akshay Lazarus, an advisor to AIVI. The next step is bots dealing with bots: when Mihaila had an AI agent shop 100 lenders for a home equity line of credit, only 13 responded. The self-funded company is aiming for $10 million in annual recurring revenue next year.

CesiumAstro Inc. builds communications infrastructure for space, from semiconductors and active phased-array payloads to complete satellites. Its pipeline has grown from about $6 billion a year ago to $53 billion in named opportunities as governments back sovereign satellite networks for both national security and broadband, shared Kenneth Smith, chief financial officer of CesiumAstro. On Oct. 1, the company launched Element, which it calls the first fully turnkey active phased-array satellite. It also acquired fabless chip designer Jariet Technologies to secure the advanced chips its payloads require. CesiumAstro received a $10 million grant from the Texas Space Commission last year, and it envisions AI routing traffic between satellites and the ground on its own.

Catch the complete conversation on theCUBE.

Steadily Inc. insures about 300,000 of the 20 million rental properties in the U.S., mostly for small landlords who own one to three homes. AI gains were modest in 2024, but newer models now deliver about 300% higher throughput per engineer than two years ago, according to Darren Nix, co-founder and CEO of Steadily. Because insurance is regulated, a human approves every important decision, with AI preparing work that is correct about 85% of the time. Voice AI takes claims mainly after business hours, when callers do not expect a person. Steadily doubled this year to about $600 million in annual premium with close to 300 employees. Nix estimated that three years ago, a company at this scale would have needed two to three times as many people.

I haven’t really been invested in Overwatch since before the fiasco that was Overwatch 2. And that’s without diving into the company’s chilling cornucopia of controversies.

Yet the game seems to be back on track, thankfully dropping the “2” from its name and delivering quite a lot on the gameplay front. As someone who played a lot of Overwatch before dropping it for good, there’s something comforting about seeing it touch the lives of so many people that, 10 years since its release, it still has a place to bring silly joy to avid and lapsed players alike. This seemed to be organic, too—a nice change of pace from when the community has to band together to ask for more than crumbs.

The first new card I found in Forbidden Solitaire's new DLC The Discarded gave me the power to throw a card like a knife at whatever horror was trying to kill me. Although nothing warranted such a desperate move, I fear it won't be long before I find myself out of options now that I've seen the devilish new tricks the developers have dealt in the DLC's first hour.

I'm lucky I just spent the last week playing through the base game because The Discarded offers no help: From the moment you double click the DLC executable on the game's '90s-inspired PC interface, you're on your own. It's not quite enough to understand how to play solitaire; you need to know how to play Forbidden Solitaire, a haunted version of the classic card game where you must change your approach based on the modified cards the developers throw at you.

Some cards might be locked away until you play the right suits. Others might be surrounded by bones, requiring them to be played twice before leaving the board. Your deck will run out quickly if you don't strategize around these factors and try to knock off as many cards as you can in one turn.

Thankfully, you can cheat… by jamming gems into your flesh. The eyeball in the wall that compels you to mutilate your hand for power is back. This time, you can even sear glyphs into your skin, too. Each unholy decoration gives you bonuses that can help tilt the balance in your favor, like an increased chance to deal extra damage during combat—oh yeah, there's combat.

Right away, The Discarded's enemies don't play around. In the original game, you're taught to pay close attention to whatever horrible thing a monster plans to do once you finish your turn. This can be simply an attack that drains your health or a variety of curses that warp the cards. The beast at the start of the DLC can block you from playing cards by covering them in wet paint, and later you have to deal with marbled cards that don't contribute to your combos—and thus your damage.

I never knew solitaire could have so much depth until I played Forbidden Solitaire, and The Discarded sprinkles even more challenges that change your approach to what is otherwise a pretty straightforward game.

It also helps that there's an entire mystery to unravel as you go. You play the new DLC as Robyn Cierra, a character digging through Forbidden Solitaire, the in-universe 1995 CD-ROM game, in search of their sister. There's another character sending you texts with images and fuzzy FMV clips that detail all the bizarre events surrounding the development of the game, back in the early '90s. And if it's like the base game's story, I don't expect things to stay relatively normal for long.

#741. With hundreds dying in crashes, LA turns to speed cameras for change

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Los Angeles turned on approximately 20 cameras at dangerous intersections around the city on Monday (Oct. 26), intended to catch speeding drivers and, hopefully, save lives.

For the next 60 days, drivers exceeding the speed limit by at least 11 miles per hour will receive written warnings by mail. Following that, tickets will be issued to offenders, with fines ranging from $50 to $500, depending on the driver's speed. By the end of the year, the Los Angeles Department of Transportation (LADOT) plans to activate 125 cameras across the city.

LA is currently trying to get a handle on vehicular violence, with car crashes in the city claiming about 300 lives annually, a toll that exceeds the annual number of homicides in the city of 4 million, according to the Los Angeles Times. The city has placed cameras near high-injury/high-death intersections, near schools, and at spots known for street racing.

LA is years behind other large cities like New York, Oakland, and San Francisco, which have already installed speed cameras. In the latter city, the cameras brought about a dramatic 80 percent drop in speeding.

LADOT expects its cameras to reduce fatal crashes at their locations by 53 to 71 percent. Malibu, a nearby city in Los Angeles County, recently launched a speed camera pilot program on 21 miles of the Pacific Coast Highway. Over the course of nine days last month, the cameras recorded approximately 100,000 speeding violations.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Alexis Pereira | 26 Oct 2026 20:40

Barcelona’s defensive injury crisis — which already included Jules Kounde and Eric Garcia — deepened on Sunday when Xavi Espart suffered syndesmosis ligament damage to his right ankle during the 2-1 El Clasico defeat to Real Madrid, ruling him out for approximately seven weeks. The injury has immediate consequences for Barcelona’s defensive options, but it also threatens to complicate two of the most closely watched January transfer storylines: Alejandro Balde’s potential move to Manchester United and any possible sale of Kounde.

Espart was hurt after a challenge by Aurelien Tchouameni. With Kounde and Garcia both already sidelined, Hansi Flick was forced to use Balde off the bench, with Joao Cancelo switching to the right side having started on the left. The improvisation illustrated the scale of the problem.

Manchester United had identified Balde as a leading target for the January window to address their ongoing problems at left-back. The Catalan defender had fallen behind in the hierarchy at Nou Camp, and a loan or permanent departure looked plausible. The injury situation now changes the calculation for Flick.

If Balde takes advantage of his increased opportunities to perform well, the case for keeping him through the winter becomes considerably stronger. A defender who was a candidate for sale on the back of limited game time looks considerably different once he is playing regularly. United’s January plans in this area may need to be reconsidered.

The defensive crisis also affects the Kounde situation. Recent reports suggested Barcelona were prepared to let Kounde leave in January, with Chelsea and Liverpool among those tracking the French defender. The absence of Garcia and Espart — both capable of covering right-back — changes the arithmetic. With Kounde now the only natural option for that position once he recovers from his own absence, the argument for allowing a winter sale is considerably weaker. Barcelona may be pushed to keep him through January regardless of incoming interest.

Flick acknowledged the depth issue openly after the Clasico. ‘We do not have many options — we must manage with what we have,’ he said. ‘We may have to call on reserve and academy players.’ Guillem Victor, a regular in the Barcelona B squad at right-back, has been mentioned as a short-term option.

Welcome back to World Brief, where we’re looking at the electoral defeat of former Serbian President Aleksandar Vucic, Brazil’s surprise presidential election results, and the race for United Nations secretary-general.

Tens of thousands of Serbs flooded the streets of Belgrade on Monday to celebrate the electoral defeat of strongman Aleksandar Vucic, who has dominated the country’s politics for more than a decade. His ouster is the culmination of nearly two years of student-led protests against one of Europe’s most staunchly populist regimes, and it ushers in a new era of governance led by a youth-organized grassroots movement.

Since taking power in 2014, Vucic has cracked down on democratic freedoms. He has relied on systemic election fraud to keep his Serbian Progressive Party in power, used violence to quell unrest, and sidled closer to Russia and China despite European condemnation. Oscillating between the president’s and prime minister’s office to remain in control, Vucic managed to extend his reign for years.

But a student-led protest movement years in the making appears to be what finally upended Vucic’s grip on power.

In November 2024, demonstrations erupted over the collapse of a concrete railway station canopy that killed 16 people. Activists accused Belgrade of negligence, corruption, and poor state oversight. Security forces responded with excessive force, arbitrary arrests, and digital repression.

Still, the protest movement never died out, and on Sunday, the Student List group won 52.54 percent of the vote, with voter turnout hitting nearly 69 percent—a more than 30-year high.

“It is clear that we have lost these elections,” Vucic said in a televised speech.

Vucic blamed foreign interference for his defeat without providing evidence. “This was the verification of a revolution that they carried out directly with support of different European countries and the deep American state, the one that is also against President [Donald] Trump,” Vucic said. Trump has sought closer ties with Vucic in recent months to try to keep him in office.

However, international observers noted that Vucic’s party held an unfair advantage heading into Sunday’s vote. “Entrenched advantages enjoyed by the ruling party, including misuse of state resources, together with intimidation and pressure on voters, candidates, and civil society, as well as disruptions of campaign activities, further marred the electoral environment,” the Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe’s international election observation mission wrote in a statement on Sunday.

The Student List is expected to win 140 seats in the country’s parliament, giving the bloc a ruling mandate without having to rely on coalition-building. Now, the party faces the difficult task of choosing a prime minister to steer Serbia forward. The Student List has yet to name anyone to the post, though experts suspect that Ilija Srdanovic, a cardiologist who led the movement, is a top contender. Former central bank governor Dejan Soskic, who resigned from that post in 2012 to protest alleged government interference, is also seen as a front-runner.

Following Sunday’s results, acting President Ana Brnabic must also now call for an extraordinary presidential election no later than Dec. 27; Vucic resigned from the presidency last month to run for the prime minister’s office. It is unclear whom the Student List will nominate for that role.

Lula’s big win. Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva secured a surprise victory against far-right Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro in Sunday’s presidential election runoff. With a more than 4 million vote gap, Lula’s win served as a rebuke of the conservative wave sweeping Latin America, and it defied Trump’s efforts to establish another populist ally in the region.

Lula’s comeback stunned analysts, as the incumbent had finished nearly 2 percentage points behind Bolsonaro during the election’s first round earlier this month. “Faced with the vivid possibility of another Bolsonaro presidency, however, progressive Brazilians and some centrists mobilized with a level of grassroots organizing uncharacteristic of recent years,” Catherine Osborn wrote in Foreign Policy.

Foreign congratulations have already begun to pour in. Notably, though, Bolsonaro (like his father) appears unwilling to accept his loss. “It is impossible to recognize the election results, given the circumstances under which things unfolded,” Bolsonaro said on Monday, citing the Supreme Federal Court’s last-minute decision to extend voting by two hours due to alleged voter transport irregularities. Top security officials from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey met with Iranian leaders in Tehran on Monday to discuss opening channels to “prevent the return of military escalation” in the Middle East. The high-level talks are the first between Tehran and the so-called Mecca Pact since fighting erupted between Yemen’s Houthi rebels and Saudi Arabia in July, and they come amid fears that the United States and Israel are preparing to launch a fresh offensive against Iran after next week’s U.S. midterm elections.

In early October, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey activated a mutual defense pact in response to repeated Houthi strikes on critical Saudi infrastructure. However, that hasn’t stopped the Iranian-backed rebel group from targeting the kingdom’s energy facilities, civilian airports, and Muslim holy sites.

Members of the Mecca Pact seek to convince Tehran to force the Houthis to dial back their assault before the United States and Israel return to active fighting. However, Iran appears more focused on the threat from Israel. According to Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei, Monday’s talks will include a discussion of “Israel’s expansionist policies, developments in the Palestinian territories and Lebanon, and the repercussions of U.S. and Israeli military action against ‌Iran.”

Japan has the world’s second-oldest population, and its senior citizens are often the target of online scammers; last year, fraudsters stole around $2 billion from the country’s older residents.

#3709. Laika and Fathom Take Legal Action to Stop ‘Wildwood’ Screenings at L.A.’s Bruin Theatre

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

L.A.’s historic Bruin Theatre met its ambitious fall reopening target on Friday, October 23, but its return is already facing a challenge from the companies behind its first attraction. Laika and Fathom Entertainment say they have taken legal action to stop the Bruin from showing “Wildwood.”

The film was previously advertised as a week-long run at the newly reopened Westwood venue. But in a joint statement provided exclusively to IndieWire, the animation studio and its theatrical distribution partner said concerns about operator Rui Pereira‘s history of controversial comments and behavior prompted them to seek an end to screenings. “When Laika became aware of the Bruin engagement, we immediately raised concerns with Fathom Entertainment, our theatrical distribution partner. Laika and Fathom commenced legal action to end the film’s screenings at the theatre,” the companies said in an email.

“We have not yet received confirmation that the screenings have stopped. We are continuing to explore our options, as the concerns regarding the theatre operator are incompatible with the values of our two companies.” IndieWire independently confirmed that “Wildwood” screenings had continued at the Bruin through at least Sunday afternoon, October 25, and as of publication, the theater’s website still advertised daily screenings at 1:30 p.m., 4 p.m., 7 p.m., and 9:30 p.m.

Laika and Fathom Entertainment did not specify what kind of legal action they had taken to stop showings of their film. On a phone call with IndieWire, Pereira asked how we had obtained a cease-and-desist letter. IndieWire had neither received nor reviewed such a letter and told Pereira we had communicated directly with the companies. He continued, “You guys are assholes. Everything you have written is false.”

The dispute introduces an immediate programming problem for Pereira’s revived movie palace, which reopened with “Wildwood” after sitting dark since Regency Theatres departed the space in July 2024. It also follows months of scrutiny involving Pereira, whose rocky history operating Toronto’s Kingsway Theatre became the subject of an IndieWire investigation on September 3.

IndieWire’s earlier reporting examined allegations of assault, nonpayment, racial discrimination, online harassment, and unauthorized film exhibition. The reporting drew on interviews with more than 25 sources in the U.S. and Canada, as well as court records, digital correspondence, social media posts, and years of advertised Kingsway screenings some rights holders say they did not authorize. Pereira has broadly disputed those allegations and characterized the ongoing scrutiny of his businesses as an effort to undermine his work as an exhibitor.

“There are no controversies and any attempt to generate them is dishonest and disingenuous,” Pereira said then. “The Bruin Theater is a gift, not only to Los Angeles, but the world — a cultural heritage site that unites generations of movie goers in the experience of cinema.”

Several journalists have described being targeted by Pereira after publishing critical coverage or omitting the Kingsway from their reporting. Speaking with IndieWire on the phone and in a subsequent email on October 26, Pereira referred to IndieWire staff as “presstitutes” and accused the publication of getting paid to write about him negatively.

Two volunteers left the Bruin restoration project in August after posts attributed to the Kingsway account circulated among Los Angeles moviegoers. Screenshots reviewed by IndieWire included racist and antisemitic remarks, as well as exchanges targeting individual patrons. Sources also described threats and a pattern of prolonged retaliation, alleging that Pereira continued targeting some critics online for months or years after their initial disputes.

When asked by The Hollywood Reporter in an August 23 report about some specific Kingsway posts — including one telling a target to “Go back to Poland” and another describing a swastika painted on a Jewish family’s home as “the proper symbol for zionazis” — Pereira acknowledged that the comments were “offensive” and said he “must take responsibility.”

The current dispute over “Wildwood” also follows separate questions about the Kingsway’s programming. IndieWire previously identified 55 advertised screenings of 17 films that three rights holders said they had not authorized at Pereira’s Toronto theater. Those findings came from a limited sample and did not establish that all or even most Kingsway screenings were unlicensed. “There are no issues with film licensing or any disputes,” Pereira said previously.

Notably, Laika and Fathom’s statement does not suggest that the Bruin’s “Wildwood” booking was unauthorized from the outset. Rather, the companies say they sought to end the Bruin screenings over specific concerns about Pereira as operator. “Wildwood” opened in nearly 3,000 theaters nationwide, and a booking on that scale is hardly an endorsement of every exhibitor.

Still, independent theaters often build their identities around the taste and reputations of the people running them. At a beloved landmark like the Bruin, affection for an old movie palace can translate into intense trust in whoever promises to save it. For now, the location has achieved the reopening its new operator promised. Whether it can continue showing the film that brought audiences back is in dispute.
