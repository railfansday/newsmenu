#3222. Biotech stocks are surging. How investors can play the new drug innovation boom

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Moderna gave investors a scare as its stock plunged on Thursday. But it's still one of the hottest biotechs this year. And it's hardly alone – the sector is on a roll and looks poised for more gains.

The iShares Biotechnology ETF (IBB) is up 24% through October 23, building on a 28% gain in 2025. Two dozen of the ETF's 233 holdings have more than doubled year-to-date. Moderna leads the pack with a 587% surge — even after an 18% decline following a leak of clinical data for its cancer vaccine (which was actually positive).

Analysts say a new biotech cycle is underway, fueled by clinical trial results, M&A activity, and promising technologies such as genomics, mRNA — and AI.

"We're in the midst of a powerful innovation cycle," said John Hall, portfolio manager of the T. Rowe Price Biotechnology ETF. "What's super exciting is we're seeing newer modalities start to make it to market." Companies "understand the disease biology better," he adds, and that could ultimately lead to more novel drugs making it to market.

Investors are excited about technologies like mRNA and genomics, along with a steady stream of new drug approvals. The FDA approved about a dozen novel biologic drugs in 2025, out of 46 approvals in total. Roughly 66 novel drugs are in the pipeline for FDA approval, including potential blockbusters like Moderna and Merck's cancer drug for melanoma, and a next-generation obesity drug from Eli Lilly.

Analysts say AI could also lead to more approvals as companies deploy the software to speed up drug development.

"Biotech is a phenomenal application for artificial intelligence and machine learning," says Piper Sandler analyst Ted Tenthoff. "You can set up exciting predictive models that, frankly, can do things that we've never been able to do in the space in terms of novel drug discovery."

Granted, the industry is recovering from a fallow period. The sector's last strong performance was during the Covid era when ultra-low interest rates encouraged investment in early-stage drug developers, and mRNA vaccines fueled a revenue surge for companies like Moderna, Pfizer and Biontech.

The sector then went cold as many early-stage companies fizzled. Demand for Covid vaccines evaporated, and surging interest rates put a chill in IPOs and M&A.

The risks haven't evaporated – this is still a sector whose products take years to develop, resulting in far more failures than successes. But analysts say the outlook is healthier now. While that's reflected in strong performance and richer valuations, there's still good reason to invest.

Recent clinical trial results are stoking excitement that novel drugs in the works could tackle some of the toughest problems in medicine.

Moderna and Merck's melanoma drug, for instance, uses mRNA to trigger an immune response in patients, targeting tumors that have been surgically removed and genetically analyzed. It's not a preventive vaccine, but it uses a similar immunology mechanism and showed a 42% reduction in cancer recurrence or death (used in conjunction with Merck's cancer drug Keytruda).

While the Phase 3 results disappointed Wall Street, oncologists say the outcomes were still highly positive. And they see the technology as a potential game changer, ushering in an era of personalized treatments for cancers. Moderna and Merck have said they aim to submit the drug for FDA approval and get it launched in 2027.

Inflammation treatment is another promising area. AstraZeneca and Amgen recently released positive Phase 3 results for another use for their drug Tezspire, an injectable approved for nasal polyps and asthma. In clinical trials, it showed that it successfully relieved swallowing symptoms and healed microscopic and visual tissue damage in patients with severe throat inflammation.

Roche, meanwhile, notched a win after the FDA approved its drug Enspryng, a therapy to treat rare inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. It became the first and only at-home injection for adults with thyroid eye disease.

Obesity drugs are also advancing beyond the first generation of GLP-1s. Eli Lilly's retatrutide, a "triple agonist" targeting three different hormone receptors, cleared two more clinical trials over the summer. The drug produced 28%-30% weight loss at the highest doses in the study, Lilly said. The company plans to submit the drug for FDA approval in the first quarter of 2027.

Much of the innovation isn't just happening in drug-making – it's also in areas like gene sequencing, mRNA, and high-tech instruments.

Illumina has more than doubled this year. The DNA and gene sequencing company is seeing accelerating demand for its NovaSeq X high-end sequencing platform. Pharmaceutical companies including Bristol-Myers Squibb, Merck and Kura are working with Illumina to develop precision oncology solutions.

Another big winner is 10x Genomics, up more than 300%. The biotech firm offers its lab instruments to help scientists research cancer, immunology and neuroscience. The company's Atera platform is helping produce data sets to train AI models, a process that helps accelerate personalized therapies.

On the AI front, companies are using the technology for more efficient drug discovery, says Shreefāl Mehta, president of Guidant BioTherapeutics. Using AI, "you can now bring a drug to clinical trials with less capital. More investment is also going into companies that are demonstrating that," he said.

Global AI in drug discovery will grow from a $20 billion market in 2025 to more than $160 billion by 2035, according to forecasts by Towards Health, a research and consulting firm.

One other catalyst for the sector could be M&A. Dealmaking for biopharma hit $134 billion in the first half of 2026, up from $112 billion for all of 2025, notes Jeet Mukherjee, biotech analyst at BTIG. That's a positive signal, indicating more drugs in development are showing clinical promise.

It also helps that Big Pharma has a "patent cliff" ahead. Products representing $180 billion of revenue will lose exclusivity by 2030, according to research firm RTW Institute. Companies with looming expiries "are looking for novel products and innovative products to put into their pipeline," Mehta says.

GLP-1 blockbusters have also left some companies flush with cash for acquisitions. Eli Lilly alone has completed 10 acquisition deals in 2026 worth $25 billion.

ETFs can capture the investing theme, though they aren't all alike. The iShares Biotech ETF is one of the lower cost funds. It tracks a market-cap weighted index of biotech stocks, led by companies like Gilead Sciences, Amgen, Vertex Pharmaceuticals, and Moderna.

The State Street SPDR S&P Biotech ETF holds stocks in equal weight, offering more exposure to small- and mid-cap names. Its top holdings are Moderna, Natera, Twist Bioscience and Halozyme Therapeutics.

Piper Sandler calls Illumina and 10x Genomics the "primary infrastructure plays" in the life science arena, saying their technologies are "driving the next generation of precision medicine."

Piper analyst David Westenberg has a buy rating and $305 target on Illumina, implying 14% gains from recent prices around $267. He has a hold rating on 10X with an $80 target, above recent prices around $70.

Twist Bioscience is another name to consider. The company makes customized synthetic DNA and sells DNA sequencing tools. Twist has partnered with Eli Lilly's AI and machine-learning platform and linked up with Anthropic in August to start testing its AI model, Claude, for protein development.

Twist isn't expected to turn an EPS profit until 2029, according to consensus forecasts. But revenue is expected to go from $572 million in 2027 to $833 million in 2029. Analysts have an average target of $168 on the stock, implying 21% returns from recent prices around $138.

In the small-cap space, Piper Sandler's Tenthoff likes Monte Rosa Therapeutics, a pioneer in protein degradation that selectively destroys disease-causing proteins.

The firm has partnered with Novartis to develop a drug for inflammatory conditions and autoimmune diseases. The companies announced a Phase 2 clinical study in September for an autoimmune disease treatment, which has been "triggering milestones to Monte Rosa," Tenthoff said.

Tenthoff has a buy rating and $37 target on the stock, which recently traded around $10.70.

Also in the small-cap arena, BTIG's Mukherjee recommends Faeth Therapeutics.

The biotech is developing a novel two-drug regimen to treat breast cancer and other tumors. The regimen "has the potential to address safety and dosing limitations of another recently approved drug for breast cancer," he says. And it could become "a mainstay of the future breast cancer treatment paradigm and other cancers."

Faeth is a clinical-stage company that has yet to bring a drug to market. Yet the stock is up 93% year-to-date on positive clinical trial results, trading around $18. Mukherjee has a $55 target on it.

Kymera Therapeutics is worth considering too. The company is developing an oral version of the blockbuster drug Dupixent (made and co-developed by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals and Sanofi). Dupixent is an injectable, however, and oral agents "are frequently preferred by patients," Mukherjee says. "The drug has the potential to not only take share from patients taking Dupixent for numerous immunological diseases but also expanding the market to those not taking systemic biologic drugs," he tells CNBC.

Kymera has said it doesn't expect a patent fight over Dupixent because its drug is distinct, not a biosimilar or biologic copy.

T. Rowe's Hall also likes Kymera for its potential "to unlock disease targets that have been classically considered un-druggable." The stock is the second largest holding in T. Rowe's Biotech ETF, accounting for 4.2% of the portfolio.

#3841. Judi Dench Explains How Losing Sight Has Changed Her Life

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Now aged 91, it's understandable that Judi Dench might not appear in as many films as she did at the peak of her 70-year career. But the outspoken actor, known for taking unexpected positions on topics as varied as The Crown (she's against it), Harvey Weinstein (she encourages forgiveness), and trigger warnings in the theater ("if you’re that sensitive, don’t go") says her retirement isn't consensual, and is instead a result of her deteriorating eyesight.

In 2012, Dench was diagnosed with age-related macular degeneration, a condition that progressively impairs vision. In 2023, she admitted that she could no longer learn lines for a role, and that a community of friends and family assisted her in that task. “I can’t see on a film set any more,” she said then. “And I can’t see to read. So I can’t see much. But, you know, you just deal with it. Get on."

Judy Dench as Queen Elizabeth I in Shakespeare in Love.

By 2025, her vision loss had grown so severe that she had reached a stage of “no eyesight," which required her to enlist a companion any time she ventures out. “I can’t see and I’ll walk into something or fall over,” she said then.

In an interview broadcast Thursday by BBC Radio 4, Dench provided an update on her condition. Speaking on the Today program, the Shakespeare in Love star said her career is essentially over. “I can't be on stage. I can't do a play anymore. I can't do a film anymore. I can't read anymore,” she told interviewer Emma Barnett.

Javier Bardem, Judi Dench, and Daniel Craig on October 22, 2012.

However, the actor says she doesn't let the general effects of aging hold her back. “There's nothing to say for it at all," she said when asked about getting older. "[I] don't think about it too much."

Judi Dench's long career has spanned theater, television, and film. In 1999, she won the Oscar for Best Supporting Actress for her role as Queen Elizabeth I in Shakespeare in Love, despite her relatively brief screen time. She also broke ground in t1995's GoldenEye, as the first woman to portray “M,” the James Bond world's fictional head of British intelligence. She continued in the role until 2012, when her character was killed off in the franchise's 25th film, Skyfall.

When Barnett asked if Dench had an opinion on who should play the next James Bond, she remained noncommittal. She simply replied, "no," but reminisced about a lunch she had with former MI6 chief Sir David Spedding. The meeting was “very exciting,” she said, though it was plagued with challenges.

According to Dench, she was told “we'll send a car to you, we'll come and pick you up” for the meeting, but the driver that was dispatched couldn't find her home. As a result, she was 20 minutes late to visit with the former official, Dench told Barnett. “I said, 'I'm sorry for being so late because your driver couldn't find my house.' I loved it."

#532. A hidden bias in human language challenges 70 years of science

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the University of Vermont have discovered a surprising pattern in human language that challenges a scientific theory widely accepted for more than 70 years. Their findings suggest that the meaning of words is shaped by something more fundamental than emotion: our understanding of power, danger, and order.

The study, published in Science Advances, introduces a new approach called "ousiometrics," which uses mathematical methods to measure the essential meaning of language. Its most striking finding is that the words people use show a consistent and widespread preference for safety over danger.

This hidden safety bias could transform how researchers study language, understand human behavior, and develop artificial intelligence systems that interpret what people say and write.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Safety Bias in Human Language

Since the 1950s, much of the scientific understanding of word meaning has relied on a framework developed through pioneering research by psychologist Charles Osgood and others. Known as the VAD model, it describes meaning through three emotional dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (excited vs. calm), and dominance (controlling vs. submissive).

This approach became influential in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, particularly in systems designed to identify the emotional tone of written language.

However, the University of Vermont researchers found evidence that this long-established model overlooks important aspects of how meaning is organized.

Their analysis examined billions of individual uses of more than 20,000 words across a wide range of real-world texts. Using advanced computational techniques, the team identified a different underlying structure. They found that the three dimensions in the traditional VAD model are not independent of one another, as previously assumed.

Instead, their results indicate that word meaning can be more effectively described through three separate dimensions: power (weak vs. powerful), danger (safe vs. dangerous), and structure (ordered vs. chaotic).

The research, supported by the US National Science Foundation, Google, MassMutual, and other funders, found that this new framework accounted for more than 90% of the variation in measured word meaning. The traditional VAD approach explained approximately 72%.

That improvement could be especially important as large language models, automated content moderation, and other AI technologies increasingly depend on understanding human communication.

Why Human Language Favors Safety Over Danger

One of the study's most unexpected discoveries emerged when the researchers examined how words actually appear in everyday communication.

Across books, news reporting, social media, and spoken language, words associated with safety appeared much more frequently than those associated with danger.

This finding offers a new explanation for a familiar phenomenon called the Pollyanna principle, which describes the tendency for human language to lean toward positive rather than negative words.

For decades, researchers generally interpreted this pattern as evidence that people favor positive emotional expressions. The new analysis suggests that something more fundamental may be responsible.

According to the researchers, the apparent preference for positivity largely reflects an underlying preference for safety.

"The Pollyanna principle's positivity bias," the study concludes. "is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias."

"This is a big observation that comes out of this work," said Peter Dodds, director of the UVM's Complex Systems Institute and senior author of the study. "Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."

Could Human Language Be Shaped by Survival?

The discovery raises an intriguing possibility: the structure of human language may reflect evolutionary pressures associated with staying alive.

From this perspective, words do more than communicate feelings. They also help people recognize threats, evaluate uncertainty, establish trust, and coordinate actions with others.

Being able to distinguish between safety and danger is essential to survival. The researchers suggest that this basic human priority may also influence how meaning is organized in language.

Throughout daily life, people constantly exchange information about whether individuals, places, situations, and behaviors are threatening or safe. The new findings suggest that these distinctions may be more central to communication than previously recognized.

They also offer a different interpretation of why positive language is so common. Rather than simply expressing happiness or approval, many positive words may convey a sense of security, stability, or predictability.

Julia Zimmerman, a postdoctoral researcher in UVM's Computational Story Lab and a co-author of the study, said the three newly identified dimensions reflect experiences that are fundamental to human life.

"Power, danger, and structure," she said, "are relevant to every person that's ever lived."

Researchers have also long recognized that people tend to favor language associated with goodness and low aggression. The team argues that these familiar tendencies may arise from the same deeper pattern.

"We now understand," the team writes, that these are "shadows of an underlying linguistic safety bias."

Although the results are consistent with a possible connection between language and survival, they do not establish that evolutionary pressures directly caused the safety bias.

A Discovery That Could Change How AI Understands Language

The findings have implications that extend well beyond linguistics.

Many artificial intelligence systems use sentiment analysis to determine whether language expresses positive, negative, or neutral feelings. Some approaches also draw on the traditional VAD framework to measure emotional meaning.

If that framework does not fully capture the underlying structure of language, systems built around it could overlook important distinctions in what people communicate.

The researchers suggest that incorporating power, danger, and structure could eventually help AI models interpret language more accurately and explain their assessments more clearly. This may be particularly useful when analyzing trust, risk, and decision-making.

In linguistics, the findings offer a different way to understand the foundations of meaning. Instead of treating emotional tone as the primary organizing principle, the new framework emphasizes distinctions related to strength, safety, and predictability.

Psychology could also be affected. Decades of studies have used the VAD model to investigate emotions, perception, and behavior. A different set of fundamental dimensions could encourage researchers to reconsider how some of those findings are interpreted.

The discovery may also interest neuroscientists. The human brain is highly sensitive to threats and signals of safety, and the newly identified linguistic bias suggests that these biological priorities may be reflected in language. This potential connection could help researchers explore relationships between brain activity and symbolic communication, although further research would be needed to establish them.

A New Tool Measures the Hidden Meaning of Words

To investigate these patterns, the researchers created analytical methods designed to measure meaning across enormous collections of written and spoken language.

One of their central innovations is the "ousiometer," a tool that rapidly evaluates the essential meaning of large bodies of text and produces an average meaning score.

(The word "ouisa" comes from Ancient Greek and is a root for the English word "essence.")

The ousiometer builds on an earlier tool developed by the research team called the "hedonometer" (a happiness meter). While that instrument measures the overall emotional tone of language, the new approach examines broader patterns of meaning.

The researchers demonstrated that the ousiometer can analyze material from remarkably different sources, including Jane Austen's novels, Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, the New York Times, Wikipedia, Twitter, and transcripts of talk radio programs.

The tool can also track how meaning shifts as a story unfolds.

For example, the researchers analyzed an English translation of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and mapped its changing meaning throughout the novel.

They called this changing pattern an "ousiometric trajectory." The visualization resembles the twisting shape of a multicolored protein, with the narrative moving across a grid based on four contrasting pairs: dangerous/safe, weak/powerful, gentle/aggressive, and bad/good.

By following this path, researchers can observe how the underlying meaning of the text changes from one section of the story to another.

Why Counting Word Frequency Changes the Results

Another important innovation involved distinguishing between words themselves and how often people actually use them.

In linguistics, researchers describe individual words as categories called "types," while each appearance of a word in text is known as a "token."

(For example, as a category "apple" is a type and every time the word "apple" is used in a sentence is a token.)

This distinction matters because a word that appears millions of times contributes much more to everyday communication than one that is rarely used.

Earlier research often gave each word equal importance, regardless of its frequency. The Vermont team instead incorporated how often words appear in real communication, allowing patterns to emerge that might otherwise remain hidden.

This approach was particularly important for identifying the widespread preference for safety-related language.

The ten-member research team was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, professors in UVM's College of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences. Their collaborators included researchers from the Santa Fe Institute, the Complexity Science Hub in Austria, Howard Hughes Medical Center, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Adelaide, and MassMutual Data Science.

Rethinking What Human Language Reveals About Us

Understanding this hidden safety bias could have consequences for how researchers study communication across society.

If words consistently reflect a preference for safety, that tendency may influence how stories are told, how information spreads, and how people interpret events and experiences.

The findings could eventually inform research into political communication, mental health messaging, and AI systems designed to interact with people.

More broadly, the study challenges the idea that human language can be understood primarily through emotions such as happiness, fear, and excitement.

Instead, the researchers propose that meaning has a deeper structure connected to power, danger, and order. Their findings suggest that language may reflect the challenges humans face when navigating uncertain environments, maintaining relationships, and identifying threats.

By revealing this previously overlooked structure, the team offers a new way to understand what words communicate and why certain kinds of meaning dominate everyday conversation. Human language, their research suggests, may be more closely connected to our fundamental need for safety than scientists have traditionally recognized.

With the Z51 package and the new LS6 6.7-liter V-8 under the hood, how does the updated Stingray compare with the old one?

Welcome to Car and Driver's Testing Hub, where we zoom in on the test numbers. We've been pushing vehicles to their limits since 1956 to provide objective data to bolster our subjective impressions (you can see how we test here).

It should come as no surprise that the 2027 Chevy Corvette Stingray is a performance monster. Every C8 Corvette—hell, just about every Corvette—punches above its weight class in terms of performance, but with the launch of Chevy’s new LS6 small-block V-8, the Stingray is officially a 200-mph car for less than $75,000. Now that we’ve tested the 2027 Stingray, we figured it was worth comparing how the new version stacks up against the old one. Given that our example was the entry-level 1LT trim level, we figured the fairest comparison would be to look at the last 1LT Stingray we tested, which was all the way back in 2020 (the mid-engine C8’s first model year).

As anticipated, the new Stingray is quicker, though not by much. Our tester came equipped with the $5395 Z51 Performance package, which adds Michelin Pilot Sport S5 summer tires, stiffer springs, better cooling, an electronically controlled limited-slip differential, and considerably larger brakes. Add it all together, and the sprint to 60 mph arrives in a mere 2.7 seconds. That’s incredibly quick, but not much quicker than the 2020 car’s 2.8-second run.

Keep your foot in it, though, and the gap widens a bit. The new car hits 100 mph in 6.9 seconds and 150 mph in 18.6 ticks. The previous Z51 required an additional three-tenths to break into the triple digits, and it cracked 150 mph as the stopwatch read 19.4 seconds. The 2027 is quicker through the quarter mile too, rushing through in 11.0 seconds flat at 124 mph vs. the 2020 car’s 11.2-second run at 122 mph.

While the base Stingray is good for an even 200 mph, adding the Z51 package actually dials that figure back to 183 mph—one mph lower than the previous Z51. The top-speed penalty comes from a combination of larger cooling ducts for the brakes, the added drag from the front splitter and rear wing, and, perhaps most importantly, a shorter 5.56:1 final-drive ratio (same as in the Z06), which hampers its overall top speed.

On the one hand, the Z51 package limits top speed. On the other hand, it makes up for it in the braking department. Stopping from 70 mph happens in just 149 feet for both the new and old Stingrays. When stopping from 100 mph, the newer car took 297 feet, versus the older car’s 301 feet, basically a dead heat.

Both cars wore 245-series front and 305-series rear Michelin Pilot Sport S tires, while the 2020 car wore the 4S and the ’27 gets the newer S5 rubber. The 2027 car gave up at 1.02 g on our skidpad, making the new version negligibly less grippy than the 2020 car, which pulled 1.03 g. Our 2027 tester tipped our scales at 3702 pounds, some 55 pounds heavier than our 2020 tester, which never helps when it comes to performance.

Unsurprisingly, given the 2027 Stingray’s additional pint of displacement, the new Vette is a touch less efficient than the old one. The LS6-equipped Stingray drops by one mpg across the board compared with the LT2-equipped car. Based on EPA estimates, that comes out to 18 mpg combined, 15 mpg highway, and 25 mpg on the highway.

But the latest Corvette more than makes up for any fuel-economy slide with a lumpy idle and glorious V-8 soundtrack. At idle, we measured 53 decibels, a whopping 6 dBA higher than the 2020 car. #5492. NHTSA proposes cyan lights for robotaxis, following Mercedes/China

The NHTSA has proposed using cyan-colored lights to identify vehicles operating autonomously, along with other additions to get ready for autonomous vehicles.

As the autonomous vehicles start to spread on US roads, questions have come up about how to deal with these new driverless vehicles.

One question involves emergency response – not only do the vehicles need to be prepared to respond to emergency personnel, those personnel need to recognize when a car is operating autonomously. A police officer needs to know that nobody’s in the driver seat, or an emergency responder needs to know that if there’s nobody in the driver’s seat, that’s not necessarily because there’s a missing driver somewhere who needs rescue.

These are just some of the reasons one might want to know whether a car is operating autonomously, but as of yet, there is no standardized messaging to suggest that a vehicle is doing so.

This week, NHTSA proposed an answer. In a draft proposal released Friday, it suggested a whole new color for vehicle lamps, cyan, to signal autonomous operation.

The proposal was actually first put forward by Mercedes, alongside its DRIVE PILOT level 3 driver assist system. This is the first true hands-free system offered in the US, which allows drivers to forgo responsibility for the car under certain conditions, and let the car truly drive itself. This differs from systems like Tesla’s Full Self-Driving, which is actually only a “level 2” system, where the driver is still responsible when the system is on.

We tried Mercedes’ system out in Santa Monica traffic, and were impressed at how quickly we adjusted to our temporary robotic chauffer.

When we saw that system, we also saw Mercedes’ proposal for lighting: cyan lights around the exterior of the vehicle, denoting autonomous operation.

Unfortunately our demo was on a bright sunny day (a necessity to operate the level 3 system, which doesn’t run at night or in bad weather), so the lights didn’t pop as much as they might have in lower light, but you get the drift.

Mercedes trialed the system in California for two years, and it seems the NHTSA is happy with the results, since it is proposing to take the idea national (after input from the SAE, which also likes the idea of using cyan).

The new draft regulation would allow for optional inclusion of cyan lights, but would not require them. So, it’s entirely possible that automakers would decide to save the small cost that it would take to engineer and include these lights.

But by putting the idea out into the regulations, it gets everyone thinking about a future where this color might be required (and I could see that requirement coming quite quickly) – so automakers are likely to start thinking of where these lights would go, and including them, since LEDs don’t really cost anything anyway.

The regulation states that the lights should turn on when the car is operating autonomously, and turn off when it isn’t – they should never be on when a driver is actively driving the vehicle. They should all turn on and off simultaneously, and they should be visible from any angle, with markers on the front, sides and rear, all at least 15 inches off the ground.

China just announced essentially the same regulation earlier this week. The country released a draft standard that would take effect in 2028, is optional, and uses turquoise as its proposed color. This, too, was a reaction to the proliferation of lights on autonomous vehicles in China, and the government has asked automakers to stop doing their own thing and instead follow the new standardized system.

#552. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

From the March 1979 issue of Car and Driver.

Japanese manufacturers are building the best small American cars money can buy. Firms like Datsun and Subaru cling to products that are more in the Japa­nese tradition, but Toyota, Mazda, Mit­subishi, and Honda are cranking out trick little cars that would warm the cockles of a Detroit product planner’s heart. Of these, Honda’s Accord is the best. It is also, alas, the most expensive, but it is a class act and a car that could probably solve the transportation prob­lems of at least 75 percent of the people currently driving around on the high­ways of the good old U.S. of A.

The great trading companies of Japan have apparently decided that the world wants cars combining the size and weight of European econoboxes with the cushy velour and gadget features of traditional American bargain-cruisers, and the sales of Japanese cars in the Western world would seem to support them in that point of view.

In almost ev­ery case, things like ride, handling, roadholding, and interior accommoda­tions are much more like those of the best American cars than the best Euro­pean cars. Honda’s effort is a bit more European. Somehow Honda has made the Accord in such a way that it appeals to both foreign-car enthusiasts and American-car loyalists in this market. It’s also possible that none of the above speculation is true. Maybe people from all walks of life and every automotive persuasion buy Hondas simply because they’re terrific little cars, and never mind all the punditry about American­-European-Japanese blendings of auto­motive philosophies. Whatever it is, Honda is a remarkably successful car in this country, in spite of the fact that it costs the very earth to buy.

This new Accord four-door looks and feels like a mini-Mercedes. All it lacks is torque. If it had the dig of a Ford Fiesta, it would be perfect. It carries four American adults in remarkable comfort, it cruises at 75 or 80 effortlessly and quietly, and its ride is smooth and sup­ple. It doesn’t handle or hold the road as well as a Rabbit or a Fiesta, but it’s better than most others, either Japanese or European. Ironically, Detroit simply hasn’t yet come up with anything to touch this most American of imported cars. The Chevette is a great little trouper in its way, but one could “LX” the living hell out of a Chevette and still not have a Honda Accord. The Accord is more than fancy trim and ingenious extras; the Accord is a better idea. If the price strikes you as mini-Mercedes too, then consider the Honda Civic: another terrific little car, still a bargain, that has been somewhat overshadowed by its snazzy big brother.

One’s first visual impression of the new Accord four-door sedan is a kind of Teutonic seriousness and restraint in the styling. Like the Accord coupe, this Honda eschews the “Gee, Dad, it’s a Wurlitzer!” look that characterizes so many Japanese cars. Available only in silver or burgundy, it is quite hand­some, and that handsomeness—along with the strong signal of quality it con­veys—kind of sets you up for the price sticker. “You” in this case would have to be a knowledgeable car enthusiast, however. That culturally deprived seg­ment of American society that still be­lieves that small cars are, by definition, cheap and chintzy will choke on the price and never come to know all the goodies that await on the other side of the sticker.

Those goodies are many and varied. Much of the deluxe “LX” trim from the coupe has been incorporated in the se­dan’s appointments, and this helps con­vey a feeling of BMW/Mercedes luxury rather than Rabbit/Fiesta corner-cut­ting. Standard equipment includes an AM/FM radio, steel-belted radials, a ta­chometer, a trick warning system that lights up in the center of the instrument panel to tell you if any door or the deck lid is left ajar or if the brake lights aren’t working, a maintenance reminder, a fuel-reserve warning light, fully reclin­ing front bucket seats, rear-seat head­rests, a remote trunk-lid release, a trunk light, a vanity mirror, and lighted ashtrays in both rear doors and the right front door. There’s also a handy, con­sole-mounted lock for the rear doors, with its own warning light. Sitting down, closing the door, fas­tening your harness, and starting the engine all convey cumulative sensa­tions of well-being and reassurance.

The look of German quality also comes through as a quality feel. The five-speed transmission’s shift linkage is positive and accurate. The power steering, with variable boost, is the same. Every con­trol, whether push-pull, twist, or slide, works with commendable economy of effort. Headlights operate at a twist of the turn-signal knob; high-beams and overtaking flasher are on the same stalk. The windshield-wiper-and-washer control, with two speeds plus an intermit­tent mode, is on a similar stalk, 180 de­grees away, on the right side of the steering column. Seat adjustments for both fore-and-aft movement and back­rest angle accommodate drivers and passengers up to 6 feet 3 inches without infringing unduly on the rear passen­gers’ legroom. Front headroom was suf­ficient for the writer wearing anything but his really serious cowboy hat, but rear headroom is less generous. The front seats themselves conform to the accepted fashion in European buckets, being both comfortable and orthope­dically correct, and the rear seat is bet­ter than many in much larger cars. The plush upholstery fabric, the same shade of dark red in all cars, is soft and warm to the touch, while the seats it covers are quite firm.

Driving and riding posi­tions are generally pleasant and non-­fatiguing—even on one long and tense junket through 200 miles of pea-soup fog and slippery two-lane. Any drive of more than ten minutes’ duration in the Honda should suffice to make a small-car believer out of all but the most hidebound big-car loyalist. There’s no doubt that you’re in a small car—the wheelbase is unchanged from the coupe, though overall length is in­creased by eight inches—yet the anthro­pometric envelope that surrounds each occupant is as comfortable and commo­dious as he’d have found in a traditional Ford or Chevy intermediate a few years ago. The driver is well placed before his instruments and controls, and visibility front and rear is better than average. The engine is very quiet. With only 1751 cc displacement (an increase of roughly 10 percent) and 72 horsepower at 4500 rpm, one might expect it to be a little noisy in its work, but it hums along with minimum fuss and actually sounds quite pleasant.

The only glitch we discovered in the performance of our five-speed-manual­-equipped test car was a definite flat spot at low revs. Beyond 2500 rpm the engine pulled nicely all the way to its 6200 red­line, but getting away smoothly in first was never easy—slipping the clutch was mandatory—and dumping it into fifth gear below 3000 rpm gave the distinct impression that its little Japanese heart had stopped beating. Full throttle and lots of revs eliminated the problem, and the car fairly sings under that kind of treatment—even though it really isn’t what you’d call quick—but unfortunate­ly that isn’t the way most people drive. After you’ve spent a full day behind the wheel, the knowledge that it’s probably going to balk when you let the clutch out at the next traffic light starts to be­come obsessive, and the problem gets worse and worse.

Serious high-speed motoring is not the sedan’s strong suit. Heavier than the coupe by 95 pounds, it’ll cruise all day at speeds that would cause the National Safety Council cardiac arrest, and it’ll do it with less fuss and bother than a lot of highly touted imported and domestic “performance” machines, but it really wants to be treated gently. Wailing over the bumps and through the hair­pins is okay once in a while, but the Ac­cord really prefers to show its stuff when you take the family to Grandma’s house or carpool three friends to the office. Used the way most Americans use their cars, it is superb. The ride is smooth, everything works, and the lug­gage space is ample. The Honda does what the average guy always felt he needed a standard American car to do, but it does it with charm and flair.

As is our wont, we drove the Accord pretty hard, in spite of what I’ve just said. The most interesting day for us, and the most challenging for the Hon­da, was one Sunday when we drove clear across the state to sample the product of an aspiring young winemak­er. When we left home at sunrise, the roads were covered with ice. When we turned off the Interstate and onto the back-country roads, we were confronted with four to six inches of fresh snow and slush. It didn’t take much time or too many sips to prove our prejudices about Michigan wine—it was kerosene—so we tramped through the snow-covered vineyards, ate icy-cold unpicked grapes, which were as good as the wine was ghastly, and got back on the slippery roads. We skipped the Interstates alto­gether for the trip home, and that was negotiated in near impenetrable fog.

For the first half of this adventure the Honda’s running mate was a brand-new Mazda RX-7, which was sideways most of the time and actually went off the road twice. During all this sturm und drang the little Accord never put a foot wrong. It cruised along without ever breathing hard, towed along by its transverse engine and front-wheel drive as true as a die, fully capable of running off and leaving the Super-Omigod Maz­da in a cloud of ice particles anytime the driver wanted to put his foot down.

How much of this superiority was Hon­da and how much of it was simply due to the well-known advantages of front­-wheel drive might be debatable, but the three people in the Honda were too busy enjoying themselves to think about it much. We were safe and secure and comfortable in a little car with a volcanic heater, efficient wipers and washers, a fine radio, and roadholding and traction that absolutely would not quit in the ice and snow. It was a memorable day for three car freaks, and we’ll never forget the Honda because of it.

It’s a real shame that all these minia­turized benefits cost so much money. As good as the Accord sedan admittedly is, it cannot represent more than about $5000 in genuine consumer value. All the rest is politics, economics, and mon­etary fluctuations. However, car people make no bones about their own imprac­ticality when it comes to falling in love with cars. If you drive the Accord sedan, and it works as well for you as it did for us, you’ll probably decide to fork over the $6365, even as you’re telling your­self that that's too much to pay for an economy sedan.

I’ve always been of the opinion that the smaller the car, the more each passenger needs his own door. And since the Ac­cord is one of the smaller cars extant, I’m happy to see that Honda has obliged me in this regard. Now I can enjoy an Accord without so much as the inconvenience of bending forward when one of my passengers disembarks.

Adding two more doors brings the Ac­cord even closer to being one car that pleases all people. But what’s more amaz­ing is that the Accord doesn’t achieve its universal appeal by being innocuous. Instead, it’s bursting with good-natured personality and sheer roadway talent. Here is a car that’s good basic transporta­tion: reliable, economical, and comfort­able. It’s also luxurious and stylish. And on top of that it’s quick and agile—very much a driver’s car. That it also happens to be eminently affordable brings us back to the subject of doors. In most respects, this Honda blows everyone else’s off. —Rich Ceppos

The interior of this new Accord offers four good-sized grown-ups the chance to enter, each through his own door, and be seated amidst a multitude of clever Hon­da comfort and convenience touches. And the exterior has the look of a mini­-Mercedes, to boot. But this little rooster offers even more. The power steering is light at low speeds, masking front-wheel-­drive pull, and gets firmer as you go fast­er. The handling, with surprisingly little understeer, is downright pleasing, and feels better than that of early Accord coupes. Moreover, when Old Man Winter retches up slush, snow, and glare ice, the Accord will pull through. If Accords, one each, had been issued to Napoleon’s troops for the holiday season in Mother Russia, his descendants would rule the world today.

Gripes? The pedals are too close when your reach to the wheel is right, and the shifter is a little rubbery. That’s as long as my complaint list gets. There are a nun­ber of little four-doors around, but few with the Accord’s prime blend of hospi­tality that invites you in to take a seat any­where you'd like. —Larry Griffin

Oh boy, do I like this little beauty. Well, actually, I shouldn’t call it a beauty. Its exterior styling is a little too bland for my tastes, although the shape is obviously well suited for its assigned task of carry­ing four passengers. Form can successful­ly follow function, you see. And that goes for the interior as well. Clean, informative instrument panel, good driving position, good visibility, and tasteful but subdued luxury. I didn't like the power steering, which was too light for my tastes, and I was disappointed with the lack of power. But overall, this is easily one of the best small sedans ever. Honda showed us it knows how to make cars with the Civic, proved itself again with the Accord coupe, and now has gone three-for-three with the four-door. Let's hope the long-­rumored 2+2 sports car extends the win­ning streak. —Mike Knepper
