#1116. Paramount and Warner Bros merge into one Hollywood giant

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Paramount has completed an $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery, as its owner Skydance creates a new entertainment behemoth. Billionaire David Ellison's hostile takeover bid defeated rival efforts from Netflix.

Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#1036. Nobel Prize in literature goes to Canada's Anne Carson

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Carson won for her "bold and inventive oeuvre." The decorated poet and essayist is known for her reverence for Latin and Greek classics.

The Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature to Anne Carson on Thursday, praising her "bold and inventive oeuvre."

Her work has "in playful dialogue with the classical tradition...created new forms for contemporary literature," said Ingrid Carlberg, permanent secretary of the academy.

The 76-year-old writer is a longtime professor of classics, creative writing, and comparative literature at prestigious institutions like McGill and Princeton universities. She is only the 19th woman of 123 laureates to win the award.

Born in Toronto in 1950, Carson moved with her family among several small towns in Canada during her youth. She has credited a beloved Latin teacher for sparking her interest in language and classics.

Her first book of criticism, 1986’s Eros the Bittersweet, was published to great acclaim and has had significant influence on other classicists, poets, and even film and television. Carson has also published her own poetry such as the 2001 collection The Beauty of the Husband as well as highly regarded translation of ancient Greek poets such as Sappho and Sophocles.

Carson is also heavily influenced by the King James Bible and more modern writers including Emily Dickinson and Virginia Woolf. She has received numerous awards such as the T.S. Eliot Prize for Poetry, a MacArthur fellowship, and the PEN/Nabokov Award.

Deeply private, Carson often declines to speak about her personal life in interviews and discourages autobiographical interpretations of her work. However, she did reveal in 2024 that she has Parkinson's disease.

The winner of the Nobel Prize in literature receives prize money of 12 million Swedish kronor, equivalent to about $1.2 million. The winner also receives a medal and a diploma at an official ceremony in December, and is expected to deliver a speech known as a Nobel lecture.

On Friday, the winner of the Nobel Peace Prize will be announced, followed by the Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on Monday.

#3674. How Two Rival Columnists Created Hollywood’s Gossip Machine — and Taught America to Worship Attention

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In her new book, “Hollywood Whispers: The Untold Story of the Original Gossip Queens, Louella Parsons and Hedda Hopper,” Eileen Cope delves into a rivalry that dramatically shaped the entertainment industry and culture at large throughout the mid-20th century. After the birth of the star system in Hollywood, Parsons and Hopper rose to prominence as gossip columnists who could make and break careers, and choose to keep or expose celebrities’ secrets. With a combined readership of 75 million at their peak, Hopper and Parsons wielded extraordinary influence — and instilled paralyzing fear. As columnist Liz Smith has written, “The studios created both of them. And they thought they could control both of them. Hollywood has always been a hall of mirrors. What began as an industry of light and illusion had, by the 1940s, become a machinery of control—an empire built on fantasy, running on fear. Its dreammakers no longer needed scripts or cameras to keep order. They had Louella Parsons and Hedda Hopper.

By then, both women had transcended the ordinary limits of journalism. They were not simply reporters; they were gatekeepers of belief, the quiet regulators of a nation’s imagination. To cross them was to risk vanishing—not merely from the screen, but from the collective story America told about itself.

Hollywood insiders referred to them—half jokingly, half in dread—as “the High Priestesses.” Studio secretaries clipped their columns before the morning dailies reached the lot, marking any mention of the studio’s stars with red pencil. Publicists phoned assistants in panic if a name appeared in the wrong context. “They could wreck you before lunch,” remembered producer Pandro Berman. “And by dinner, they’d make you again—if it suited them.”

It was easy, at first, to mistake their power for personality. Louella, with her sanctimonious charm and the backing of William Randolph Hearst, ruled the studios from her desk at the Los Angeles Examiner. Her typewriter was a throne. Her column ran like clockwork through America’s breakfast tables, a sermon of what was right and who had sinned. Hedda, the actress-turned-crusader, cultivated a different tone—brassy, righteous, vengeful. Her voice carried across radio waves like a call to arms. She didn’t just write gossip; she announced it, her cadence turning every name into an accusation or an absolution.

Both women understood theater. Louella played the role of moral mother, her column written in the soothing cadences of propriety—rarely venomous on the surface, yet devastating in its implications. Hedda was the showwoman. “When she walked into Ciro’s,” recalled gossip photographer Frank Worth, “it was like someone had switched on the lights.” Even those who loathed her found themselves glancing up, afraid she might be looking at them.

Louella and Hedda were two sides of the same coin, minted in a culture that worshipped celebrity and feared moral chaos. Each understood what the studios feared most: scandal, exposure, the sudden rupture of the illusion. And they learned to trade in that fear, turning rumor into regulation.

Louella had built her empire first. By the early 1930s, her column appeared in hundreds of newspapers through the Hearst syndicate, reaching an estimated twenty million readers daily. Hollywood Hotel, her weekly radio show, brought the glamour of opening nights directly into American living rooms. Its mix of studio orchestras, star appearances, and previews of upcoming films created a powerful cycle of cross-promotion: her print column drove listeners to the broadcast, and the broadcast reinforced her authority in print.

She was feared as much as courted. Stars who crossed her found themselves frozen out. Studio executives who annoyed her might find their films receiving tepid coverage. A single line hinting that an actor appeared “unhappy with recent assignments” could trigger studio meetings and contract reviews. Louella’s prose was brief, polite, and devastatingly coded; her readers learned to interpret her tone as carefully as her text. The fear she inspired was not only of what she printed but of what she might choose not to.

The competition she could not have predicted came in the form of a woman she had once treated as a harmless curiosity. By 1935, Hedda Hopper’s film career was essentially over. She was fifty, though she claimed forty-five, watching her savings evaporate and terrified about her future. When a studio executive suggested she could “transition to extra work,” Hedda turned the humiliation into fuel. If she couldn’t star in movies, she would make herself the story. The weapon she would wield was not beauty or youth, but the pen.

When a friend suggested she try writing a gossip column, Hedda was insulted. Writing gossip seemed like a public acknowledgment that she couldn’t make it as a performer. But desperation has a way of making humiliation seem acceptable. She borrowed a typewriter, set up at her kitchen table, and began by writing what she knew best: the petty rivalries of actors who had underestimated her. “I never lied,” she said later, “but I didn’t always tell the truth either.” The early columns were messy but alive, full of energy and vengeance.

Then Hedda got angry. She stopped trying to write like a journalist and started writing like herself— sharp, critical, unsparing, willing to say things other columnists wouldn’t dare print. Twenty years as a working actress had also given her something valuable: relationships with the people Louella ignored. Character actors, crew members, hairdressers, chauffeurs, and wardrobe girls had no loyalty to Louella, and many were happy to talk to Hedda, who had once been one of them.

The Los Angeles Times noticed. Hearst’s primary competitor in Southern California saw Hedda as a weapon against his dominance through Louella. In 1938, the paper offered Hedda a contract with better pay, more resources, and a much larger platform. She would compete directly with the woman who had been Hollywood’s undisputed gossip queen for fifteen years.

Most people thought she would be crushed. Instead, Hedda transformed herself for the opportunity. She began wearing the enormous hats that became her trademark—creations decorated with feathers, flowers, fruit, and even stuffed animals. The hats ensured that everyone at a Hollywood event knew when Hedda Hopper had arrived. She also developed a confrontational style that contrasted with Louella’s cozy approach. Where Louella praised, Hedda criticized. Where Louella protected relationships, Hedda burned bridges. Where Louella sought to be loved, Hedda was content to be feared.

In the beginning, the difference between them seemed simple enough. Louella had the empire; Hedda had the instinct. Louella’s authority came from her link to Hearst—a patriarchal structure she both served and mastered. Hedda’s came from sheer velocity. She moved fast, wrote fast, spoke fast. She had learned that outrage was more contagious than affection.

At first, the two women coexisted uneasily, even politely. Louella sent Hedda a congratulatory telegram when her first radio broadcast went national. Hedda responded with a bouquet of orchids and a note that read, “To the Queen of the Kingdom I am only visiting.” But the civility evaporated as soon as Hedda’s column began pulling readers away from the Examiner. In 1940, when Hedda’s column syndication reached more than six million households, Louella persuaded Hearst’s editors to bar her rival from every Hearst-owned paper.

As the 1940s unfolded, their feud became public theater. Columns answered columns. Louella hinted that Hedda was “overly dramatic.” Hedda fired back, calling Louella “a relic of studio servitude.” Studios courted both women, sometimes feeding them different versions of the same story to see who would publish first. Readers began to choose sides.

In 1945, Photoplay published “Battle of the Typewriters,” complete with caricatures of the two women as dueling queens—Louella seated on a gilt throne, Hedda brandishing her hatpins like weapons. The story outsold every issue that year except the one featuring Clark Gable’s return from the war. The public couldn’t get enough. For a nation recovering from global conflict, the spectacle of two powerful women waging a cold war of words was irresistible.

Their methods echo through the media that followed. The tabloid boom of the 1980s, TMZ, Instagram, celebrity influencers, blind-item accounts, and the daily social-media outrage cycle all trace their lineage to the template Louella and Hedda built. They understood that access could be traded, intimacy manufactured, morality weaponized, and a public image constructed or destroyed through repetition. The machine they built continues to hum behind every red carpet and every scandal recycled for entertainment. What they truly enshrined was attention—and America never stopped worshiping it.

The women themselves could not stop Hollywood from moving on. As the studio system declined and stars emerged, the two gossip queens became relics of the world they had once governed. When the Los Angeles Examiner folded in 1962, Louella suffered a professional blow. Already showing signs of physical deterioration, she nevertheless continued going out, bejeweled and bewildered, like a dowager empress whose country had overthrown her rule. She finally retired in 1965, beset by further medical problems.

Hedda had always advised leaving a party before the glow faded. On February 1, 1966, she died in Los Angeles of double pneumonia at the age of eighty. Her cryptic parting words were entirely in character: “They should know what I haven’t written!”

Louella’s daughter, Harriet, visited her mother at the Santa Monica nursing home to deliver the news that Hedda Hopper was dead. Louella sat in profound silence, then appeared confused. She would live for another six years, largely withdrawn from the world she had once ruled, before dying of arteriosclerosis on December 9, 1972, at the age of ninety-one. But on hearing that her great rival had finally departed, Louella gathered herself long enough to offer one last, unmistakable verdict: “GOOD!”

#3738. A Rod Serling War Story Led To One Of Paul Newman's Most Underrated Movies

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Arnold Laven's 1956 military courtroom drama "The Rack" isn't terribly well-remembered these days. If it's remarked upon at all, it may be for its performance from Paul Newman in one of his earliest film roles. It's also notable because its screenplay, by Stewart Stern, was adapted from a 1955 episode of the theatrical anthology series "The United States Steel Hour," which was penned by Rod Serling. Said episode, also called "The Rack," originally starred Marshall Thompson in the central role of Edward H. Hall Jr., a U.S. military Captain who has been accused of collaborating with the enemy during the Korean War.

In the movie version of "The Rack," Newman plays Captain Hall, while Walter Pidgeon plays Hall's father, a retired colonel. Hall returns from the war, having survived in a POW camp for a spell, and is mourning the loss of his brother, Peter, in combat. It's soon revealed that Captain Hall is about to be court-martialed for collaborating with the enemy. When Hall's father confronts him on the matter, he instantly admits that yes, he did collaborate with the enemy.

During the subsequent trial, it's revealed that Hall followed enemy instructions, making speeches and signing documents for them. Other American soldiers, meanwhile, withstood torture rather than giving aid to the opposing side. Hall is accused of being a coward. He wishes to plead guilty. Naturally, there is more to this story than initially implicated. The climax of the movie then reveals the horrors that Hall suffered at the hands of the enemy. However bad you think it is, dear readers, it's probably worse. Newman's performance is frank, tormented, and very good, and "The Rack" itself is one of the many underrated Paul Newman movies you should check out.

The "The Rack" was clearly designed to explore the horrors of war. When one goes back to the original Rod Serling teleplay, one can clearly see that "The Rack" is meant to be a condemnation of war violence, detailing just how horrible combat and wartime torture are. One can further read an essay on the Rod Serling website detailing Serling's PTSD and how he feared becoming a monster after fighting in World War II. Men his age had a unique set of phobias and concerns about combat. "The Rack" was clearly a means to explore Serling's fears.

The hour-long 1955 TV version of "The Rack" has been handily archived by the UCLA Film & Television Archive and can be accessed on YouTube. One will see immediately that the TV version is far more melodramatic than the movie iteration and features bigger, showier speeches. "I sold my soul for a filthy blanket that stunk of fish and urine!" Marshall Thompson's Captain Hall yells. "And three hours of uninterrupted sleep! And you know what else, Colonel? At the time, I felt it was one hell of a bargain!"

Thompson is a more demonstrative actor than Paul Newman, so one cannot even really compare their performances. It's like night and day. Thompson plays every emotion enormously, allowing his Captain Hall to emerge as wounded and pathetic. Newman, however, underplays everything, which makes the character richer and more intriguing. He's someone who knows how deeply his sins run.

As for the film version of "The Rack," one can find a vintage New York Times review, written by the inimitable Bosley Crowther, that praised Newman's performance. At the same time, though, Crowther disliked the movie's screenplay, describing it as "thin."

Having watched some of both versions of "The Rack," I can say that the TV version is way preachier. Rod Serling, as we know from his most famous creation, "The Twilight Zone," wasn't always a subtle author, preferring to get to the point.

Both iterations of "The Rack" were notable career turning points for Serling and Paul Newman, in turn. It was only after writing the TV version of "The Rack" that Sterling began contributing more heartily to the TV series "Playhouse 90," and, later on, writing screenplays for movies like "Patterns," "Saddle the Wind," and 1968's "Planet of the Apes." Then, "The Twilight Zone" kicked off in 1959, putting Serling on the American cultural map in perpetuity.

Newman, meanwhile, was a rising star when the big screen version of "The Rack" was released. He had previously been in the films "Somebody Up There Likes Me" and "The Silver Chalice," a disastrous flop that Newman once called his worst movie ever. (Seriously, it's bad.) Similarly, he had only made his TV debut a few years before on the underrated sci-fi anthology show "Tales of Tomorrow" (namely, in the 1952 episode "Ice from Space").

From there, of course, Newman continued to act for decades. No matter when you grew up in the time that followed, you were most likely introduced to him as a child, whether it was through his performance in "Cool Hand Luke" in the 1960s, "The Sting" in the 1970s, "The Color of Money" in the 1980s, "Nobody's Fool" in the 1990s, or even "Cars" in the 2000s. The man lived quite the life.

"The Hunger Games: The Sunrise on the Reaping" is coming to theaters soon, and fans immersed in the world of the Hunger Games — specifically, the transformed North American continent known as Panem under a totalitarian regime — will finally get to see Suzanne Collins' 2025 book play out on screen. I recently spoke to Francis Lawrence, the director of every single Hunger Games adaptation aside from the first one (which was helmed by Gary Ross), about one of the most essential bonds in this story: namely, the one between our protagonist Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) and his fellow District 12 tribute Maysilee Donner (McKenna Grace).

As Lawrence revealed, he and his actors kept some things close to the chest. "There were a lot of conversations with both Joe and McKenna because we started with the games, and so a lot of our lead-up to shooting was rehearsals of fights, then action sequences and things in Spain where we were shooting," the director said. "And so I had the two of them a lot. And so we would just talk constantly about the two of them." He continued:

"Quite honestly, I think the three of us have some secrets about their relationship. I've just always found it really interesting that when she sees him, she slams the door in his face and they've known each other their entire lives. And I think there's probably other elements, too, to their story that Suzanne hasn't written about. So we all have our own secrets that we use to get underneath what the characters are and how they relate to one another and why they may have conflict and why they bond and all of that. So that stuff's really fun. And they're great at it because they're both great actors."

If you're about to yell "spoiler alert!" at me, calm down; the relationship between Haymitch and Maysilee is actually revealed in Suzanne Collins' first literary sequel, "Catching Fire," which was published back in 2009. As that trilogy's heroes and District 12 tributes Katniss Everdeen and Peeta Mellark (played on-screen by Jennifer Lawrence and Josh Hutcherson) prepare to head back into the arena for the Third Quarter Quell — a super-evil edition of the Hunger Games, which puts former victors back into the arena — they watch footage of the 50th Games, where Haymitch and Maysilee formed an alliance against double the number of tributes. That's how they watch Maysilee's bitter end: "Haymitch runs for her, anyway. He arrives only in time to watch the last of a flock of candy pink birds, equipped with long, thin beaks, skewer her through the neck. He holds her hand while she dies."

Maysilee is also important to Katniss in a fully unexpected way. As Peeta points out, her mockingjay pin came from the Donner family, and Maysilee's abandoned twin Merrilee (the mother of Katniss's friend Madge), who "spends half her life in bed immobilized with terrible pain, shutting out the world." Katniss continues:

"I think of how I never realized that she and my mother shared this connection. Of Madge showing up in that snowstorm to bring the painkiller for Gale. Of my mockingjay pin and how it means something completely different now that I know that its former owner was Madge's aunt, Maysilee Donner, a tribute who was murdered in the arena."

The entire premise of the Hunger Games is disgusting on several levels, but something particularly insidious about them — and something that Suzanne Collins explores in each and every book she writes — is the fact that the kids pitted against each other in the arena end up forming bonds. With four District 12 tributes in Haymitch and Maysilee's games instead of two, Haymitch initially tries to avoid any alliances. But the youngest of the group, Louella McCoy (Molly McCann in the upcoming film), already has a long-standing relationship with him characterized by Haymitch's protective feelings for the girl. When Louella ends up at the center of a tragic accident, the sickest thing in the entire Hunger Games franchise to date happens, Louella is replaced by a brainwashed lookalike, Lou Lou (Iona Bell), who's forced to compete in her stead.

Francis Lawrence told me that he "[mapped out] the relationships" between Haymitch and his fellow tributes, and because Haymitch and Louella were playground "sweethearts," he made sure to sketch that out. Beyond that, though, he pointed out the parallels between Louella and Katniss, both of whom are young brunettes that favor braids and find themselves in the arena. "There's the connections that if you know the other stories and movies, that you might make of Louella with the braid and Katniss, calling her sweetheart, and calling Katniss sweetheart and all of those kinds of things, which are a bit Easter eggy if you don't really know the books and things, but all that stuff is fun," Lawrence mused.

"The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping" arrives in theaters on November 20, 2026 — and you'll see all of these relationships play out on the big screen then.

#3758. Decades Before Landman, Billy Bob Thornton Was In A Sitcom Featuring A TV Legend

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Billy Bob Thornton might be 71 years old, but as his Paramount+ series "Landman" proves, he hasn't missed a step. Taylor Sheridan's oil drama has been the perfect showcase for Thornton's considerable talents, and despite the fact "Landman" has yet to earn any Emmy nominations, the show has been consistently well received, with Thornton garnering Actor Award and Critics Choice Award nominations. Long before he reminded us all of his acting prowess, however, Thornton appeared on a very different TV series when he supported sitcom royalty and the future co-star of his film "Sling Blade," John Ritter, on the early 1990s CBS sitcom "Hearts Afire."

The show was created by Linda Bloodworth-Thomason, who'd previously written for such well-known shows as "M*A*S*H" and had contributed an episode to "Rhonda" (one of many Emmy-winning sitcoms that we don't really talk about anymore). Of course, she remains best known for her 1980s/'90s sitcoms "Designing Women" and the Burt Reynolds-led "Evening Shade." In the case of "Hearts Afire," Bloodworth-Thomason teamed up with another established star in Ritter, who famously played Jack Tripper on the legendary ABC sitcom "Three's Company" between 1977 and 1984. She also drafted in Markie Post of "The Fall Guy" and "Night Court" fame.

The two stars play politically opposed individuals who develop an attraction to one another over the course of the show's first season. Thornton, meanwhile, plays Ritter's best friend, who divorces his wife and manages to stick around for the entire three seasons of "Hearts Afire," despite a major cast shakeup mid-way through.

In 1996, Billy Bob Thornton cast John Ritter as Vaughan Cunningham in "Sling Blade," the Oscar-winning movie that Thornton claims changed his life. By that point, the pair had worked together for three years on "Hearts Afire," and according to a 2003 Herald Times article, Thornton took the opportunity between takes on the sitcom to "crack Ritter up with the mentally challenged hillbilly character he'd use four years later as the centerpiece of 'Sling Blade.'" During their time on "Hearts Afire," then, Thornton and Ritter developed a close relationship that lasted until Ritter's passing in 2003.

At the time Thornton was cast on "Hearts Afire," he was still an up-and-coming actor. He'd made his screen debut in 1986's "Hunter's Blood" and racked up some small roles in TV shows including "Matlock" and Linda Bloodworth-Thomason's "Evening Shade." Thus, when Bloodworth-Thomason set to work on "Hearts Afire," she thought of Thornton.

The show stars Ritter as John Hartman, a father of two whose wife has recently left him for her therapist. John is also a conservative senator's (George Gaynes) aide whose political views aren't exactly aligned with those of Georgie Anne Lahti (Markie Post), a reporter who hopes to become the senator's new press secretary. Nonetheless, the two are drawn to each other.

Thornton, meanwhile, played Ritter's friend Billy Bob Davis. (He'd previously used his real name in "Hunter's Blood" and the pilot for a show called "Circus" as well.) In the series premiere, Billy Bob is kicked out of his house by his wife, Mavis (Wendie Jo Sperber). Meanwhile, Georgie moves in with John, and the first season follows their developing romance and experiences in Washington D.C. But when Season 2 arrived, the show underwent a major retooling.

"Hearts Afire" Season 1 debuted in September 1992 and ran for 23 episodes, ending in March 1993. When it returned for a second season in October of the same year, it was a different show. According to a contemporaneous report from Deseret News, the series' first season was "both too political and often of questionable taste" due to multiple jokes that were "completely inappropriate for an early time slot." Whatever the case, CBS decided the show needed reworking, and in the Season 2 premiere, the now-married John Hartman and Georgie Anne Lahti decide to move back to John's home town in the midwest with John's two children. There, they buy the local newspaper and attempt to create a wholesome life for their family in a town that John often doesn't recognize from his childhood.

While the political aspect of the show was abandoned along with several characters, Billy Bob Thornton's Billy Bob Davis made it to the second season, moving with the couple alongside his mother, Velma Davis (Maxine Stuart). It was a good thing the producers kept him on, too. As the Deseret News put it, Thornton's "deadpan observations and dry wit steal almost every scene he's in."

"Hearts Afire" ran for two more seasons, wrapping up after its third season in February 1995. During that time, the show benefited from the presence of another towering TV figure, Ed Asner, who played Georgie's father, George Lahti, in 12 episodes of the series. While the show didn't continue past Season 3, Thornton and Ritter's relationship very much did, with the pair re-teaming for "Sling Blade" and reuniting once again when Ritter was cast alongside Thornton in "Bad Santa."

#3785. How The Red Rising Movie Will Differ From Hunger Games And The Long Walk

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Francis Lawrence might not be a director whose name you know offhand, but you've definitely seen his work. Not only has Lawrence helmed projects like "I Am Legend" and "Water for Elephants," but aside from the first film in the Hunger Games franchise, which was directed by Gary Ross, Lawrence has directed every single film in the franchise, including the first prequel "The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes" and the forthcoming Haymitch Abernathy origin story "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping." (That character was originated by Woody Harrelson in the Hunger Games trilogy, and is played by Joseph Zada in the prequel.) I recently got the chance to speak to Lawrence about his recent string of dystopian movies focused on teenagers living under fascism, and he talked about his work in the Hunger Games universe, his excellent 2025 Stephen King adaptation "The Long Walk," and the fact that he's set to adapt Pierce Brown's novel series "Red Rising" into a movie.

Lawrence was thoughtful and careful while expressing that "Red Rising" does differ from the other two properties in one important way: the protagonist, Darrow, must penetrate a higher class to discover the truth. As he told me:

"Red Rising, on the other hand, even though there's similar elements [to Hunger Games and 'The Long Walk'], to me, is an amazing infiltration story. So I think that brings its own set of parameters and moral choice dilemmas when you are sneaking into a society that you're not supposed to be a part of, and what happens when you start to like the enemy and you're not supposed to like the enemy and the choices that you make. So again, they have similar things, but for me, they're completely different stories."

As Francis Lawrence told me while we discussed the Hunger Games film franchise, "The Long Walk," and a future potential Red Rising franchise, even though he can see the similarities — "in competition, youth, high stakes, obviously all those kinds of things" — he sees the stories "completely differently."  "The Long Walk," as Lawrence tells it, is about the relationship between Ray and Pete, the characters played by Cooper Hoffman and David Jonsson. Lawrence continued:

"I fell in love with the relationship between [Ray and Pete] and the rest of the boys and how they became like a brotherhood and fell in love with one another in a way where you'd expect there to be more competition. And so I found a real beauty in the relationships. So for me, it's less about the games and more about the relationships and what that means and what the long walk means in terms of a metaphor for existence."

So what about the world of the Hunger Games, where teenagers are forced into an arena every single year so they can fight to the death until one victor emerges, all for the amusement of the all-powerful Capitol of the dystopian state of Panem? Lawrence is spot-on once again. "And Hunger Games are very different in that they tend to be, book to book, about very different kinds of themes," he told me. "['Sunrise on the Reaping'] is about implicit submission and this idea of not being apathetic and finding your voice and standing up and fighting for what you believe in, even if it's at great cost. So that's very specific."

When I initially asked Francis Lawrence about the narrative overlaps between many of his recent projects, I also asked if he's comfortable with a large section of his career being tied to fascism, dystopias, and the oppression of young people. Not only did Lawrence say that he was, but he also revealed something fascinating about his preferences as a filmmaker:

"I am. And what's weird is that I think the dystopian aspect of these worlds, a lot of it, for me, is about the world-building. I like the visual aspect of filmmaking, so world-building is a very, very important part of what I love to do. And so if somebody were to hand me a contemporary heist movie, I wouldn't do it. I just have no interest in just going out in the streets and shooting some kind of — I just feel like there's not enough for me to do. So I'd say that's part of it."

This is reminiscent of the innovative horror director Robert Eggers telling Rotten Tomatoes in 2025 that "the idea of having to photograph a car makes me ill, and the idea of photographing a cellphone is just death," putting Lawrence in excellent company here. Throughout his work on projects like the Hunger Games films and "The Long Walk," Lawrence has handily proven his skill for telling a specific type of story, but his attention to world-building also shouldn't be overlooked ... and it's great to hear that he hopes to tell stories like this for a very long time.

"The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping" hits theaters on November 20, 2026.

#3793. 5 Best Walter Koenig TV Shows That Aren't Star Trek, Ranked

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Walter Koenig has sadly passed away at 90 years old. He was best known for playing the role of Pavel Chekov on "Star Trek: The Original Series" and was brought in during the show's second season (a move, Koenig once said, that was done to appeal to a younger audience). Chekov was a Russian character seen working alongside the USS Enterprise's main crew, and he was cute, a little cocksure, and claimed that Russians invented Western Civilization. Although rarely highlighted on "Star Trek," he was an invaluable part of the series and appeared in several "Star Trek" feature films.

But Koenig was more than "Star Trek." He began accruing credits in the early 1960s, turning up in episodes of the day's hottest TV shows. Koenig started off by scoring a small role on the revolutionary soap opera "General Hospital," and from there, it was off to the races. He soon appeared on shows like "The Alfred Hitchcock Hour" and "Jericho," leading to his time on "Star Trek." Koenig continued to work from there, acting in various projects and taking on numerous voice roles for practically the rest of his days.

Many may not be familiar with Koenig's non-"Trek" career, so we're here to offer some guidance. The following is a list of notable, fun, funny roles that Koenig tackled on TV, including his most famous part outside of the "Star Trek" franchise.

Starting in 1986, young fans of Ivan Reitman's 1984 blockbuster "Ghostbusters" were treated to a creative and exciting animated spin-off TV show called "The Real Ghostbusters." The animated series re-envisioned the four leads from Reitman's film with new designs but very much the same character traits, and it sported some amazing voice performances. Lorenzo Music initially played Peter, Maurice LaMarche played Egon, Frank Welker played Ray, and Arsenio Hall played Winston after Ernie Hudson was rejected from reprising the role (for a very silly reason). Because of its episodic structure, "The Real Ghostbusters" had the leeway to introduce a wide menagerie of ghosts, ghouls, and demons, and it would even occasionally stray into the Lovecraftian.

Case in point: Vladimir Pavel Maximov, the character played by Walter Koenig in the episode "Russian About." Maximov was a scientist who had been laughed out of the scientific community for his zany theories. (The Titanic sank, he said, because it crashed into Elvis.) In the episode, Maximov comes to possess a Necronomicon-like grimoire called the Nameless Book, which has access to incantations that can summon the Old Ones. The Old Ones, as fans of H.P. Lovecraft can tell you, are a gaggle of evil gods that have been biding their time before reclaiming the Earth.

Maximov uses the Nameless Book to summon a god called Yibb-Tstll, much to the annoyance of the Ghostbusters. It's fun to hear Koenig play a goofy cartoon villain like this. Koenig was a talented voice actor, and folks loved his Russian accent. (Koenig, notably, was of Russian descent, although he was born in Chicago.)

The one-season sci-fi TV series "The Starlost" has a great premise. The series is set on board a 200-mile-long starship called Earthship Ark, outfitted with dozens of enormous biospheres, merrily making its way through the cosmos and hoping to relocate humanity to a new world. A century into the voyage, an accident on the ship seals off all the biospheres from the others, and everyone on board has to fend for themselves. The action of "The Starlost" takes place 405 years after said accident, in the year 2790. Keir Dullea plays Devon, an agrarian farmer who manages to flee his biome and discover the now-forgotten mission of Earthship Ark. Devon and a few of his friends spend the series exploring the ship. William Ostler plays the computer who instructs them.

Walter Koenig appeared in two episodes of "The Starlost" as a humanoid alien named Oro, hailing from the planet Exar. A year prior, Oro's ship crashed into the Ark, and he managed to get on board. He has since been tearing apart the Ark, looking for the components he needs to rebuild his ship and employing the aid of Idona (Alexandra Bastedo), who also fled one of the ship's biospheres.

Koenig plays Oro as a cold, calculating character, an opportunist with plans of his own. The character also returns a few episodes later, promising Devon and the crew a place to settle (naturally, he's lying for his own purposes). Koenig's presence on "The Starlost" was like getting a blessing from the sci-fi head honchos.

"The Starlost" was created by the infamously grumpy and undeniably great writer Harlan Ellison. It may be obscure these days, but it's worth looking into. I love the premise.

Gen-Xers like me remember Stretch Armstrong as a mere toy, a smiling superhero figure with goo-filled rubber limbs that could be stretched across a room. But I'll wager that not many of us were aware that in 2017, Netflix adapted Stretch Armstrong into an animated series called "Stretch Armstrong and the Flex Fighters." The new series was about a trio of teens who were accidentally exposed to a miracle chemical called Flexarium that turned them into stretchy, rubbery superheroes. They become Stretch (Scott Menvile), Wingspan (Steven Yuen), and Omni-Mass (Ogie Banks).

"Stretch Armstrong" also attracted a few "Star Trek" alumni. Kate Mulgrew from "Star Trek: Voyager" played a seeming antagonist in the first season of the series, before becoming a mentor figure in the second. Wil Wheaton from "Star Trek: The Next Generation" played Jonathan Rook, the benefactor of the Flex Fighters, but also secretly a were-monster. Keith David, Henry Rollins, Clancy Brown, and many other notable actors had small roles.

In a few episodes, Walter Koenig played a character named Mr. Savic, a mad scientist who created an intelligent plant creature called Anastasia (Tara Strong). He was a biology teacher who, in the show's second season, learns how to turn into a massive superhero monster called Biomass. Koenig brought a certain degree of energy and humanity to the role. He was a nerd with genuine enthusiasm. Audiences also see that Mr. Savic is a lonely man who only wanted to create a family for himself. It's actually a pretty great role.

In the "Futurama" episode "Where No Fan Has Gone Before," Fry (Billy West) learns that, in the 31st century, it is illegal to say the phrase "Star Trek." In the centuries since the present, Trekkies became religious zealots who started wars. Thereafter, Earth took precautions against future conflagrations by ridding the planet of all episodes of the original "Star Trek" series. The cast, reduced to living heads in jars, fled the planet.

Armed with Leonard Nimoy's head, Fry goes looking for the rest of the cast and finds that William Shatner, Walter Koenig, George Takei, DeForest Kelley, and Nichelle Nichols have been resurrected on a distant planet by a weird energy being named Melllvar (Maurice LaMarche), who has fallen in love with "Star Trek." Melllvar's "action figure" collection is complete when Fry brings Nimoy along. James (James Doohan was replaced by a new character named Welshie.)

The entire cast of "Star Trek" has a great sense of humor about their place in popular culture, and they are all game to make fun of themselves a little bit (Doohan notwithstanding). At one point, Fry points out that everyone can achieve their goals in life, pointing out that after "Star Trek," Walter Koenig became an actor.

That's a bit of an insult, but Koenig expands on the joke by saying that he became an actor, but also a person with friends and keys. The plot of the "Futurama" episode involves the cast of "Star Trek" fighting the cast of "Futurama" in hand-to-hand combat, and Koenig points out that he has no experience actually fighting, "except with you guys," he says to the "Star Trek" cast.

Koenig had a sense of humor, and that's important to remember. Shatner, meanwhile, called Fry voice actor Billy West to make sure the episode was on the up and up.

One of Walter Koenig's best roles was Alfred Bester in 12 episodes of "Babylon 5." Bester was a member of the Psi Corps, a cadre of human psychics that are, in the lore of the series, required to become cops or else be injected with psychic-dampening drugs. Because there are no checks to the organization's power, they hastily became somewhat fascistic. Fans of "Babylon 5" might be able to tell you about the show's storied Telepath War.

One of the show's wickedest Psi Cops was Bester, a war criminal who passed through Babylon 5 occasionally. Bester was first introduced investigating another rogue psychic, and presented as a cool, calming, insidious presence that was clearly using his low energy as a manipulation tool. While many admire Koenig for his energetic "Star Trek" performances, his best acting came from "Babylon 5." Bester was an arrogant figure, able to diffuse any threats made in his direction. There is a scene wherein Babylon 5's security chief Garibaldi (Jerry Doyle) says that he'd like to beat Bester like a piñata, and Bester takes it as a compliment: "So, you think of me as something bright and cheerful," he says, "full of toys and candy for young children? Thank you. That makes me feel much better about our relationship."

Unlike Chekov, Bester drove the stories on "Babylon 5," and his character had a grand arc and was given a great deal of background. If anyone only ever saw Koenig as a supporting player or a side character, know that he was capable of evil, pathos, and rich, intriguing characters. Bester might be the actor's best role, period.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

HGTV’s smash show “Home Town” is officially international with the debut of “Home Town Takeover Canada,” and the accomplishment has left original host Ben Napier feeling very sentimental.

The new series premiered on Oct. 25 and is hosted by Bryan and Sarah Baeumler, whom reality TV fans will recognize as the hosts of “Renovation Island.”

Ben told People magazine the Bauemlers are “the perfect hosts” for the show.

“Bryan and Sarah are rock stars,” he said. “They’re crazy popular, and it felt good handing it off to them.”

Set in Port Alberni on Vancouver Island, the newest installation is part of the “Home Town Takeover” spinoff, which previously saw Ben and his wife and co-host, Erin Napier, traveling to transform small towns across the United States.

“This was never something that we even dreamed of, being on TV,” Ben said. “And so, the fact that this show about us working to bring back our little town has now gone to multiple states and now across the border to Canada is just crazy.”

While the Napiers hosted the spinoff of their original show for the first three seasons—focusing on Wetumpka, AL, Fort Morgan, CO, and Sebring, FL—they could be involved in “Home Town Takeover Canada” only as special guests, due to legal restrictions.

“Our production company is Canadian. The people who make our show, they’re passionate about this Canadian takeover,” Erin told Cinemablend. “But because we are Americans, we are not legally allowed to host the show. We can only be in it as, like, guest stars.”

“Yeah, a Canadian broadcast show has to be hosted by Canadian talent,” Ben confirmed.

Ben likened the experience of traveling to Canada to launch the series to being “on vacation,” as he and Erin were required to take a backseat for this particular set of projects.

“There were several times I jokingly told Bryan or Michael, who’s the project manager—they would ask me about something—and I would throw up my hands and say, ‘I’m on vacation. This is your little red wagon,’” he said.

Bryan and Sarah also opened up about the new series and the experience of putting their own mark on the legacy the Napiers had begun—which they have “so much respect for.”

“Erin and Ben have built something people love. As Canadians, getting to shine a light on a Canadian community makes it especially meaningful,” they said. “We wanted to bring that same heart to the show, along with our own approach to construction and design.”

Sarah also gave the Laurel, MS–based couple a special shoutout in an Oct. 26 Instagram Story.

“Thank you Ben and Erin for welcoming us into the Home Town Takeover family,” she wrote, sharing some photos of the couples on-set in Port Alberni.

In a preview for the series, Bryan and Sarah gush over their excitement “to tackle the biggest project—by far—of our lives,” noting that they had just three months to help “transform” the town, breathe new life into small businesses and residential homes, and give “back to people who are working hard to move it forward.”

“Home Town Takeover Canada” is not yet available to watch in the United States, but airs Sundays on HGTV in Canada and can be streamed on a Canadian service called Citytv+.

According to an Aug. 1 social media update from Erin, fans can expect more stateside “Home Town” on the way in the near future, as production is underway for Season 11 of the show—which debuted its first season in January 2016.

“When is more ‘Home Town’ coming? Soon!” she teased in a caption alongside an Instagram Reel breaking down the family’s daily routine during a “typical day” of filming, which she added “runs year round,” although they had previously been on a brief hiatus for the “summer break.”

While an official release date has yet to be confirmed, “Home Town” has historically set season premieres for December or January.

#457. Tiny magnetic vortices could help computers work more like your brain

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Tiny magnetic structures called skyrmions could help pave the way for a new generation of computers that process information more like the human brain while using far less electricity. Although most people have never heard of skyrmions, these microscopic magnetic vortices may eventually play an important role in how computers store and process data.

A study published in the journal Nature Nanotechnology provides new insights into how skyrmions behave and how scientists can control them. The findings could help researchers develop smaller, more efficient computing systems and advance emerging quantum technologies.

Most of the research was conducted at the Institute of Physics at Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz, Germany, with contributions from a professor at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU).

"Skyrmions are small, magnetic vortices that can store information in a completely new way," explained Asle Sudbø, who leads NTNU's Centre for Quantum Spintronics (QuSpin).

Unlike conventional technologies, which require increasing amounts of space and energy to handle growing quantities of data, skyrmions could offer a much more efficient alternative.

"Instead of the current technology, where we constantly need more space and energy to process data, skyrmions could make it possible to build much smaller and more energy-efficient data storage systems," explained Sudbø.

Tiny Magnetic Vortices Could Enable Brain-Like Computers

The potential applications of skyrmions extend well beyond conventional data storage. They are also important to spintronics, which explores ways to use the magnetic properties of electrons in electronic devices, and to research involving topological quantum effects.

One particularly promising possibility is the development of computers that operate in ways similar to biological brains.

"These nanoscale vortices could help develop computers that resemble biological brains," said Sudbø.

Rather than relying on conventional transistor-based computing, such systems could use magnetism to process information. In principle, this approach could dramatically reduce electricity consumption, potentially allowing future computers to perform complex tasks with very little energy.

Turning that possibility into practical technology, however, requires scientists to understand exactly how skyrmions behave and how to manipulate them reliably.

The latest research represents an important advance toward that goal.

Scientists Film Magnetic Skyrmion Lattices Melting for the First Time

Skyrmions do not always exist as isolated magnetic structures. Under certain conditions, they organize themselves into repeating patterns known as lattices, resembling the orderly arrangement of atoms in a crystal, but on a much smaller scale.

Scientists can manipulate these arrangements, a capability that could be essential for using skyrmions in future computing devices.

In the new study, researchers achieved something that had not been demonstrated before. They recorded the melting of a magnetic skyrmion lattice as it happened while simultaneously controlling individual skyrmions.

"For the first time, researchers have managed to film how a magnetic skyrmion lattice melts in real time, while simultaneously being able to manipulate each individual skyrmion directly as they film it. For example, both their shape and size can be changed 'on the fly'," said Sudbø.

The ability to observe this process directly provides a clearer picture of how skyrmions respond when their organized structures break apart. It also allows researchers to study how changing the size and shape of individual magnetic vortices affects their behavior.

A New Step Toward Energy-Efficient Computing

Understanding how skyrmions behave during the breakdown of their lattices could help scientists develop more precise ways to control them. That control is particularly important if these magnetic structures are to become useful components in future computer systems.

The findings therefore offer more than a new way to observe an unusual magnetic phenomenon. They provide information that could help researchers overcome challenges involved in developing practical skyrmion-based technologies.

"The findings give us new insight into how these types of particles behave. This could therefore become important for the development of more energy-efficient computer technology in the future," said Sudbø.

Although substantial development is still needed before skyrmions can be used in everyday computing devices, the research highlights their potential for improving data storage, enabling new computing architectures, and supporting advances in quantum technology.

By learning to observe and manipulate these tiny magnetic vortices in real time, scientists are gaining capabilities that could eventually help reshape the design of computers.

"This could revolutionize the way we think about computers," concluded Sudbø.

#4624. In 1934, a doctor brought a dog back to life. He tried to do the same with humans.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

Under the harsh glare of overhead lights at a laboratory at the University of California at Berkeley, Lazarus II lay motionless.

Dr. Robert E. Cornish, who’d asphyxiated the rangy white fox terrier with ether and nitrogen, watched the clock. Six minutes after the final heartbeat, the scientist gathered up the dog and strapped him to a teeterboard, a see-saw-like invention whose back-and-forth, up-and-down motion Cornish believed was key to restoring the blood’s circulation.

He pushed a saline solution primed with oxygen, heart-stimulating adrenalin, and the liver extract heparin into a vein in Lazarus’ thigh, then lowered his head over the patient. As his assistant rocked the teeterboard and rubbed the little body vigorously, Cornish exhaled deep breaths into the dog’s mouth and lungs.

Long seconds turned to minutes and then, suddenly, Lazarus gasped and jerked his leg. Slowly, his heart began to beat. Lazarus II had returned from the dead.

With the invention of modern cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) still a quarter century off, resurrecting Lazarus after his heart stopped was something of a miracle in 1934. But while the fox terrier lived for another eight hours and 13 minutes, he did not live well, stuck in a restless zombie state punctuated by the occasional whine, bark, and panting.

“Irreversible damage occurs to either the heart or the brain after a period of time, making a good recovery impossible,” Samuel Tisherman, professor of surgery and director for the Center for Critical Care and Trauma Education at the University of Maryland, Baltimore, tells Popular Science.

Lazarus II wasn’t Cornish’s first attempt to revive dying patients using his teeterboard. He wasn’t even the first Lazarus. The young scientist, a former child prodigy who’d earned his doctorate from UC Berkeley at age 22, became obsessed with resuscitation sometime around 1933.

Before working on dogs, Cornish began his reanimating work on people. A 62-year-old printer who died of cardiac arrest was the first to receive Cornish’s reanimation treatment. Four-and-a-half hours after his death, Cornish arrived at the hospital to strap the man’s heating pad-swaddled body to a teeterboard. After see-sawing the patient up and down for 90 minutes, Cornish failed to revive the subject—although Cornish noted a point at which the printer’s “face seemed to warm up suddenly, sparkle returned to the eyes, and soft pulsations were observed in the soft tissue between windpipe and sternum,” according to science journalist Frank Swain’s How to Make a Zombie: The Life (and Death) Science of Reanimation and Mind Control.

Two weeks later, Cornish tried the same treatment on a 22-year-old machinist who’d been pulled dead from the San Francisco Bay three hours before, working his body on the teeterboard for a full four hours. It, too, failed, along with a third attempt the following day in which Cornish submerged an electrocuted cadaver in a warm bath instead of using heating pads before placing the body on the teeterboard.

Dissatisfied with the outcomes, Cornish decided it would be wise to perfect his methodology on fox terriers before returning to human subjects. He named each one Lazarus after the biblical man Jesus brought back to life.

Cornish’s biggest breakthrough came with Lazarus IV. Though blind and unable to stand on its own, within 13 days the dog could crawl, bark, and sit up. Its appetite, too, had returned. Over the few months of his life after death, Lazarus IV went through almost a pound of meat each day.

The next patient, Lazarus V, recovered even more quickly. It took just four days for the dog to resume some normal behaviors despite suffering blindness and brain damage. Like his predecessor, he too lived only a few months.

Despite their mixed outcome, Cornish was praised for his Lazarus experiments. In the press, the scientist who’d often been considered an oddball by his peers for pursuing projects like underwater reading glasses was hailed as a celebrity. A movie about his work, Life Returns, was released in 1935.

But Cornish’s resuscitation experiments were not without controversy. Dogged by animal rights protests and other outcries from the public, UC Berkeley’s administrators had him removed from campus. Cornish continued his work from home, turning a garden shed into a laboratory that neighbors claimed stunk up the street.

The science behind resuscitating a dying patient has come a long way since the Lazarus experiments. These days, “how to restore a pulse depends upon the cause of the cardiac arrest,” says Tisherman.

“The first, most important intervention is assuring good chest compressions,” he continues.

How quickly intervention begins also plays a role. “It’s generally been considered that if the period of no blood flow at all, before someone starts CPR, is greater than five minutes, the outcome will likely be poor,” says Tisherman—though that idea has been challenged and “no one knows for sure.”

While drugs and medications similar to those used by Cornish to improve blood flow and return heart rhythms to normal in his Lazarus experiments play a part in modern resuscitation protocol, emergency medical practitioners have never adopted the teeterboard he believed was key to restoring life.

Cornish never had the opportunity to prove that his teeterboard worked, despite appealing to the governors of Nevada, Arizona, and Colorado for permission to revive criminals executed by the gas chamber shortly after completing his Lazarus experiments. Their refusal didn’t stop Cornish from making one final attempt in 1948, sending multiple requests to the warden at San Quentin State Prison to resuscitate kidnapper and murderer Thomas McMonigle following his upcoming execution by gas chamber—all of which were rebuffed.

Once news of Cornish’s plan for McMonigle became public, more than 50 other “mostly single men” volunteered to be brought back to life by his teeterboard but by then, it seems, Cornish had moved on to something a little less extraordinary: formulating and marketing toothpaste to the American public.

You Can See Everything co-director Lance Oppenheim calls him a “very unconventionally hyper-masculine man.” We get what he’s talking about.

And while the headlines of You Can See Everything are largely about the access to Holmes as a public figure and things she did or didn’t say, the reality is that it’s more of a Nathan Fielder movie than an Elizabeth Holmes movie. Fielder, who broke out with the docu-spoof series Nathan For You on Comedy Central before moving on to more surreal takes on similar subjects with HBO’s The Rehearsal and Paramount+ and Showtime’s The Curse, is at the center of the story all along, looking for something, anything, in Holmes and Evans as he talks to them. For a film of this structure to work, the person we’re along with for the ride needs to be someone we both understand and don’t mind spending that kind of time with. While You Can See Everything ultimately leaves us questioning everything we’ve just seen, Fielder, to his credit, is someone who makes the three-hour runtime an easy watch. He’s dry, yes—that’s his on-screen persona. But he’s also vulnerable and often humanistic. When he faces down Holmes, for example, in a scene where he shows her Abbott and Costello’s famous “Who’s On First?” routine for the first time, he reacts in a way most viewers could probably see themselves reacting.

Oppenheim did an interview with GQ, and he suggested that Fielder’s nature in the film makes him something of an emblem for modern men. “I feel like he's at his most masculine in You Can See Everything,” he said. “I'd always tell him, ‘You look so handsome in this.’ And I was just like, ‘I love being this close on your face. I'm so tight on your face right now and you look amazing.’ And he'd be like, ‘Dude, you could see my blackheads and my zits and stuff.’ I'd be like, ‘You look great. I actually think it makes you look better.’”

His quote alludes to the way Fielder carries himself in the movie: as something of an audience surrogate (at least during the first part of the film), and someone the audience can see themselves in. This marks a shift from Fielder’s previous on-screen personas, when he was a bit more of an on-screen joker or troll, egging his subjects a bit more into saying or doing something that will make them look silly. Here, he’s basically a blank slate for the audience to graft onto.

It’s interesting to read the quotes from Oppenheim, who last month also directed Primetime, a scripted A24 film starring Robert Pattinson. Pattinson, of course, is a traditionally handsome movie star, someone who’s played heartthrob roles like Edward Cullen in Twilight or literal superheroes like Bruce Wayne in The Batman. There’s, of course, a vision of masculinity in that. Outside of Fielder, Evans is the only other male figure substantially present in the film, and he does partake in some more traditional expressions of masculine energy: He goes to the gym, he shoots guns in a range once he moves to Texas, and he even partakes in a shower scene of full-frontal male nudity as Fielder looks on. But it’s that parallel that Oppenheim implies is important—Fielder isn’t ever nervous or uncomfortable. “He's a very unconventionally hyper-masculine man,” Oppenheim said. “That's a compliment, I guess.”

The movie works because of the access we’re getting, and the way Fielder and Oppenheim set things up and draw us in and spit us out at the end. But it also works because Fielder so clearly knows who he is and understands who he is—blackheads be damned. And when the result is You Can See Everything, does anything else really matter?

#250. In the biotech world, you can’t see everything

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

A reporter’s unsettling takeaway from You Can See Everything.

Earlier this week, I attended a screening of You Can See Everything, the buzzy new documentary about the disgraced former biotech CEO Elizabeth Holmes.

I went in not really knowing what to expect. I’d avoided spoilers (and won’t be giving anything away here, either!). I was wondering what new information this film could reveal, given how familiar many of us are with the Theranos story thanks to years of media coverage, the best-selling book Bad Blood, and the Hulu series The Dropout, starring Amanda Seyfried.

Like many others who have seen the film, I came away feeling unsettled. But not just about Holmes’s fraud and the somewhat disturbing interactions between the subjects. The film reignited a concern I’ve had about covering science for most of my career: Despite our best efforts, we never can actually see everything.

In case you’re rusty on the details, Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford University in 2003 to run Theranos, a diagnostics company she founded, when she was just 19. She went on to claim that her company had invented a way to run over 100 diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood.

Holmes raised hundreds of millions of dollars for Theranos, which was valued at $9 billion in 2014. Forbes named her the youngest self-made female billionaire—she was worth $4.5 billion when she was just 30 years old.

Things started to unravel in 2015, when an investigation by the Wall Street Journal raised concerns by former Theranos employees that the tests didn’t work. Some also said that the company’s device had not been tested properly, and that blood samples had been analyzed on machines from other companies instead. MIT Technology Review named it one of the biggest technology failures of 2015.

Forbes had revised Holmes’s worth to $0 by 2016. That year, a damning government report said that the company’s lab was struggling with basic procedures and gave patients results even when they knew they could be wrong.

In 2018, Theranos, Holmes, and former company president Ramesh Balwani were charged with “massive fraud.” In 2022, she was sentenced to a little over 11 years in prison for defrauding investors. She began serving her sentence in May 2023.

It was around a month before this that filmmakers Nathan Fielder, Lance Oppenheim, and their colleagues began documenting Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, at home with their two young children. I’m not giving anything away when I say it’s an extremely intimate portrayal—at least of how the two adults interact with Fielder, who is also a central character in the film.

As you might know if you’ve seen the trailer, there are plenty of super close-ups. I felt as if I spent most of the film staring straight into the faces of those people. And almost the whole way through, they all seemed to be trying to figure each other out. Holmes is known for her dishonesty, but she invited cameras into her home in an apparent bid to reframe the narrative and position herself as an honest person with an unusual mind. Will anyone believe her?

I couldn’t help feeling some empathy for Holmes, a mother we see preparing to leave her two kids for a lengthy prison sentence. And even though I have long known the facts, Holmes is clearly an effective manipulator. You can see her plant the seeds of doubt in Fielder’s mind. At times, I felt she was convincing me, sitting in my cinema seat. Maybe she really didn’t mean to lie?

But then I remembered how other journalists felt after being taken in by her claims. “I blew it,” Fortune’s Roger Parloff reflected in 2015, a year after profiling Holmes and her company. “I’m pretty sure she lied to me,” STAT’s Matthew Herper wrote recently, having interviewed Holmes in 2015. Even the former employee turned whistleblower Tyler Shultz, who knew the product didn’t work, said that he could “have a five-minute conversation with her and walk away feeling like my work was changing the world,” he said in 2022.

I didn’t cover Theranos in the 2010s. If I’d interviewed Holmes, would I have fallen for it all?

One of the early red flags in the Theranos case was the lack of published, peer-reviewed data. Much of the company’s work was classified as a “trade secret.” Scientists tend to publish their work in journals—a slow process that involves having the work evaluated by often anonymous experts in the field, who provide feedback, highlight questionable conclusions, and often ask for clarification. Peer review is meant to add rigor to the publication process. You should be able to trust published scientific papers.

Scientists aren’t perfect. Some will exaggerate their results or inflate their importance. Some manipulate data to serve a hypothesis. Sometimes these infractions are caught during peer review, but not always. There are plenty of examples of scientific findings that could never be replicated by other labs and ended up collapsing under the weight of real evidence. Many papers ultimately end up with corrections. Some are withdrawn—10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023.

At any rate, there’s been a shift to sharing research in other ways. Today, many scientists opt to publish their work at preprint servers like arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv before they submit them to established journals. Those outlets bypass peer review. So do company press releases, social media posts, and many conference presentations. And I get a lot of my stories from conversations and interviews.

I’ll admit I’ve looked at a scientific finding and thought: A scientist has just said they’ve done that. How can I be sure they really have?

I like to think that I hold myself to high journalistic standards here, as does everyone at MIT Technology Review. I would never publish a story about some new scientific development without doing my own form of peer review, and asking scientists—ideally people I know and trust—what they think of a new claim.

But the film is a stark reminder of how easy it can be to fall for an appealing narrative. While most scientists are, I hope, pretty honest, and Holmes is certainly an outlier, we should all continue to interrogate the claims being fed to us. If you’ve seen the film, let me know what you think!

The house had been oval in shape, with a low door leading to an interior hearth. Other conveniences included sunken storage areas and stone-lined water storage tanks. Under the floors of these houses, though, and under the feet of the occupants, was a fascinating feature that evokes the opening scenes of a classic horror film.

As the archaeologists slowly dug through the floor of the structure, they came unexpectedly upon a human skeleton, legs tucked up in the fetal position. Another skeleton, similarly arranged, was found beneath a different roundhouse’s floor. These people had clearly been laid to rest with care, with stones laid out around them.

When archaeologists began to study the remains, though, they were staggered to learn that each body’s parts had not all come from the same person. These were composite mummies, assembled with the remains of at least six people who had died over the span of two hundred years. But this wasn’t a jumbled-together macabre collection or evidence of a Bronze-Age serial killer. These bodies had been treated carefully and buried in stone-lined graves. So what were the people of Cladh Hallan doing with the dug-up remains of their ancestors?

More than four thousand years ago, early farmers began to work the soil on the Outer Hebrides islands off of Scotland’s northwestern coast. At the time, the Roman Empire and even ancient Greece were specks on the chronological horizon. Along the Aegean coast, Minoans and Mycenaeans were building palaces and cities. Farther north, Germanic tribes were exporting amber and expertly forging bronze and gold artifacts.

At Cladh Hallan, early farmers grew barley, herded sheep, and foraged the abundant resources on the island’s beaches. Their descendants continued to thrive on the island for centuries, the population grew, and so did the need for land.

Nearly a thousand years into Cladh Hallan’s occupation, with living space on the small island becoming harder to find, people started building over the burial grounds of their ancestors. But the islanders were definitely aware of the graves beneath their feet—in fact, they went out of their way to acknowledge the people buried in them.

Residents of Cladh Hallan dug down into these older burials containing both cremated and unburnt remains, removing some parts and leaving the rest undisturbed. Then they assembled the exhumed remains into the requisite anatomical combinations that modern archaeologists have found centuries later. Once this was done, the composite bodies were once again laid to rest under the foundations of the roundhouses.

The two best-known adult skeletons from Cladh Hallan are the most unusual. Each was found under a roundhouse. One skeleton was identified as male, the other female, based on their pelvic bones. The bodies date to between 1300 and 1500 BCE…mostly.

The skeleton originally identified as a woman had a man’s skull where her own head used to be. Two of the teeth were missing from the skull, and those had been placed in each of the woman’s hands. By radiocarbon dating the bones, researchers found that the skull actually belonged to a man who had died at least a century earlier, perhaps a relative from an older generation.

The body originally identified as male was put together from three different people—all confirmed as male through DNA testing. The burial was made up of a man’s torso, a second man’s skull, and a third man’s jawbone. Each of these men had died at different times over the span of about 150 years. Again, ancestry and family identity seem to play a big part in these re-burials.

It isn’t terribly uncommon for cultures to include their deceased loved ones in celebratory, ritual, or even daily activities. Every few years, families in the Toraja region of Sulawesi, Indonesia exhume the remains of loved ones, clean them, dress them in fresh garments, and honor them with festivals.

South Korean “death beads” are made from the cremated remains of family members and displayed in the home.

Pre-colonial Inca religious tradition included regular ceremonial processions featuring the remains of people “buried” in natural alcoves in the Andes mountains and mummified by the dry, cold mountain air. The Cladh Hallan skeletons had once been mummified as well, though this was not mummification as it was practiced in ancient Egypt, nor like the mountainside Inca tradition.

First, the bodies were tightly wrapped in fabric, which kept the bones and soft tissue flexed into the fetal position. This is why the limbs remained so tightly curled towards the torso, and why the bodies were so well-preserved. Archaeologists were able to identify this type of preservation based on microscopic changes to the surfaces of the bones from the acidic environment of the peat bogs in which the bodies were submerged after wrapping.

Peat is a dense, wet accumulation of centuries of decaying plant matter, and is a feature of lots of landscapes, particularly in the northern latitudes. People have used dried peat as fuel for thousands of years, since it burns well and slowly, and is plentiful in areas where there aren’t a lot of trees for firewood. Peat from the Scottish isles, notably, is responsible for the smoky, earthy taste characteristic of Scotch whiskey.

Peat bogs are so packed with plant material that there is almost no oxygen present, which is great for preservation, as is the typically cool temperature beneath the peat’s surface. The lack of oxygen keeps microbes and other critters from consuming the body’s soft tissues, and the tannic acid from all the decaying plants in the bog turns skin dark and leathery.

By first burying the bodies of family members under layers of peat, the people at Cladh Hallan ensured that the remains would be as intact as possible when later generations exhumed them. This not only means that the Bronze Age inhabitants of the island were well aware of peat’s preservative properties, but that the entire process was a deliberate sequence of events.

Archaeologists interpret the pieced-together reburials as a part of a social or religious tradition that emphasized family lines and reverence for ancestors. Stones clearly marked the graves under the roundhouses, so the homes’ placement was definitely on purpose.

The initial bog burials were deliberately used as a source of family remains, and the parts that composed the buried skeletons seem to have been deliberately chosen, though we may never know why or how. It seems as though ancestor-recycling may have been a regular process at Cladh Hallan in the later part of the Bronze Age, as older generations gave way to new.

Archaeologists also surmise that the exhumed mummified remains were initially kept above ground, maybe in a special place in the house, and included as a part of daily life. These multi-ancestor figures might have been consulted when important decisions were made, or they may have been seen as household protectors.

Archaeology can never really uncover the full story of what people thought and believed thousands of years ago. Thoughts do not fossilize, and decay and weathering affect even the best-preserved archaeological sites.

We will probably never know exactly what it was like to live at Cladh Hallan when it was a busy, thriving settlement. We may never understand how the people that lived there conceived of their world, or how they thought about spirituality. Thanks to the burials at Cladh Hallan, though, we have a tantalizing glimpse of how people may have seen death in their universe as a continuous, pieced-together chain of lives lived over generations.

#579. Scientists propose a way to detect dangerous “mirror life” before it exists

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Scientists have proposed what they believe is the first way to detect "mirror life," a hypothetical form of life built from molecules that are reversed versions of those found in living organisms on Earth.

Researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University have developed a proposed detection technique that uses molecules capable of emitting light with distinct left and right-handed properties. By analyzing these light signals, scientists may eventually be able to distinguish conventional organisms from their mirror-image counterparts.

Although mirror life does not currently exist, some experts consider its potential creation a serious biological safety concern. These artificial organisms could behave in unpredictable ways when interacting with Earth's ecosystems and might evade certain natural defenses that protect people, animals, and plants.

The likelihood of mirror life emerging is considered low, but its possible consequences could be severe enough to justify developing detection methods in advance.

Why Mirror Life Could Pose a Biological Threat

Concerns about mirror life have prompted scientists to recommend restrictions on research intended to produce organisms capable of reproducing using mirror-image biological molecules.

The potential dangers have also attracted attention from governments and international organizations, including the UK Government Office for Science and the United Nations Secretary General's Scientific Advisory Board.

The underlying science involves a property of molecules known as chirality, often described as molecular "handedness." Just as a person's left and right hands are mirror images that cannot be perfectly aligned, some molecules can exist in two forms that have identical chemical components but different three-dimensional arrangements.

These subtle structural differences can have major biological consequences. Two mirror-image versions of a molecule contain the same atoms connected in the same order, yet the human body may respond to them very differently. For example, one form of a medication could successfully treat a disease, while its mirror-image counterpart might be ineffective or even harmful.

Scientists are concerned that organisms assembled from reversed biological building blocks could interact with existing life in unexpected ways.

Importantly, the researchers emphasize that mirror life remains entirely hypothetical. Their proposed detection method is intended as a precaution, not a response to an existing biological threat.

The proposed approach relies on a phenomenon called circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), which can reveal the molecular handedness, or chirality, of certain substances and biological structures.

Light can exhibit different polarization patterns, including forms that rotate in opposite directions. By studying these patterns, researchers can obtain information about the three-dimensional arrangement of molecules.

The underlying concept has a surprisingly familiar application: the polarized light technology used in modern 3D movie glasses to create the illusion of depth.

The research, published in Royal Society Open Science under the title 'Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) as a Contingency Assay for "Mirror Life" Bio-Hazards', was led by Dr. Lewis MacKenzie of the University of Strathclyde's Department of Pure and Applied Chemistry, working with Professor Robert Pal of Durham University.

Dr. MacKenzie said: "We don't expect mirror life to appear tomorrow, but if it did arise, the consequences could be severe, and it makes sense to develop detection methods before they are needed. Professor Pal and I had been working on CPL technologies before recent concerns became prominent, and guarding against mirror life was a clear fit for our research."

A Molecular Fingerprint for Detecting Mirror Life

To identify a possible mirror organism, the researchers suggest using specialized fluorescent probes that attach to particular structures inside cells.

Once bound to their targets, these probes would generate characteristic CPL signals. Scientists could analyze the resulting patterns to determine whether a biological sample contains the molecular handedness associated with ordinary terrestrial life or its reversed equivalent.

In effect, the light signals could serve as a molecular fingerprint, revealing differences that might otherwise be difficult to identify.

However, relying on a single fluorescent probe could produce misleading results. To improve reliability and reduce the risk of false readings, the team proposes combining several probes designed to recognize different cellular structures.

By examining multiple signals together, researchers hope to develop a more dependable way to distinguish conventional organisms from hypothetical mirror life.

Testing the Technology Without Creating Mirror Organisms

One important advantage of the proposed technique is that scientists could begin investigating and refining it using ordinary biological samples. There would be no need to manufacture mirror organisms simply to explore whether the underlying detection principles work.

The researchers also believe the method could eventually be incorporated into several types of scientific equipment, potentially allowing mirror-life screening in different environments.

Professor Pal has already demonstrated laboratory-scale CPL detection technologies involving microscopes, cameras, and biological cell plate readers, which are instruments commonly used to examine large numbers of cell samples.

However, the complete detection method specifically designed to identify mirror life has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Further research will be necessary to determine whether the proposed approach can reliably distinguish between different forms of molecular handedness in biological samples.

Professor Pal added: "CPL detection relies on the same fundamental concept that allows modern 3D cinema glasses to create a stereoscopic image.

"This CPL detection approach can be adapted to many types of systems for analysis of cells, including biological plate readers, microscopes, and cameras. This means our mirror life assay approach could be generalized to many environments depending on end user needs."

#4204. Tenebris Somnia Review – A Hair-Raising And Entertaining Throwback

Tenebris Somnia channels the old-school appeal of classic point-and-click adventures, survival horror, and full-motion-video (FMV) experiences, then smartly modernizes them to create a creepy, entertaining experience. Surviving the twisted threats of a demonic dream world is a tightly packed adventure that rewards you with what’s essentially an impressive indie horror film peppered throughout. It’s a nightmare you may not want to wake up from.

The blend of live-action and in-game storytelling works well in Tenebris Somina’s adventure, which begins when protagonist Julia visits the apartment of her filmmaker ex-boyfriend, Ivan, after suffering recurring nightmares of him being massacred by monsters. What begins as a well-meaning check-in on his status spirals down an unsettling rabbit hole when Julia’s worst fears are realized and then some. The dialogue-free tale presents a scary but fascinating exploration of obsession, both in art and in romance, that constantly delivers surprising, and at times upsetting, revelations during its eight-hour runtime. While Tenebris Somnia sometimes leans a little too far into “tell, don’t show” territory when explaining the cool lore of its supernatural threats, I never felt lost or confused about the wild things that regularly unfold on screen.

FMV experiences are nothing new, but Tenebris Somnia’s live-action scenes are a highlight. They’re well shot, sport impressively gruesome practical effects, and even present visual clues that make them important to gameplay. These scenes are additive, never taking too much time away from the in-game action, and they feel well placed to elicit the strongest emotional response. I also appreciate how the live scenes fit thematically, as much of Tenebris Somnia’s tale centers on a horror film shot by Ivan and Julia that significantly impacted their former relationship; at times, it’s like watching a movie about the movie. While I enjoy smaller moments, like opening a door to trigger a live-action jump scare, the filmmaking succeeds in selling Julia's emotions and the cast's reactions. Seeing real fear, concern, and determination in Julia’s eyes as she navigates a dreamworld made up of Ivan’s memories helped endear me to what would otherwise be a featureless pixel sprite.

Gameplay may look unimpressive due to its old-school adventure game presentation, but Tenebris Somnia’s spin on familiar survival-horror mechanics hits the right notes. Taking place entirely within a dreamlike version of a four-story apartment building, you’ll traverse floors collecting and combining objects and deciphering clues to solve well-crafted puzzles. I’m impressed by how well the game’s puzzles make the most of its limited graphical presentation, including clever scenarios involving mirror/screen reflections I won’t spoil, and how consistently creative they are. One devious exercise even utilizes the game’s limited save points as an unexpected resource in a way that changes how you approach your adventure going forward. No matter how strange puzzles become, they are never opaque, requiring just enough out-of-the-box thinking to make me feel smart when I connect the dots.

Battling grotesque monsters (which, naturally, look even creepier in live-action) also feels good, with melee and gunplay sporting deliberate animations reminiscent of classic adventure games without feeling mechanically clunky. Taking on multiple enemies by swinging a pipe or blowing them away with a shotgun feels better than it looks, and the most intense encounters end just as they approach the line of becoming overwhelming.

Boss battles, perhaps most unexpectedly for a largely puzzle-focused adventure, are fun and distinct from each other, be it wave-based bouts against small gremlin-like foes, protecting a fragile effigy of yourself from a parade of demented house servants, or battling literal mirror images of a tentacled flesh monster the Resident Evil series would be proud to claim. Managing limited ammo and health strikes a good balance of feeling tense but not oppressive. Playing conservatively is rewarding, as few non-boss threats need to be engaged if you can evade them well enough. But when I did find myself down to my last bullet or on the verge of death, Tenebris Somnia lets you feel the pinch just long enough before dropping a box of bullets or a health vial at the right moment.

Tenebris Somnia mines the enjoyable nostalgia of playing the horror games of yesteryear without feeling dated in the worst way. Weaving a solid indie horror film adds to the experience rather than feeling like a gimmick. The result is a captivating, well-executed survival-horror romp that maintains a hair-raising atmosphere with a fraction of the pixels of other genre giants.

Natalie Wood with her daughters Natasha and Courtney.

#4804. Some people really are werewolves—at least, in their minds

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

Dr. Nick Medford’s patient had plenty of reasons to be terrified of a simple blood test.

His body had shrunk down to a fraction of its size, or so he believed, and the physicians looming over him were enormous. Then there was the needle about to pierce his vein: to the patient’s eye, it was larger than his entire arm.

It might sound like something out of Alice in Wonderland. But this patient’s story belonged to a different genre entirely. Horror, namely. As far as the patient was concerned, there was no point in taking his blood. It was no longer human. Neither was he.

Medford’s patient was suffering from clinical lycanthropy, defined as the delusional belief that one is turning into a werewolf. And unlike the flesh-tearing beasts of folklore, who terrorize the unsuspecting by the light of the full moon, patients like Medford’s are often the ones haunted, trapped inside a body-horror nightmare.

According to Medford, his patient knew something was wrong, but was as confused about his condition as anyone. “Not only did he think he was a werewolf, but he would also talk about things like wanting to realign the cosmos and wanting to kill God,” says Medford, a neuropsychiatrist with the South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust.

“And then you would say, ‘Do you believe in God? Are you religious?’ And he’d say, ‘No.’ His ideas didn’t really make sense even to him, which was a part of his distress.”

So, what causes this rare condition? And why does it take the form of one of Halloween’s classic monsters?

If you’ve never heard of clinical lycanthropy before, there’s a good reason: Hollywood’s werewolves easily outnumber the real-life cases of this condition. Only about 25 instances have been reported in the medical literature.

Because there are so few cases, Medford says it is difficult to identify a common cause or predictable disease course. However, he says that typically it is seen in patients already experiencing mental illness.

“In around 40 percent of published cases, it occurs in the context of schizophrenia,” Medford says. “There are also reports of it occurring in the context of bipolar disorder or psychotic depression. So, in the context of severe mental illness, basically.”

Medford’s own patient had been diagnosed with Huntington’s disease, an inherited, fatal brain disease that causes psychiatric symptoms and uncontrolled body movements. The case, which Medford documented in a 2023 report, was the only published instance of clinical lycanthropy associated with Huntington’s. Although Medford says it is uncertain what then led his patient to believe he was a werewolf, he says the physical changes associated with Huntington’s may have fed this idea.

“There may be some change in the person’s body or in their perception or their experience of their body,” Medford says. “That might be, if you like, the seed from which this idea can develop.”

This seems possible in the case of Medford’s patient, who exhibited involuntary writhing movements and other strange physical sensations.

“He did not seem to be aware that he was having these abnormal movements, but he was aware of some weird sensations,” he says. “He described freezing sensations and electric-shock-like sensations. He had some sense that something strange was happening in his body.”

But physical changes alone don’t explain why the patient imagined he was turning into a werewolf. Which raises the question: Why do these delusions so often focus on lupine metamorphoses?

They don’t always, Medford notes. There’s a broader term—therianthropy—for the delusion that one has turned into any animal. But most of these cases involve canines, he acknowledges.

“Most of them are wolves or dogs or something of that type,” he says. “And then there’s a whole range of other strange things. There’s a report of someone who thought they’d turned into a bee.” That would make for an underwhelming horror movie, to be sure.

So, is it a coincidence that many of these patients report delusions ripped straight from a creature feature?

Probably not. In their case report, Medford and his co-author, neuroscientist Natasha Sigala, note that “delusions are influenced by culture,” and few cultural images of human/animal shapeshifting are as deeply embedded as the werewolf.

“The werewolf folklore and werewolf mythology goes right back to antiquity,” Medford says. “And it’s kind of interesting that the werewolf or something like it seems to pop up in a lot of different cultures and different times and places.”

Why is the werewolf legend so potent and enduring? Medford suspects it may tap into something primal.

“The werewolf symbolizes something about the human condition,” he says. “We live in civilized societies, and we adhere to social norms, but there’s a sort of animal part of us that perhaps chafes against that.”

That internal conflict may explain some of the patient’s other fixations. He described urges to strangle his wife and to defecate in a church. Medford notes that Huntington’s may also have impaired his sense of disgust, which can happen as the disease progresses.

Yet the patient also fought against his own impulses. At times, he lunged at staff, only to pull back and apologize.

“You could see the conflict in that moment,” Medford says. In that sense, the patient wasn’t so far from the werewolves of fiction. Like a movie werewolf beholding his mirror image in horror, he looked at his reflection and saw himself changing in ways he couldn’t control.

For Medford’s patient, that dreaded blood test ultimately was part of a turning point in his treatment. The bloodwork allowed Medford to keep the patient on clozapine, a potent antipsychotic. As Medford increased the dose, the patient’s delusional beliefs dwindled, and although the underlying Huntington’s disease remained, the werewolf soon was gone.

Medford hopes patients like his will be treated with compassion rather than sensationalism. “These cases, they’re rare, but when they do happen, they tend to arise in the context of serious psychiatric or neurological disturbance,” Medford says. “They are people meriting sympathy and treatment, rather than romancing about it.”

#4833. In 1871, this nightmarish ‘Creeping Baby Doll’ was all the rage

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 21, 2026 1:13 PM EDT

Already looking for children’s toys for the holidays? Are you also a big Halloween fan? Well, do we have a  gift for you! Allow us to introduce the present no child could ever love (unless maybe they were possessed by a demon or a big fan of The Nightmare Before Christmas): The Creeping Baby Doll!

This nightmarish 1871 creation is everything you don’t want in a children’s toy. It’s noisy. It moves—seemingly propelled by painted plaster baby arms and legs. And it’s way creepy.

The mechanical baby doll has two legs, two arms, and a head all made of painted plaster (which is undoubtedly a bit chipped after more than 150 years). These plaster bits are really the only thing that actually look somewhat like a baby. The rest of the doll’s body is made up of brass clockwork. Some sort of dress or outfit would’ve likely covered its brass body back in the day.

Both the arms and legs are attached to gears that make the plaster limbs move so it looks like the baby doll is crawling (hence, the name—though we would argue there’s perhaps a reason they went with “creeping” instead of “crawling”). The entirety of the toy moves using two toothed wheels hidden under the baby’s body. A flat piece of wood covers the baby’s back to hide the mechanical instruments that make up the baby’s insides.

New York City-based inventor George Pemberton Clarke developed the horrific toy as an improvement to his colleague’s own creeping baby doll. That’s right, there were two! His associate, inventor Robert J. Clay, had filed the patent for his own creeping baby doll on March 14, 1871. Clarke later filed his patent on August 29th of that same year. They really thought this toy would be a hit apparently.

To be fair, there’s a long history of inventors trying to mimic human movement in machines. As far back as ancient Greece, there are stories of human mechanical models dumbfounding awestruck audiences. In the ninth century, Persian alchemist Jabir ibn Hayyan created moving statues. In the 10th century, Chinese engineer Yan Shi (nicknamed the Artificer) created life-size, human-like, moving figurines to the delight of King Mu of Zhou.

Mechanical automata reached their heyday during the Enlightenment and into the Victorian age. Automated sailing ships decorated tables, mechanical birds sang from gilded boxes, and silver swans floated down peaceful streams.

But even when you take into account the long history of automata, it is hard to look at Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll and not think it’s a horror movie prop. In fact, it kind of was.

According to a Boing Boing, the antique toy actually inspired a very obscure 1972 horror film called Miriam: The Wind-Up Killer Doll. The film follows a group of friends who find a vintage crawling baby doll at a garage sale. But, in true horror movie fashion, an evil force possesses the baby doll, and the demonic doll starts killing off the friends one by one. (We didn’t say it was an original scary movie.)

That said, maybe we don’t actually recommend getting your favorite child Clarke’s Creeping Baby Doll. That decision may come back to haunt you.

#4399. Dawn of War 4 clearly loves the Warhammer 40K strategy games that came before, but it misses that they loved to reinvent themselves

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I may have set my expectations for Dawn of War 4 too high, but it's a series that, to my mind, never retreads the same ground. It's therefore been disappointing to feel, having played it across two its campaigns that this long-awaited sequel may have taken too few risks - or been too reverent of the games that came before.

Back in the hands of Relic Entertainment, the original Dawn of War was closest to a traditional RTS game. Yet its use of cover mechanics, unit customisation, and onscreen violence felt distinctly new compared to other games I was playing at the time. As someone who'd grown up on Age of Empires and Command & Conquer, seeing units pair off and take part in animated scuffles brought the battlefield to life, especially after spending years of Saturday afternoons sliding static Warhammer figurines around a tabletop.

Then, in Dawn of War 2, Relic dropped traditional basebuilding and pushed the camera in to focus the action on commanding just a few squads. It upended the tried and tested mechanics of the genre I was used to. The hard unit cap meant it simply wasn't possible to assemble a vast army with which to overwhelm the enemy. The only way to succeed was to use each differently-specced squad as it was designed: heavy bolters supplying cover, assault marines launching into the fray to break up the enemy's defensive positions, recon units circumventing the frontline to attack a vulnerable flank. It was a bold reinvention when they could have just offered more of the same.

Admittedly, I played much less of Dawn of War 3, but it too was responding and reacting to what was happening in the RTS genre at the time. Dota 2 and League of Legends were in their ascendency and, rather than ignore the former mods of RTS games, Relic tried to thread their DNA back into the Warhammer-y battlefield. Hero characters were beefed up and each unit gained special abilities to be used in combat. Armies were made both larger and weaker, meaning there was a much higher death toll in each mission. And base building returned, but cover was ejected, changing the dynamics we had got used to in the previous two games.

I run through all this history so you can see why Dawn of War 4 is both a good game and an unexciting one. From what I've played of the Orks and Adeptus Mechanicus campaigns, it can produce enjoyable afternoons of pushing virtual soldiers around a sci-fi battlefield, but all the while it feels too familiar to what came before it. King Art Games have made no secret that of the Dawn of War games, they've mostly closely been cribbing from the original. The battles aren't as large as Dawn of War 3, but the unit cap is significantly larger than Dawn of War 2, and both base building and cover make a return. As a result of this deference to the 2004 original, m first impression – and this is a preview rather than a review – is that Dawn of War 4 is a competent RTS that immediately feels dated.

I went back to the original Dawn of War to compare the two. Dawn of War 4 doesn't entirely miss the opportunity to expand: your units, for instance, have far more customisation options, allowing you to swap out their weapons and equip them with additional abilities. Plus, there are more tools available for grouping and selecting your units, making it a little easier to manage your forces. And, in all the ways you would expect, it looks like a more modern game, with sharper looking textures, more detailed models, and a camera that lets you peer even closer upon the violence. But while the original Dawn of War is fun to play today, it has decades-old issues that nonetheless crop up in Dawn of War 4 as well.

Despite your being able to group units, select all on screen, and select all units of single type, the larger your army grows, the less your ability to make meaningful tactical choices becomes. The unit toolbar, which Dawn of War 4 places at the bottom of your screen, fills with squashed unit portraits that eventually become indistinguishable. The Orks suffer from this more even than the Adeptus Mechanicus, as their roster is so cheap to recruit. With the growing inability to be selective, in mission after mission, I just grabbed as many units as I could and threw them at the enemies' defences. It was effective and I don't want to complain too much about the victories, but they weren't satisfying when attaining them involved so few of my own, meaningful choices. This was also the weakness of the original Dawn of War's design and why the move to smaller squads to focusing on squads was so effective in its sequel. The ability to roll over your enemy with a large army was removed entirely.

The Adeptus Mechanicus, at least, make a much better first impression. Unlike the Waaargh-obsessed boyz, it's hard to grab a lump of soldiers, chuck them at the enemy, and expect results. I was more inclined to play around the specialisations of certain units, like the Skitarii Rangers, excellent long-range anti-infantry snipers who - when plonked into cover - will whittle down any greenskins or Necrons that march on their position. Meanwhile, the Skitarii Vanguard, armed with radiation-emitting ammunition, can vapourise the more heavily-armoured troops. However, both Skitarii ranged units are truly awful in melee, so to keep them effective, I paired them with units of Sicarian Ruststalkers. These blade-armed cyborg assassins are brutal in close combat, and can hold back infantry while the Rangers and Vanguard finish them off. Combine and position your units well, and you'll scythe through enemy troops, but do it haphazardly and they'll crumble just as quickly.

The early missions in the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign introduce you to Potentia Delta-9, a Technoarcheologist exploring the surface of Kronus to learn about what stirs in its depths, and Skitarii Marshal Sek-IX-23, who is doing his best to put down the Ork uprising. It was the opening where the Adeptus Mechanicus were most enjoyable to command., the shape and smaller scale of the battlefields giving a lot more opportunities to embrace the tactics of managing their extreme strengths and weaknesses.

In one early defence mission, Potentia finds herself surrounded by newly-awoken Necron troops. (If you've seen Dawn of War 4's trailers or know the history of planet Kronus from the previous games, you won't be surprised to learn it's Necrons that are stirring from a decades-long slumber, like Sleeping Beauty if she were clad in self-repairing battle armour.) You must hold out against waves of the immortal robots with their fizzing green eyes and even fizzier laser cannons. It's a set up that pressures you into thoughtfully applying your resources, ensuring that each entrance to your base has a firm defensive line composed of mixed troops. Then, as the waves against you grow stronger, and the attacks come against you from different angles, you must relocate your forces to bloody the nose of the attackers. Complicating things is the appearance of trapped Adeptus Mechanicus units around the map; if you can break out from your base and rescue them, they'll bolster your army, but at the risk of depleting your defences.

It's a mission design I've seen before in many RTS games, which does undermine the enjoyment, but it hints at the tactical play that's missing in the Ork campaign. Sadly, the further I get into the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign, the larger the battles become, with fewer opportunities to focus on unit-specific tactics. The result is that these too are won by treating your forces as a bolter-armed blob that just rolls over the enemy. It doesn't matter whether you're fighting Necrons or Orks, they all fall down before the bolter-blob.

As I say, I may have set my expectations for a new Dawn of War game too high. Over the past year, I've spoken to the team about their attention to detail in capturing units in the Adeptus Mechanicus and Necron armies, like the perpetual motion machine walkers that can't stand still, or the mega unit you can summon who makes no secret of the fact they hate you. Those conversations only stoked my hopes, which also might point to why I'm feeling a little deflated after what I've played.

I will still be playing through the full four campaigns when I get the opportunity (the preview build is limited to two of the four factions and I'm only able to talk about the opening six missions in each of those stories). And one change I will be making will be to whack the difficulty up. If I can make whomping the enemy harder, perhaps it will push me into thinking more tactically.

Still, while that may make Dawn of War 4 more enjoyable, it won't hide the fact this feels like a missed opportunity in a series that has marked itself by its reinvention, especially as it's recreated weaknesses of the original game which were addressed in its sequels.
