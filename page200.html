#541. Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Astronomers may have found the first moon beyond our Solar System, but the mysterious object is unlike any moon we know. Observations from the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope (ESO's VLT) have uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body in the CD-35 2722 star system. Rather than orbiting a planet, this unusual object circles a brown dwarf, a celestial body more massive than a planet but not massive enough to become a star.

If confirmed, the discovery could mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System, potentially changing how astronomers define moons and planets.

Kevin Hoy, an ESO student based in Chile and lead author of the study published in Nature, spent months examining the CD-35 2722 system. He describes its unusual arrangement as "super weird" compared with our own Solar System.

At the center of this relatively young system is CD-35 2722, a star with approximately half the Sun's mass. Orbiting the star is a brown dwarf, an object that occupies the murky boundary between planets and stars. Brown dwarfs are generally too small to sustain the hydrogen fusion that powers ordinary stars, yet they are more massive than planets.

The newly identified object orbits this brown dwarf, creating a remarkable arrangement in which a massive satellite circles an object that itself revolves around a star.

"This system is somewhat hard to define using Solar-System-based words like 'planet' and 'moon'," states Hoy, who is also affiliated with the Universidad Diego Portales and the Millennium Nucleus of Young Exoplanets and their Moons (YEMS) in Chile.

The researchers refer to the newly detected object as an exosatellite. It has at least as much mass as Jupiter, while the brown dwarf it orbits is more than 30 times as massive as Jupiter. This makes the possible moon far more massive than any natural satellite in our Solar System.

"The exosatellite is clearly massive enough to be a planet, but it does not orbit a star, though it orbits an object that orbits a star," says Hoy. "Being the third wheel in this system makes us want to call it a moon, even if it is nothing like the small, rocky moons we have in our system."

A Giant Exomoon That Challenges Cosmic Definitions

The unusual discovery highlights a fundamental problem with applying familiar astronomical labels to objects found in distant star systems.

An exosatellite is a natural satellite located beyond our Solar System, while the term exomoon generally refers to a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another celestial body outside our Solar System. However, astronomers have not established a universally accepted definition of an exomoon.

That distinction becomes especially important in the CD-35 2722 system, where the possible moon is itself massive enough to be considered a planet under some classification schemes.

Alice Zurlo, director of YEMS and a collaborator on the study, explains: "The satellite we report is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion, itself several times the mass of Jupiter."

The arrangement is very different from the familiar structure of our Solar System, where planets orbit the Sun and moons orbit planets.

"We have a clear delineation between the planets and the Sun in the Solar System, so defining things like moons is simple. In the CD-35 2722 system, where we are blurring the lines between stars, planets, and moons, the whole thing becomes more complicated to describe," adds Zurlo, who is also an astrophysicist at Universidad Diego Portales.

Scientists Search for the First Confirmed Exomoon

Astronomers have spent years looking for moons around objects beyond our Solar System, but no candidate has yet been detected with enough certainty to establish a confirmed discovery.

That makes the latest findings particularly significant. Although scientists have identified more than 6,000 exoplanets, only a handful of possible exomoons have emerged from observations, and the evidence supporting them remains limited.

Earlier in 2026, a research team led by Quentin Kral reported possible signs of a satellite in the HD 206893 star system. Their observations, obtained with ESO's Very Large Telescope Interferometer, suggested that a moon-like companion might exist, but they did not establish a definitive detection.

The CD-35 2722 system now offers another promising opportunity, supported by measurements of how the suspected moon affects the motion of its larger companion.

How Astronomers Detected the Possible Alien Moon

To investigate CD-35 2722, Hoy, Zurlo, and their colleagues used the CRIRES+ instrument installed on ESO's Very Large Telescope.

The team applied a technique known as the radial velocity method, which was also used to discover the first exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star.

This approach works by measuring extremely small changes in an object's motion. When two celestial bodies orbit one another, their mutual gravitational attraction causes both to move around a shared center of mass. Even a much smaller companion can produce a detectable wobble in the larger object's motion.

By examining subtle changes in the brown dwarf's motion, the researchers identified what they consider strong evidence that another massive body is orbiting it.

"As exotic as it is, this system is truly unique and represents a breakthrough: the first plausible detection of an exosatellite," says Zurlo.

Confirming moons beyond our Solar System would do more than add another category of objects to astronomical catalogs. These discoveries could help researchers understand how planetary systems form, how their components interact, and why some systems develop structures dramatically different from our own.

Future observations could also reveal whether massive satellites like the one proposed in CD-35 2722 are rare exceptions or part of a much broader population of unusual celestial objects.

ESO's upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is expected to play a major role in that search. Equipped with an enormous 39-meter mirror and advanced scientific instruments, the telescope will give astronomers the ability to detect smaller exomoons that are currently beyond the reach of existing observatories.

As scientists begin identifying more satellites in distant planetary systems, they may discover that familiar distinctions between stars, planets, and moons are not as universal as once assumed.

A satellite is any object that travels around another object. Satellites can be natural (like our own moon) or artificial (like a spacecraft).

An exosatellite is a satellite located outside our Solar System. The term exomoon generally describes a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another object beyond our Solar System, although astronomers have not agreed on an official definition.

The possible companion in CD-35 2722 illustrates why that definition matters. With a mass comparable to or greater than Jupiter's, it resembles a planet in size and composition, yet its orbital relationship with the brown dwarf makes it a candidate for an entirely different classification.

#4541. How This Former Weightlifter Lost 80 Pounds After GLP-1s Stopped Working

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

For years, Dana Bichler was a competitive weightlifter. He felt fit and strong competing at 200 to 210 pounds. But wear and tear on his body and aging eventually pushed him to step away from weightlifting. Without the training, he began to gain weight slowly and steadily.

“I tried running, and a few other things, and the weight just crept up,” he told Healthline.

Dieting allowed him to lose about 50 pounds, but he would then regain it. At his heaviest, he weighed close to 300 pounds, which inspired him to visit a weight-loss clinic, where he followed a plan centered on the clinic’s foods.

“It’s a life of deprivation,” Bichler said. “You just can’t live like it.”

About five years ago, he tried a GLP-1 medication and two others a few years later. Each helped him lose weight initially but then stopped working.

“There was really no long-term positive effects or benefits that I saw,” he said.

However, he still takes Mounjaro for blood sugar control because he is prediabetic.

A few years ago, he was diagnosed with fatty liver disease. His condition was tied to obesity and caused scarring on his liver.

Bichler understood the seriousness of the condition as his two younger sisters and his oldest brother also had fatty liver disease. His brother and youngest sister developed cirrhosis, and his brother died after the disease affected other parts of his body.

“I had to do something,” he said. “What I was doing was not working.”

From his home in St. Cloud, Minnesota, Bichler traveled to the University of Minnesota to see a hepatologist, who referred him to a gastroenterologist who had worked with Boston Scientific on the instruments used to perform endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG).

ESG is a minimally invasive weight loss procedure performed using an FDA-authorized endoscopic suturing system, such as one offered through Boston Scientific’s Endura Weight Loss Solutions.

During ESG, a doctor passes an endoscope through the mouth and uses sutures to fold the stomach into a narrower sleeve. No portion of the stomach is removed, and there are no incisions. The smaller stomach limits how much a person can eat at once.

“Patients typically go home the same day and can return to routine daily activities within two to three days,” Brian Dunkin, MD, chief medical officer at Boston Scientific, told Healthline. “For the right person, that provides another less-invasive option to consider with their healthcare professional.”

While ESG may be a good option for some people who don’t achieve weight loss goals with GLP-1 medications, can’t tolerate them, or don’t want to stay on medication long term, Dunkin said he doesn’t think of it as ESG versus GLP-1 drugs.

“One of the best things GLP-1 medications have done is get more people talking to their doctors about obesity and recognizing it as a chronic disease that deserves treatment,” he said.

Each approach has different benefits, risks, and side effects.

“The goal is finding the right treatment, or combination of treatments, for the right patient at the right time,” said Dunkin.

ESG is indicated for adults with obesity with a BMI between 30 and 50, but Dunkin said BMI is only part of the conversation. ESG may be an option for people who aren’t getting the weight loss results they want, who don’t want to stay on weight loss medication long term, or for whom bariatric surgery may not be appropriate or desired, he added.

“Obesity is a chronic disease that requires long-term care,” said Dunkin. “ESG is one part of that care, which is why I believe so strongly in a multidisciplinary approach. The goal is to look at the whole person and find a care plan they can sustain over time.”

In March 2025, Bichler decided ESG was a good fit for him and received the procedure at Boston Scientific. At the time, he was 67 years old and on Medicare, which pre-approved the procedure.

“I just felt comfortable with it not being invasive, and I really needed to deal with [my weight], so I did,” he said.

When Bichler underwent ESG he weighed somewhere in the 260s. Today, he weighs 178 pounds.

While the procedure went smoothly, he said the following month was difficult. Because bread, pasta, rice, and carbonated drinks were off the table, he had to consume clear liquids, then slowly moved to other liquids, and eventually cream soups.

“I found that extremely difficult,” he said.

What surprised him most was depression. Looking back, he calls it a perfect storm. Working with his weight loss doctor, he had stopped taking his antidepressant about two weeks before the procedure. After surgery, his eating habits changed dramatically, disrupting the social life he’d built around food.

“I was in a pretty deep funk for a while,” he said. “I really was.”

He restarted his antidepressant, and over time, he adjusted to a new way of eating.

“I’ve discovered I like cream soup,” he said. “I still have it for meals.”

Although he’s lost more than 80 pounds, Bichler said the bigger change is how good he feels. He exercises four to five days a week and has more energy and moves more easily than he has in years.

“I haven’t felt this good in 30 years,” Bichler said. “I’m not kidding you.”

He’s able to enjoy things he loves like working on the farm he and his wife own. At the farm, he hunts ducks, turkeys, pheasants, and small game, and runs a trapline, all activities his weight had made nearly impossible for a long time.

“It used to be really hard for me to do because of the physicality of doing some of these things,” he said.

Although he retired from his profession, Bichler said he’s busier than ever. He’s a protodeacon in the Eastern Orthodox Church and also serves as a chaplain for his local police department, a role that has him climbing in and out of squad cars and up and down steps.

“Before I’d be sucking air trying to keep up,” he said.

Most meaningful of all is that he has more energy to give to his family.

“I have a grandson that I can actually do things and be active with now,” Bichler said. “It’s like starting all over. I just feel like a new person.”

#568. Scientists discover a hidden flaw that challenges 40 years of superconductor science

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Warwick have uncovered a surprising secret inside a superconductor that researchers have studied for 40 years. Long believed to have a uniform crystal structure, the material actually contains a complex patchwork of different atomic arrangements. Using an advanced 3D imaging technique, the team was able to see deep inside the crystal for the first time, revealing hidden features that challenge decades of scientific assumptions.

High-temperature superconductivity ranks among the most important scientific breakthroughs of the past four decades. Superconductors can carry electrical current without any resistance, eliminating the energy losses that normally occur as electricity travels through conventional materials. They also have the potential to support extraordinarily powerful magnets, making them promising for future power grids, medical scanners, and quantum computers.

The new findings, published in Physical Review Letters, come from researchers in Warwick's Department of Chemistry working with the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. Their investigation focused on a class of copper-based superconductors known as cuprates, which can conduct electricity without resistance at unusually high (though still very cold) temperatures.

Advanced 3D Imaging Reveals a Hidden Crystal Structure

For four decades, scientists have generally built their theories of cuprate superconductivity around the assumption that these materials have the same crystal structure throughout their interiors. The new research shows that this fundamental assumption does not always hold.

To investigate, the team used scanning 3D X-ray diffraction, a sophisticated imaging method that reveals how atoms are arranged deep within a crystal. The approach is similar in principle to a medical CT scan, except that it maps atomic structures rather than tissues and organs.

This allowed the researchers to construct a detailed three-dimensional picture of the superconductor's interior, exposing structural variations that conventional techniques had not revealed.

Instead of finding a uniform crystal, they discovered a patchwork of regions containing two slightly different atomic arrangements. Even more surprising were the boundaries separating these regions, which were hundreds of times wider than expected.

These unusually broad transition zones were not simply narrow dividing lines between neighboring structures. They were extensive enough to behave almost like distinct structures themselves, potentially influencing how electricity moves through the material.

A 40-Year Assumption About Superconductors Overturned

Professor Mark Senn, who led the study at the University of Warwick's Department of Chemistry, explained why the findings could have major implications for superconductivity research.

"For forty years, the working assumption has been that these materials are the same all the way through, and nearly all the theory is built on that picture. We've shown it doesn't hold. The crystal is fundamentally patchworked and textured, with unusually wide boundary regions that likely work against superconductivity rather than just sitting alongside it.

"This might explain why some materials perform better than others and means some existing bulk measurements will need to be reinterpreted and future theoretical models built that incorporate this structural complexity."

The discovery suggests that the internal boundaries may actively interfere with superconductivity rather than simply coexist with it. If that is the case, the hidden structural differences could help explain why certain superconducting materials perform better than others.

It also means that some previous experiments, particularly those measuring the overall properties of these materials, may need to be reconsidered. Future theories will likely need to account for the complex structures inside real crystals rather than treating them as perfectly uniform.

What the Discovery Means for Future Superconductors

The researchers suspect that similar hidden structural patterns may be widespread among other cuprate superconductors. They could also occur in related materials being investigated for their ability to become superconducting under extreme pressure.

Beyond challenging existing theories, the study demonstrates the power of a new generation of imaging technology.

The advanced measurements were made possible by a recent 150 million euro upgrade to the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which provides extremely intense X-ray beams for studying materials in extraordinary detail.

This technology now allows scientists to examine the three-dimensional interiors of many different materials with a level of precision that was previously unavailable. By revealing how hidden atomic structures affect a material's behavior, the technique could provide valuable insights across materials science.

For superconductivity research, the implications are particularly important. Understanding how internal crystal structures influence electrical performance could eventually help scientists identify ways to improve superconducting materials, bringing more advanced energy systems, powerful magnets, medical imaging devices, and quantum technologies closer to reality.

Kim Aris tells the Guardian chance to speak with detained Nobel laureate was ‘public relations’ move from military leaders

Myanmar’s former democratic leader Aung San Suu Kyi appears to be in good physical and mental health but is living in near-complete isolation, her son has said after the first call with his 81-year-old mother in more than five years.

“She still sounds certainly all there mentally, and physically she sounds strong,” Kim Aris said. “I don’t think she has anybody outside of the military or the police around her, so essentially she’s been held in solitary confinement from what I gather.”

After being deposed in a 2021 military coup, the Nobel Peace prize laureate has been moved to an unspecified location in the capital. “I’m still not entirely sure where she is,” said Aris. “I think it’s a residence in Naypyidaw, which is fenced off and it has a scrubby bit of land around it.”

Aris, who has lived in the UK most of his life and whose late father was a British historian, was allowed to speak to his mother on Thursday at the Myanmar embassy in London. While he welcomed the chance to talk, he said the invitation was part of a “public relations” campaign by the country’s military leaders.

The former junta chief Min Aung Hlaing has sought to rebuild international diplomatic ties, and in April switched his military fatigues for civilian clothes after elections that were widely condemned as a sham.

Activists have long demanded “proof of life” for Aung San Suu Kyi, as well as scores of other political prisoners. In August, she was finally photographed during a Red Cross visit. Last week, a government spokesperson published images of her smiling in a wood-panelled room as officials delivered two gardening books given to her as gifts by Britain’s King Charles. A paper calendar on the wall behind her showed the date.

Aris said these photo-ops were well-known stunts that the regime had used before to pretend his mother, a national icon, was being treated fairly. The daughter of the assassinated national independence hero Gen Aung San has spent much of her life since the late 1980s in and out of house arrest for leading pro-democracy movements.

“She remains completely shut off from the rest of the world. I think she’s unaware of what’s happening outside of where she is because she’s not allowed any newspapers or radio or television,” Aris said. “She’s been locked up for five years on trumped-up, politically motivated charges and she should be free.”

Ruled for decades by generals and suffering from western sanctions, Myanmar experienced a brief democratic moment after Aung San Suu Kyi was allowed to run in a 2015 election that her party overwhelmingly won. She took up the role of “state counsellor” under a civilian-military power-sharing agreement.

Her international reputation was soon tarnished after the 2016-2017 mass killings and expulsions of Rohingya Muslims. Aung San Suu Kyi flew to the international court of justice in The Hague, where she said she acknowledged military units might have used “disproportionate force”, but defended the government against accusations of genocide.

Aris said that his mother was never “silent” on the Rohingya crisis and what she said on the issue “could fill volumes”. Under her leadership, Myanmar launched a domestic inquiry and appointed the former UN chief Kofi Annan to head a commission.

He added that as state counsellor, Aung San Suu Kyi was “always walking on a tightrope”, knowing the military could strip her of her fragile power at any moment.

“The very hard-won nascent democracy that she was trying to lead was holding on to things by a thread,” he said. “The military could have taken over at any point, and they were doing their best to make my mother’s situation a lot more difficult and to undermine her position. And I think in that respect they did a pretty good job.”

The 2021 power grab by the military triggered a civil war that has killed more than 100,000 people, according to conflict monitors.

Aris said the level of public global support for his mother had “dwindled incredibly” since the Rohingya crisis and that the whole country was now suffering from a lack of international attention. While many governments have called for Aung San Suu Kyi’s release, she is not treated as the beloved international figure that she once was.

“Never mind my mother, the fact that people ignore what’s happening in Burma shouldn’t be down to what was being done to the Rohingya,” he said, using the country’s former name. “And that seems to be the case sometimes, that people say: ‘Oh, well, look how the Rohingya were treated. We can ignore what’s happening in Burma.’”

#4236. Mediterranean diet may boost tiny proteins that protect the heart and brain

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A new study suggests that the Mediterranean diet may protect the heart, brain, and aging cells through a surprising biological mechanism involving tiny proteins produced by mitochondria, the structures responsible for generating most of the energy our cells need.

Researchers at the USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology discovered that people who more closely followed the Mediterranean diet had higher levels of two mitochondrial microproteins called humanin and SHMOOSE. Both have previously been linked to protection against cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration, the gradual deterioration of nerve cells that occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

The study, led by USC Leonard Davis Instructional Associate Professor of Gerontology Roberto Vicinanza, offers a possible explanation for how everyday food choices influence cellular health and the aging process.

"These microproteins may act as molecular messengers that translate what we eat into how our cells function and age," Vicinanza said. "It's a new biological pathway that helps explain why the Mediterranean diet is so powerful."

How the Mediterranean Diet May Protect Aging Cells

The Mediterranean diet emphasizes foods such as olive oil, fish, beans, lentils, fruits, and vegetables. It has consistently been associated with a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and cognitive decline. However, scientists are still working to understand exactly how this eating pattern produces its wide-ranging health benefits.

To investigate, the researchers examined blood samples from older adults who followed the Mediterranean diet to different degrees. Those whose eating habits most closely matched the diet had significantly higher concentrations of humanin and SHMOOSE.

They also showed lower indicators of oxidative stress, a process in which harmful molecules called reactive oxygen species overwhelm the body's protective defenses. Over time, oxidative stress can damage proteins, DNA, and other cellular components, contributing to aging and chronic disease.

Interestingly, the researchers found that certain foods were associated with different microproteins. Higher consumption of olive oil, fish, and legumes was linked to increased humanin levels. Meanwhile, olive oil consumption and eating fewer refined carbohydrates were associated with higher levels of SHMOOSE.

Refined carbohydrates include foods made with heavily processed grains and sugars, such as white bread, pastries, and many sugary snacks. These foods generally contain less fiber and fewer naturally occurring nutrients than their minimally processed counterparts.

"These findings suggest that specific components of the Mediterranean diet may directly influence mitochondrial biology," said USC Leonard Davis School Dean and USC Distinguished Professor Pinchas Cohen, the study's senior author. "Humanin and SHMOOSE could serve as biomarkers for adherence to the Mediterranean diet and have clinical significance."

Biomarkers are measurable biological indicators that can provide information about a person's health or physiological state. In this case, the researchers suggest that humanin and SHMOOSE could eventually help scientists understand how closely someone's diet aligns with healthy eating patterns and how their cells respond.

Tiny Mitochondrial Proteins With Powerful Effects

The findings extend a scientific effort spanning more than 20 years to investigate small proteins produced by mitochondria. Cohen has played a leading role in discovering and studying these molecules, which are increasingly recognized as important participants in cellular health.

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of cells because they produce much of the energy required for essential biological activities. But their role extends beyond energy production. They also help regulate metabolism, cellular stress responses, and processes associated with aging.

Unlike most proteins, which are produced using instructions stored in the DNA inside the cell's nucleus, mitochondrial microproteins originate from genetic instructions within mitochondria themselves.

These instructions are found in small segments of mitochondrial DNA known as open reading frames. Some of these regions were once considered nonfunctional, but researchers have discovered that they can encode biologically active molecules.

One of the best understood is humanin, first identified by Cohen and fellow researchers in 2003. Subsequent studies have associated humanin with improved insulin sensitivity, better cardiovascular health, increased longevity, and protection of cognitive abilities.

Insulin sensitivity refers to how effectively the body's cells respond to insulin, the hormone that helps regulate blood sugar. Maintaining healthy insulin sensitivity is important for metabolic health and reducing the risk of type 2 diabetes.

Another intriguing microprotein is SHMOOSE (Small Human Mitochondrial ORF Over SErine tRNA), which was identified more recently by Cohen's research team.

SHMOOSE has attracted particular interest because of its possible role in protecting the brain. Scientists have found that a genetic variant of SHMOOSE is associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer's disease. The normal version, meanwhile, appears to help protect neurons from damage involving amyloid, a protein that can accumulate abnormally in the brains of people with Alzheimer's.

"These peptides are emerging as key regulators of aging biology," Cohen said. "They connect mitochondrial function to diseases like Alzheimer's and heart disease and now, potentially, to nutrition."

A Hidden Mechanism for Protecting the Heart

The scientists also identified a potentially important connection between humanin and an enzyme called Nox2, which helps generate reactive oxygen species.

Although these molecules have useful roles in normal cell signaling and immune defense, excessive production can contribute to oxidative stress and damage blood vessels and other tissues.

The researchers observed that people with higher humanin levels tended to have lower Nox2 activity. This relationship raises the possibility that humanin helps limit damaging oxidative processes, potentially offering an additional layer of cardiovascular protection.

Based on these findings, the researchers propose that the Mediterranean diet could support heart health in two complementary ways. The diet may help reduce oxidative stress directly while also supporting the production of mitochondrial microproteins that influence pathways involved in cellular damage.

However, the observed associations do not establish that the diet directly increases these proteins or that the proteins themselves prevent cardiovascular disease.

"This could represent a new cardioprotective mechanism of the Mediterranean diet," Vicinanza said.

Bringing the Mediterranean Diet to a Global Audience

Vicinanza's interest in the Mediterranean diet extends beyond investigating its effects inside the body. He has also worked to promote this traditional eating pattern internationally as a way to support human health, cultural heritage, and environmental sustainability.

As part of those efforts, he collaborated with the Municipality of Pollica in Italy, which is recognized as a UNESCO Mediterranean Diet emblematic community.

That collaboration helped support the establishment of the International Day of the Mediterranean Diet through the United Nations.

Observed every year on November 16, the international event is intended to draw attention to the Mediterranean diet's health benefits as well as its cultural traditions and environmental significance.

For Vicinanza, the new findings reinforce the relationship between traditional food practices and modern discoveries about how the body functions.

"We're connecting centuries-old dietary traditions with cutting-edge molecular biology," Vicinanza said. "It supports the idea that healthy eating patterns with little to no ultra-processed foods reflect how humans have eaten over long periods and may create conditions to which mitochondria -- ancient cellular organelles -- are likely adapted."

Could Tiny Proteins Help Unlock Healthier Aging?

Although the findings are promising, the researchers emphasize that the study was relatively small and observational. That means it identified relationships between dietary habits and biological measurements but could not determine whether eating a Mediterranean diet directly caused the differences in microprotein levels.

Other factors, including participants' overall health and lifestyle, could also influence the results.

Nevertheless, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility for the emerging field of precision nutrition, which seeks to develop dietary recommendations based on an individual's biological characteristics and needs.

If future research confirms that dietary changes can reliably influence humanin, SHMOOSE, and related microproteins, these molecules could potentially become useful tools for evaluating nutritional interventions and developing more personalized strategies for healthy aging.

The next step is to determine whether adopting a Mediterranean-style diet actually increases these proteins and whether those changes lead to measurable improvements in cardiovascular health, brain function, or disease risk.

"Our goal is to move from observing associations to understanding causality," Vicinanza said. "If we can harness these pathways, we may be able to design nutritional strategies that promote healthy aging at the molecular level."

#3584. OpenLDK: Bringing the Java World into Common Lisp

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Many years ago I worked on an alternate Java compiler and runtime called gcj, the GNU Compiler for the Java Programming Language. These days, my preferred programming language for most tasks is Common Lisp, but I’m still interested in Java runtimes.

Back in late 2024, I wanted to use a Java library from SBCL, but couldn’t find an integration approach that suited the application. This led me to explore the idea of building a simple JVM bytecode processor in Lisp so I could execute the code directly in process.

I started before coding agents became useful for this kind of work, and wrote the first working version by hand.

OpenLDK is a JIT compiler and runtime for Java, written in Common Lisp. It reads Java bytecode, translates it into Lisp, and lets SBCL compile that Lisp into native machine code. Java objects live in the same process and heap as the Lisp application.

My goal is to make Java libraries feel at home in Common Lisp. For general Java execution, HotSpot will be much faster.

In the last few days, however, OpenLDK has gotten much better. I’ve experimented with JVM-hosted languages and Spring Boot application execution.

OpenLDK leads to some wonderfully strange possibilities. Java classes become CLOS classes. Lisp can add methods around methods written in Kotlin. A Clojure function can become something you pass to funcall. Java’s own compiler runs inside SBCL. And an initialized Spring Boot application can be saved in a Lisp image and launched again in about 84 milliseconds.

You can even connect Emacs and SLY to a Spring Boot application running on OpenLDK, inspect its objects, and monkey-patch its behavior while it keeps running.

Here is a tour of how it works, what it can do, and what that startup number actually means.

The compilation pipeline begins with ordinary class files. OpenLDK targets JDK 25 and uses OpenJDK’s runtime libraries, reading them from the JDK’s JMOD files or its lib/modules container.

When a class loads, OpenLDK creates its CLOS representation and installs stubs for its methods. Calling a method for the first time triggers compilation: bytecode becomes an intermediate representation, then Lisp, then native code through SBCL. The compiled definition replaces the stub.

That laziness matters. Loading a library does not mean compiling every method in it. Much of a large library may never be used by a particular application.

The signed-field operation preserves Java’s 32-bit integer overflow behavior. With typed operands, SBCL can compile this to a machine addition. OpenLDK handles Java’s semantics while drawing on the compiler SBCL already provides.

The translation also makes the program accessible to Lisp. Java fields become slots. Instance methods become methods of CLOS generic functions, identified by their JVM signatures so overloaded methods remain distinct. Static methods become ordinary Lisp functions. Java’s exception hierarchy has a corresponding Lisp condition hierarchy.

Compiled methods are retained in a persistent code cache. Subsequent runs can load cached code when the classes it depends on remain unchanged, and cache entries are separated by OpenLDK build. Different images can reuse compiled JDK methods too.

A Clojure function can come back as a callable foreign-function object. The surrounding Lisp invokes it with ordinary funcall, with argument and result conversion handled by the interop layer. The reverse direction works too: Lisp functions can become callbacks for the other languages.

Calls from compiled Java, Kotlin, Scala, or Groovy code go through it too.

Java methods participate in CLOS method combination. Lisp can supply :before, :after, and :around behavior at the point where the application needs it.

Lisp can also define a class that Java code sees as an ordinary Java class. OpenLDK’s define-java-class generates a class file and installs it through the application class loader, while its method implementations are Lisp code.

These classes can implement Java interfaces. Their methods can consult Lisp state and call other language components, while Java reflection sees an ordinary Java class.

Error handling participates in the same integration. A NumberFormatException escaping a foreign call becomes a typed Lisp condition, foreign-number-format-error, which is also a parse-error. Other mappings cover arithmetic, file, type, and storage errors.

The foreign-call interface offers use-value and retry restarts. Lisp handlers can supply a replacement value or retry a failed foreign call.

A Lisp error raised inside a callback can travel in the other direction as an appropriate Java exception. If it escapes back to Lisp, the original Lisp condition is recovered. Each side can use its own error-handling facilities.

Collections have similar accommodations. A Java list can be exposed as a Lisp sequence, converting elements as they are accessed. A Lisp sequence or hash table can be presented to Java through a backed list or map view.

The build system joins in as well. ASDF systems can contain Maven JAR components and source components for the supported languages. Kotlin, Scala, and Groovy files compile during ASDF’s compilation operation; their classes become available to later components. Maven components place libraries on the shared classpath. Dependencies must currently be listed explicitly.

Inline Kotlin and Scala blocks can also compile with the surrounding Lisp file when their splices have static types and the language compiler is running. Their class bytes are stored in the FASL. Loading that FASL does not require recompiling those blocks. The interop documentation covers these interfaces and the language-specific conversions.

These features sit on top of some substantial programs already running.

Java’s javac compiler works on OpenLDK, and the build produces a warmed javacl executable. Clojure runs with protocols, records, atoms, and futures. Kawa and JScheme run Scheme. Scala’s compiler and REPL work, Kotlin compiles source into executable class files, and Groovy runs dynamic scripts and statically compiled classes.

ABCL adds a particularly pleasing loop: Common Lisp compiled into Java bytecode, translated back into Common Lisp, then compiled by SBCL. Its REPL, compiler, CLOS, conditions, and Java interop run in that environment.

There are also Spring Boot and Quarkus applications. Spring PetClinic runs with embedded Tomcat, Hibernate, and H2. That is separate from the small command-line application used for the startup measurements below.

All that compilation has a cost. Starting a large language implementation cold can take minutes, because OpenLDK is compiling the implementation itself as it runs. Saving executable images makes a substantial difference.

A warm image runs an application during the build, exercises selected paths, and saves the compiled code. Paths the warmup never exercised still compile on first use.

Application state is a separate choice. By default, dump-warm-image resets application-class static state so initialization can happen again. With :reset-classes nil, it preserves the state left by the warmups. An application designed to reuse that state can begin with much of its initialization already complete.

#2766. Lazio-Parma, cori degli ospiti contro Lotito: applausi biancocelesti

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

All’Olimpico i tifosi del Parma hanno contestato il presidente della Lazio; dai sostenitori di casa sono arrivati applausi.

Durante Lazio-Parma allo Stadio Olimpico, i tifosi del Parma hanno intonato cori contro Claudio Lotito, presidente biancoceleste. I sostenitori della Lazio presenti sugli spalti hanno risposto con applausi. La contestazione è dunque partita dai tifosi ospiti e ha ricevuto l’approvazione del pubblico laziale durante la partita.

L’episodio si è verificato mentre Lazio e Parma si affrontavano all’Olimpico. A intonare i cori contro Lotito sono stati i sostenitori della squadra ospite. Il presidente della Lazio era il destinatario della contestazione, alla quale hanno poi reagito i tifosi biancocelesti presenti allo stadio.

Ai cori rivolti contro Lotito sono seguiti gli applausi dei tifosi della Lazio. I sostenitori biancocelesti sugli spalti hanno così manifestato la propria approvazione per la contestazione intonata dagli ospiti. Sono questi i due momenti descritti: prima i cori dei tifosi del Parma, poi la risposta dei laziali.
