#3475. Anthropic's political problem will get worse before it gets better

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Its IPO paperwork reveal a company rightly worried about its regulatory future.

Anthropic filed paperwork to go public on Monday, and while its list of business risk factors spans the fanciful (sentient AI) and the mundane (losing its executives), one stands above the rest as a likely source of headaches in the near future: the "evolving and uncertain regulatory and political environment." With an expected IPO around mid-November, and new representatives from the midterms elections to be sworn in in January, the company has a scant few months before it's almost guaranteed to incur even more political headaches.

While the current Republican-controlled Congress has not put its political will behind constraining AI companies — either the technology they produce or the resources they consume — Americans of both parties are largely agreed in their opposition to data centers and concern about AI displacing jobs. With the executive branch already willing to create difficulty for Anthropic, the predicted "blue wave" of the midterms may result in the company landing on the wrong side of the legislative branch too.

Anti-data center sentiment has absolutely been a factor in the midterms. As of this August, CNN found both Democratic and Republican candidates had spent more than $35 million on campaign ads opposing data centers, with nearly 8 percent of video ads, up from 1 percent a year earlier mentioning data centers.

NBC News currently ranks Ohio's senate race as a toss up, and the candidates' stance on data centers could be the deciding factor in the election. In a private memo from August, the National Republican Senatorial Committee warned Senator Jon Husted of Ohio could lose his seat entirely due to his track record on data centers. "More than any other thing in this race, data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck," the memo read. "If he loses and datacenters get the blame, politicians across the country will take notice — and they will not go near the next one." In 2025, the Ohio Chamber of Commerce found that the state gave $2.5 billion in tax breaks for the facilities from 2017 to 2024, a period during which Husted was lieutenant governor.

Democratic candidate Sherrod Brown meanwhile has made opposing data centers the center of his platform. "They eat up our land. They swallow up our water ... then once they're built, our electricity prices go up," Brown told supporters at a recent rally. He has labelled Husted "the face of data centers," calling him the "reason" there are more than 200 data centers across the state. Recent polling from The New York Times found voters believed by an 11 percentage-point margin that Brown would do a better job than Husted would managing the development of data centers.

In other Senate, House and gubernatorial races, the divide on data centers between Democrats and Republicans hasn't been so clear cut. New Jersey's respective hopefuls, Adam Hamawy and Watson Coleman, have both advocated to slow the expansion of data centers, of which there are at least 80 in the state. In Pennsylvania, Governor Josh Shapiro and his opponent Stacy Garrity have accused each other of flip-flopping on the issue, but broadly Democrats have advocated for policies that would make it more difficult and expensive for companies to build data centers.

In Texas, both Democratic Senate and gubernatorial hopefuls James Talarico and Gina Hinojosa have vowed to work toward repealing a bill Governor Greg Abbott signed in 2015 giving hyperscale data centers 20 years of tax breaks. Talarico's platform also calls legislation that would require new facilities to use closed-loop cooling systems, and give local communities the authority to reject data center developments in their municipality. Recent polling shows Talarico with a narrow lead on his opponent, despite Elon Musk spending more than $276 million to aid Republican Ken Paxton. If Talarico wins, he would become the first Democrat to represent Texas in the Senate since 1993.

Candidates in other battleground states have called for even more stringent regulation of AI companies. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed's "AI Under Control" proposal calls for a pause on AI model training and the creation of a Cabinet-level government agency that would inspect AI labs. Like his cohorts, El-Sayed has also advocated for restrictions on data center buildouts.

Current-term Democrats are already getting their shot against the highly unpopular AI industry and its constituent data centers lined up. Oregon's Ron Wyden has proposed plans to strip tax breaks from these facilities; Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez, meanwhile, have backed legislation to pause the development of AI models themselves. With the public broadly behind the issue of reining in this industry and a voter mandate in the form of a blue wave, Anthropic might have smartly timed its IPO to buy a few months reprieve before the political environment becomes more hostile and public sentiment turns even more sharply against AI.

Market structure is the architecture of a market: the participants, venues, mechanisms, and rules that support trading. In crypto, this includes centralized exchanges, decentralized exchanges, order books, AMMs, custody models, settlement systems, market makers, institutional execution channels, and blockchain-specific dynamics such as MEV.

A market can have high volume and still have poor execution quality if liquidity is shallow, fragmented, or concentrated. A market can also appear decentralized at the asset layer while depending on centralized venues, custodians, or data providers. Market-structure analysis reveals those hidden dependencies.

Crypto trading happens across many venues. Centralized exchanges custody user assets, run internal matching engines, and update balances on internal ledgers. Decentralized exchanges let users trade through smart contracts while retaining custody. OTC desks help large participants execute bigger trades without showing full order flow on public books.

Each venue model makes different trade-offs. CEXs can offer speed, customer support, and deep order books, but they introduce custody and counterparty risk. DEXs offer transparency and self-custody, but users face smart contract risk, slippage, MEV, and on-chain transaction costs. OTC trading can reduce visible market impact for large orders, but it depends on trusted counterparties and institutional relationships.

Centralized exchanges (CEXs) offer fast matching, familiar user experiences, deep liquidity, and fiat access. Their trade-offs include custody risk, opaque internal ledgers, and regulatory exposure.

Decentralized exchanges (DEXs) offer self-custody, transparency, and smart-contract settlement. Their trade-offs include smart contract risk, slippage, MEV, and on-chain transaction costs.

Over-the-counter (OTC) desks support large-block execution with reduced visible market impact. However, they depend on counterparties and generally offer less public transparency.

Order types encode trader priorities. A market order prioritizes immediate execution. A limit order prioritizes price control. Stop orders, IOC, FOK, GTC, and iceberg orders add more specific execution instructions. These orders interact with order books, matching engines, spreads, depth, and venue risk checks.

After an order enters a venue, it passes the venue's risk controls, joins or crosses the order book, receives priority under the matching rules, and may fill fully, partially, or not at all. The instruction determines how long it can remain active and whether an incomplete fill is acceptable.

Spread is the gap between the best available buy and sell prices. Slippage is the difference between expected and executed price. Both are part of execution cost. They become especially important in fast markets, thin markets, or fragmented markets where liquidity is spread across venues.

Liquidity is the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without significantly moving the price. Price discovery is the process by which markets convert information, order flow, and trading activity into asset prices. In crypto, price discovery can be fragmented because the same asset may trade on multiple exchanges, DEX pools, derivatives venues, and OTC channels.

Market makers and liquidity providers help reduce spreads and support smoother execution by continuously quoting prices or supplying assets to pools. Their participation improves market quality, but it also depends on incentives, risk limits, volatility, and access to reliable infrastructure.

Derivatives are instruments that reshape exposure without requiring ownership of the underlying asset. In crypto, perpetual futures are especially important. They have no expiry date and use funding payments to keep the contract price aligned with the spot market.

Funding rates are recurring payments between long and short traders. When perpetual prices trade above spot, longs usually pay shorts. When perpetual prices trade below spot, shorts may pay longs. Funding rates can signal positioning pressure, but they should not be read as standalone predictions.

Other derivatives include dated futures and options, while structured products combine instruments into packaged risk-and-return profiles. Covered-call vaults exchange some upside for option premium, and delta-neutral vaults try to reduce directional exposure by combining offsetting positions. Their apparent yield still depends on leverage, volatility, collateral, and execution risk.

Perpetual futures: Derivatives with no expiry that track spot prices through funding payments. They are a major source of leverage and positioning data.

Funding rate: A recurring payment between long and short perpetual futures traders. It helps align perpetual and spot prices and can indicate crowded positioning.

Open interest: The total number of open contracts. It provides information about outstanding leverage and market participation.

Liquidation: The forced closing of a leveraged position when margin is insufficient. Liquidations can amplify volatility and create cascading market moves.

Crypto markets include retail traders, institutional investors, hedge funds, asset managers, corporate treasuries, market makers, high-frequency trading firms, custodians, prime brokers, OTC desks, data providers, validators, miners, and protocol teams. Each participant interacts with the market through different constraints and incentives.

Institutions usually need custody, compliance, execution quality, reporting, and risk controls before they can participate at scale. This is why infrastructure matters. Custodians, prime brokers, market data providers, settlement systems, and execution algorithms shape how institutional capital enters and moves through crypto markets.

Market indicators translate activity into signals, but they do not explain markets on their own. Volume, market cap, FDV, open interest, funding rates, RSI, moving averages, spread, depth, and sentiment measures are useful only when interpreted in context.

For example, rising open interest can mean more participation, but it can also mean more leverage. A high funding rate can signal strong long demand, but it can also suggest crowded positioning. The aim is to read these indicators as part of a system rather than as automatic predictions.

Institutional participation changes market structure because large investors need reliable custody, liquidity, reporting, compliance, and execution workflows. Hedge funds, asset managers, banks, brokers, corporate treasuries, and prop shops may all interact with crypto differently.

ETFs, OTC desks, prime brokers, execution algorithms, and custody providers can make digital asset exposure easier for institutions to access. They can also change flows, liquidity concentration, and market behavior. Digital asset treasury companies and related vehicles introduce another layer of market structure by linking corporate balance sheets, token exposure, premiums, discounts, and investor demand.

Execution algorithms such as TWAP and VWAP can divide large orders across time or observed market volume to reduce visible impact. They do not eliminate execution risk, especially when liquidity is fragmented or volatility rises.

Crypto fundraising can happen through equity, tokens, or hybrid structures. ICOs, IEOs, IDOs, STOs, SPVs, syndicates, launch platforms, and DAO fundraising each shape incentives differently. Tokenomics, vesting, unlock schedules, circulating supply, and governance rights can all affect market behavior after launch.

Crypto venture capital also differs from traditional venture capital because tokens can create earlier liquidity, different governance influence, and new incentive conflicts. Capital concentration may help projects launch and scale, but it can also create decentralization and market-pressure trade-offs.

Arbitrage happens when participants act on temporary price differences across venues or instruments. In fragmented crypto markets, spatial, triangular, and statistical arbitrage can help prices converge. This can improve efficiency, but it can also favor participants with better infrastructure, faster routing, or more capital.

MEV, or maximal extractable value, is value captured through transaction inclusion, exclusion, or ordering. It can appear through sandwich attacks, backrunning, private order flow, and flash-loan-enabled strategies. MEV is a market-structure issue because it connects trading, execution, blockchain ordering, user experience, and fairness.

In August, a stray upper stage from a SpaceX Falcon 9 struck the moon. Roughly the size of a school bus and weighing 4 metric tons, it hit the lunar surface near Einstein Crater at approximately 5,400 miles per hour, carving out a substantial new crater. Had a similarly massive piece of debris survived reentry and struck a populated area on Earth, it would have caused significant damage and loss of life. And, just last week, large pieces of space debris fell on land in both Minnesota and Indonesia, presumably from a Chinese rocket launched on 14 October.

These events highlight an increasingly visible problem of space exploration: Satellite mega-constellations aren’t only transforming our night skies. Humanity’s expansion into space is beginning to reproduce the same take-make-waste model that has caused significant environmental damage on Earth.

The number of objects in Earth’s orbit has risen dramatically and is likely to increase several-fold again over the coming decade. In 2010, the active satellite population was roughly 1,000. Today, space surveillance networks track approximately 40,000 objects in the Earth’s orbit. But only around 17,000 of these are still active payloads; the rest are retired satellites, spent rocket parts, and other fragments.

When it comes to smaller space debris, the European Space Agency (ESA) estimates that more than 1.2 million debris fragments larger than 1 centimeter are already orbiting Earth. The greatest danger posed by these smaller fragments is that they will collide with functioning spacecraft. Traveling at several kilometers per second, even a small fragment can disable a satellite, while a larger impact can shatter it into thousands of additional pieces.

Those fragments can then strike other objects, producing a self-sustaining cascade of collisions known as the Kessler syndrome. The ESA’s latest Space Environment Report, published in September, identifies the potential development of the Kessler syndrome as the most serious long-term threat to space exploration.

The consequences of such an event would extend far beyond space, with profound impacts on global infrastructure on Earth. Satellites supporting communications, navigation of shipping and aviation, banking, weather forecasting, climate monitoring, disaster response, and even military early-warning systems could be severely damaged or even completely lost. This would create gaps in essential services and heighten the risk of geopolitical miscalculation, as disrupted communications and surveillance systems could be mistaken for hostile acts by other governments, instead of technical failures.

Some projections cited in United Nations space-sustainability discussions suggest that more than 100,000 satellites could be orbiting Earth by 2030, if proposed mega-constellations proceed as planned. As orbital traffic grows, so does the probability that a single collision could initiate further fragmentation and make valuable orbital regions increasingly hazardous or even unusable.

Rather than rely on maneuvers to avoid a collision (overseen by surveillance networks such as the U.S. Space Surveillance Network), more systemic solutions are needed. We should not reproduce the same wastefulness that we exhibited on Earth—a wastefulness that brought about the plastics pollution crisis. Instead, we should build a circular space economy, and fortunately, we can use solutions from Earth to do so.

Instead of treating rockets and satellites as disposable objects that are launched, used, and then abandoned or burned up, a circular space economy would entail spacecraft designed for durability, repair, upgrading, refueling, and eventual recovery.

This is no longer merely a hypothetical idea; it is becoming a major frontier of space technology and commercial innovation. The ESA has set out a vision for a zero-debris approach and a circular space economy by 2050. This approach will keep spacecraft, components, and materials in use through repair, refurbishment, upgrading, reuse, and eventually recycling in orbit. ESA-backed research is also exploring orbital recycling plants and spacecraft designed from the outset for disassembly and material recovery.

Circularity could therefore become not only an environmental safeguard but also a foundation for the next phase of space exploration. There are already commercial operators such as ClearSpace and Astroscale that are trying to develop spacecraft capable of approaching, inspecting, and capturing defunct satellites, while robotic arms and standardized docking interfaces could allow satellites to be repaired, refueled, or safely removed.

There is also an important connection to critical minerals: A circular space economy could turn orbital debris from a costly liability into a valuable economic resource. Satellites and rocket components contain aluminum and titanium, alongside smaller quantities of cobalt, nickel, and rare-earth elements used in batteries, magnets, electronics, and communications equipment.

Rather than allowing these materials to remain stranded in orbit or burn up during reentry, future recovery systems could capture, dismantle, and process defunct spacecraft. Some estimates show that the reuse of space junk could have a net value of between $570 billion and $1.2 trillion. This amounts to somewhere between 5,312 metric tons and 19,124 metric tons of scrap metal that can be recovered, not considering future materials yet to be launched. Recovering and reusing these materials in orbit could reduce the financial and environmental costs of launching replacement materials from Earth.

Elon Musk’s SpaceX is no stranger to circular solutions in space. In fact, it was reuse that (quite literally) lifted the company to the forefront of commercial spaceflight. By landing and reflying the Falcon 9’s first-stage booster, SpaceX transformed one of a rocket’s most expensive components from single-use hardware into a repeatedly used asset. It is also less expensive to refly than to build new launchers. A NASA assessment estimated an average cost of around $50 million for a flight using a previously flown booster, compared with roughly $65 million for one using a new booster. Circularity, in other words, is not a constraint on the business model; it is a major source of its competitive advantage.

Commercial success has helped turn reusable boosters into an industry standard that China and other launch providers are now pursuing. On July 10, China recovered an orbital-class booster for the first time, catching the returning first stage of a Long March 10B rocket in a net above an offshore platform after it had placed a satellite in orbit.

But the solutions are not only technical. Addressing the problem will also require some innovation of the space governance architecture and the resolution of several important legal questions. The core international rules governing space use were written in the 1960s and 1970s, when space activity was the purview of governments and relatively few objects were in orbit. Currently, defunct satellites remain under the jurisdiction and ownership of their registering states, meaning that companies cannot simply capture and repurpose them as abandoned material. Also, existing guidelines provide no standardized procedures for obtaining consent, transferring ownership, or allocating liability if a recovery operation causes damage.

The United States is racing ahead where Europe, China, and India struggle.

Negotiating a new treaty to manage space seems hopeless, but a workable mechanism exists.

Encouragingly, making these zero-waste and circular practices routine would not require constructing an entirely new system of space governance. The foundations already exist in the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, the Space Liability Convention, the Registration Convention, and broader U.N. sustainability guidelines. Several international and intergovernmental bodies already exist as well and have made orbital debris and space sustainability a priority.

The space governance architecture must be further updated and operationalized for an era increasingly dominated by commercial operators such as SpaceX, Amazon Leo, or Geespace (a private Chinese commercial operator backed by Geely) and their satellite mega-constellations. To support sustainability in orbital space, measures to prevent or minimize the creation of debris were introduced at the international level by the U.N. Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) in 2007 in the form of the Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines.

Furthermore, the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC)—an international forum of space agencies, authorized governmental and intergovernmental entities which coordinates efforts to deal with space junk—first launched its Debris Mitigation Guidelines in 2002, with subsequent updates, the latest one in 2025. An important update has been a new constellation-wide perspective. The revised guidelines recognize that risks depend not only on whether each satellite is eventually removed but also on where thousands of satellites are placed and how they travel through other orbital shells.

These guidelines have not been entirely unsuccessful: They have helped establish widely accepted practices such as passivating spent rocket stages, limiting routine debris release, and disposing of satellites after use. Compliance is also improving among commercial operators.

But the guidelines were never strong enough to reverse the overall growth in risk. There’s still a mismatch between being useful mitigation guidance drafted 20 years ago and being fit for purpose in the age of mega-constellations. And the guidelines are still voluntary and too weakly enforceable to manage the full systemic risks.

Under the Outer Space Treaty, states remain responsible for supervising private space activities, while the 1972 Liability Convention channels responsibility for damage through launching states, rather than directly to commercial operators. Back then, space exploration was solely the domain of specialized government agencies and the military, and the involvement of private companies and commercial operators as we see today was not anticipated.

Modernizing the liability framework could introduce binding end-of-life obligations, standardized consent procedures for servicing, and clearer liability rules for the growing number of commercial actors. Ideally, circularity would be a condition of receiving a launch license.

Even deposit-based systems such as “space junk bonds” have been proposed by insurers as a more market-based approach. Such as system would require operators to help cover future recovery and cleanup costs. This approach would be modeled on surety bonds, which have been used to ensure the decommissioning of oil rigs and mines. Before launch, satellite operators would pay a bond proportionate to the number, mass, orbital lifetime, and collision risk of their spacecraft. The bond would be refunded once safe de-orbiting and recovery were completed and verified.

The upcoming 60th anniversary of the Outer Space Treaty in 2027 offers a timely opportunity to address these governance gaps. The treaty itself need not be renegotiated; instead, states could develop and adopt a “Protocol on Circular Space Operations”— a supplementary instrument developed through COPUOS that would translate existing treaty principles into practical guidance and implementation.

This protocol could clarify procedures for transferring ownership of defunct satellites, obtaining consent for their capture, allocating liability during removal missions, and recognizing recovered materials as economic resources.

Space debris and the risk of catastrophic collisions are not unavoidable consequences of human space exploration. The ideas already exist, the technologies are emerging, the institutional foundations are in place, and the economic opportunities could be substantial.

Albert Einstein once argued that “a new type of thinking is essential if mankind is to survive and move toward higher levels.” If space exploration is to take humanity to those higher levels and further into outer space, we must move beyond disposable spacecraft and toward circular design that enables repair, reuse, and recovery.
