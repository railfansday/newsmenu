#4284. Even at their most chaotic and conflicted, BioWare were the heart of the western computer RPG

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Life... is strength. The gears of this world are tuned for a quick death. Doomity-doom-doom! We is all doooooo-oooomed! The Guardian says for me to be cheerful, and to not repeat what he says all the... oh. I'm Commander Shepard, and this is my favourite store on the Citadel. You simians may merely refer to me as 'Sir,' if you prefer a less... syllable-intensive workout. I have seen the throne of the Gods, and it was empty. Stand in the ashes of a trillion dead souls and ask the ghosts if honour matters. There are people like you everywhere. It's just that not everyone has the words for it yet. You exist because we allow it, and you will end because we demand it. Let's call that finished. You must gather your party before venturing forth.

I'm not even all that much of a BioWare enthusiast. I'm not sure I'm in mourning, exactly. Partly that's because videogame studios are big corporate abstractions, unworthy of our silly human emotions; the story here is that a bunch of people have lost their livelihoods, after their employers triumphantly thrust themselves into unbelievable debt. And partly it's because, as with any studio of over 30 years standing, BioWare was a much-caulked and jury-rigged ship of Theseus – paddling around in the doldrums of 2026, I'm not entirely sure what I should be mourning, any more.

Reportedly, there were around 50 staff left at BioWare, mostly labouring away on Mass Effect 5, when EA closed them this week. A lot of those who worked on the studio's formative games, including the very first Baldur's Gate, are long departed. The past decade especially has seen a steady exodus of BioWare talent, alongside a barrage of "veteran" start-ups and spiritual successor projects, many of which have sadly now faded and folded.

Within that arc, my own appreciation for BioWare has waxed and waned. I honestly can't remember enough of Baldur's Gate 2 to have strong opinions, though I overall acknowledge its brilliance. I probably felt closest to the studio during the heyday of the original Mass Effect trilogy. The original's Vigil scene still gives me shivers, and who could forget the prodigious act of fan service demolition that was Mass Effect 2's opening, to say nothing of its legendary "Suicide Mission" (I left Mordin behind).

I will always carry a candle for Dragon Age: Inquisition – a generous and unwieldy return to save-the-world-dom after Dragon Age 2's leaner city story, which I cherish for its beautiful Tarot art, its bustling hub castle, and its spread of rambunctious and difficult companion characters. I greatly enjoy Ferelden as a setting, all the different yarns the various races spin about the world's becoming. Still, I bounced right off Dragon Age: The Veilguard's on-the-nose, corridor-like design, despite Nic's guarded exhortations.

Anthem? I was keener on that than a lot of you, but I remember it mostly as a conflicted yet promising aviator with nowhere to go – so much BioWare flourish, floundering through a swamp of live service. I thought Mass Effect: Andromeda was a wide, shallow bowl of lukewarm space-gruel lent relish only by its launch bugs. There are other BioWare games I simply haven't played – Neverwinter Nights (well, apart from the five minutes I managed, before it became clear my computer at the time really couldn't run it) and its celebrated modding toolkit, the Orientalised fable Jade Empire and, whisper it, almighty KOTOR.

As for my experience of BioWare as a games journalist, it's pretty brief and polarised. On the one hand, spending three hours with a preview build of Inquisition in a London warehouse, alongside such guiding lights as former PCGamer scribe Chris Thursten, is probably as close to heaven as I'll get before my inevitable deathbed recantation. On the other, there was that humiliating interview with co-founder Dr Ray Muzyka, when I was terrified our mic wouldn't record him properly. I resorted to kneeling between Muzyka and the actual interviewer, jerking the dictaphone back and forth in my desperation to guzzle up every last drop of Muzyka wisdom. Not even Khalisah al-Jilani was this cringe. I haven't been allowed near a BioWare executive since.

The thing is, you don't have to have enjoyed, or even played many BioWare games to understand the difference they've made, and why the newly private EA's closure of the studio is such a kick in the guts, however presaged by years of interference, mismanagement, and neglect. As regards North American and European sci-fantasy incarnations, at least, BioWare have always been the RPG's sleeping sentinel. They wrote the genre around themselves, producing and revising a vocabulary over decades that so many other designers, writers and modders are in conversation with – whether courting comparison, or critiquing and even despising.

BioWare are why D&D RPGs took off. They're the creators of by far the most influential adaptation of the tabletop game's forbidding ruleset. They are the studio you probably think of, when you think of party-based stories with branching plots. They are the blockbuster joint you think of, rivalled only by Final Fantasy and more recently Larian, when you think of companions who deserve the starring role.

Even their stumbles have thrown open ground for other writers, designers and artists to investigate: that main-and-supporting-band antagonism between critical path and sidequest, for example - between yet another chronicle of destined heroes averting some great evil, and the huggable humour of playing go-between for a knight who's too shy to hit on somebody. How to reconcile the grandiosity with the closeness is the trad fantasy RPG's founding dilemma. They popularised in-game romances and, yes, sex scenes that generally feel like the convincing result of hours of careful narrative distillation, and occasionally like a helping of cheesecake unlocked by pawing away at a hidden skilltree.

They are the studio who got us tallying up colour-coded Good and Bad points to shape both protagonist and endgame - so tacky, but undeniably striking as a vehicle for stark shifts and flavourful writing, as demonstrated in reverse by the omnivorous wishy-washiness of Mass Effect: Andromeda. Their morality systems are a place from which to start thinking about other, less videogamey, more organic and conflicting entanglements of Morality and System. They introduced a particular species of videogame celebrity with Commander Shepard, and they presided over a very particular stage in the evolving friction between creators and "audience" with Mass Effect 3's much-revised ending. While I'd made up my mind to stop covering EA games, following the Saudi-led acquisition, I was curious, if not optimistic, to see what they might have done with Mass Effect 5.

Above all, BioWare were the wizards of words. They had less-good scripts but I don't think they ever penned a bad one. Looking back over their games is dizzying and deafening, like opening a door in a dark, crumbling house to reveal a crowd of revellers - demons and courtesans and krogans and fops and berserkers, painted glorious shades by secret firelight and dancing in a ring.

So many words! How many words? Tens of millions, surely. Words that conjure words, raising them from the dust: Go for the optics, Chatika! Go for the eyes, Boo! Words seemingly drawn from some personal well of pain and tenderness, easily reduced to authorial self-commentary, that stick in my mind where so much else about the ageing fantasy has lost its charm. Here is Aveline, the aforesaid timid knight, to play us out. "That big man made every step of the story my choice. I loved that. He died of the Wasting in a Denerim ward... those last weeks, I read to him. I had to take his hand to turn the pages, and I couldn't tell if he was too weak, or if it was the old game. He smiled at that, his big girl… Don't let anyone tell you how to mourn. And when someone says 'move on', you take their hand and say, 'my choice.'"

#3748. 10 Shows Like 'A Different World' You Should Watch Next

The original A Different World was a groundbreaking sitcom in its era, spending six seasons highlighting Black academic drive among a cast of characters so indelible that we're now checking in on them decades later. Spearheaded by Queen Sugar's Felicia Pride, the update has been a critical and ratings hit for Netflix, earning the rarest prize of all in streaming television: a quick second season renewal. While you're waiting for season two, here are some other smart coming-of-age shows that blend heart and humor with realistic drama.

An easy pairing with A Different World, this show follows Black-ish's Zoey Johnson (Yara Shahidi) as she leaves home to attend a (fictional) California liberal arts college (the last couple of post-graduation seasons shift the focus to her brother Andre, played by Marcus Scribner). The emphasis is on driven young people who are capable of making big mistakes on the path to adulthood. A true comedy-drama, Grown-ish feels real(-ish) in a way that few other college shows have managed. Stream Grown-ish on Hulu.

Adapted from and expanding upon the 2014 movie, this show takes us to a (fictional) Ivy League school for a comedy-drama that tackles campus life and politics from a rather different perspective than that of Rooster. Logan Browning leads the ensemble cast as Sam White, who kind of inadvertently starts the titular radio show following a racially charged incident on campus. Far from the racial screed that some bad-faith YouTubers seem to find it, it's never shy about confronting the complicated and occasionally silly contradictions of campus activism, with each episode approaching life at Winchester University from a different character's perspective. Stream Dear White People on Netflix.

Kimberly (Pauline Chalamet) is an endlessly naïve scholarship student; Bela (Amrit Kaur) is an aspiring comedy writer on the make for the hottest guys; Whitney (Alyah Chanelle Scott) is an overachieving athlete and senator’s daughter; and Leighton (Reneé Rapp) is a closeted sorority girl. They're all randomly assigned to room together as freshmen at the fictional Essex College in Vermont. Created by Mindy Kaling and Justin Noble, this comedy-drama isn't nearly as salacious as its title suggests. There's sex for sure, but mostly it's a funny, queer-friendly show that's a little less about one overarching romance than it is about growing up, and the value of female friendship. Stream The Sex Lives of College Girls on HBO Max.

Even though we're backing up a couple of years to follow teens in their high school years, this British import's setting is a decent match, finding its students living away from home and family for the first time in their lives. At the fictional prestigious boarding school St. Gilbert’s, five Black teens are newly attending on scholarships, but their integration into the existing cliques is less than smooth—especially given that they're there largely as a PR stunt. Boarders blends teen romance, coming-of-age drama, and a delightful willingness to take the piss when it comes to the whole "rich private school" thing. Stream Boarders on Tubi.

Another modern take on a beloved '80s/'90s sitcom, this one's a bit different, serving as a total reimagining with no narrative connection to the original. And while A Different World is a comedy with dramatic heft, this one's more of a straight drama. It’s jarring for fans of the beloved original to tune in to find a serious show with the same premise as the Will Smith original—one that often leans into heavy realism. Jabari Banks plays, er, Will Smith, a 16-year-old from West Philadelphia who moves in with his auntie and uncle in Bel-Air following a gun charge and a run-in with a Philly drug lord. Banks is charismatic and believable, bringing a ton of personality to a show that occasionally veers down dark alleys. Stream Bel-Air on Peacock.

Comedian Benito Skinner plays himself, kinda, in this buzzy comedy that sees a former high school jock facing his freshman year in college while desperately trying to convince himself and everyone else that he's as straight as they come (relatable, except for the jock part). Much of the appeal is in its deft blending of tones: It's both a frequently raunchy college comedy and a sweet coming-of-age story about accepting yourself without worrying about what everyone else thinks. The cast includes Adam DiMarco (The White Lotus) and Rish Shah (Ms. Marvel), and, though it's more a story of one young man's coming of age, there's a strong ensemble backing him up. Stream Overcompensating on Prime Video.

The heart of All American (for most of its run) is Spencer James (Daniel Ezra), based on real-life NFL linebacker Spencer Paysinger. A star player for his high school team in Crenshaw, he's recruited by the coach (Taye Diggs) of a team in Beverly Hills to come and play for them, forcing Spencer to navigate two very different worlds while trying to build a life and potential career. Stream All American on Netflix.

Following a family tragedy, the already awkward 15-year-old Devi Vishwakumar (Maitreyi Ramakrishnan) has a pretty terrible freshman year of high school, and decides that she’s going to turn things around the following year, hoping that a nearly academic study of sex will get her the boyfriend(s) her raging hormones have begun to demand. It works, to a point—but getting a surprise yes to sex from her crush leads her to question what she’s really ready for. More about burgeoning sexuality than sex specifically, this Mindy Kaling-produced series is both honest and charming. Stream Never Have I Ever on Netflix.

For realism in a show about the college experience, you could do a lot worse than this docuseries following several students at Gallaudet University, a school for deaf and hard-of-hearing young adults. In one sense, it's a specific setting where perspectives on deafness vary wildly, even in Gallaudet's self-contained world (aside from being a show about academically driven and horny college students, this is also a great primer for hearing people about some of the issues). But even for all of that, the challenges in building a life and future away from home are pretty universally relatable. Stream Deaf U on Netflix.

There's a reason A Different World has returned in 2026, and a lot of that has to do with the fact that there was nothing else on TV quite like it back in the late '80s and early '90s. For all of its sitcom sensibilities, the show tried hard (especially after multi-hyphenate creator Debbie Allen arrived to rework things after its first season) to paint a realistic picture of college life and an HBCU. Of the few shows on TV with largely Black casts, almost none would go so far as to highlight achievement and excellence; A Different World managed to do so while building a cast of characters so memorable that we're more than willing to catch up with them nearly forty years later. Stream A Different World on Netflix.

#4647. Ford Mustang Mach-E Gains Soft-Roading ‘Trail’ Trim for 2027

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The Mach-E Trail may not be as extreme as the Rally, but it gives the Mustang crossover more of what it needs to reach outdoorsy buyers.

The Ford Mustang Mach-E may have struggled a little to stand out in amongst electric SUVs when it launched, but it finally found a spectacular niche with the introduction of the gravel-ready Mustang Mach-E Rally. That unique performance model is a thrill for those of us who miss the Mitsubishi Lancer Evo line, but it’s also a lot more performance than the average car camper needs. A new variant, the 2027 Ford Mustang Mach-E Trail, aims to address just that.

Where the Rally is built for going fast on loose surfaces, the Trail is built for the more popular but slightly less thrilling task of getting from home to campsite. Offered as a $2295 option for Select eAWD Mach-Es, the Trail package gets the steering rack, 19-inch wheels, and Michelin CrossClimate 2 all-weather tires. it also adds a few distinct design elements, including graphics and black trim along the bottom of the front fascia.

Ride height is also increased—8/10ths of an inch higher up front and a full inch higher at the rear than the lowered GT model. (The front lift is the same as the Rally, while the rear lift is slightly less.) The Trail also gets standard 400-watt, 120-volt on-board power, a major selling point for adventure-ready EVs that allows campers to run their accessories off the car’s battery.

The Trail package may not turn the Mach-E into a Subaru Outback Wilderness overnight, but these little touches are relatively affordable and should instantly vault the Ford into contention for buyers looking at electric soft-roaders. A Mach-E with the Trail package starts at $47,335 after destination and delivery fees.

In addition, Ford added a few other updates to the lineup for the 2027 model year, such as a more affordable front motor on AWD models and a couple new colors. The biggest (and very welcome) change, however, is the axing of the smaller battery pack from the lineup; all 2027 Mach-Es now use the extended range battery, which has 88 kWh of usable capacity on mainstream models and 91 kWh on Mustang Mach-E GT and Rally. And the Select RWD version with that battery starts at $39,995 before destination and delivery, the costs of which were not specified in the announcement.

#5607. Volkswagen is launching its most affordable EV yet in 2027, starting at around €20,000

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Volkswagen’s upcoming electric car will be smaller and lower-priced than the ID. Polo, starting at around €20,000 ($22,500).

The new Electric Urban Car Family is the Volkswagen Group’s answer to Chinese automakers like BYD: a new lineup of compact, entry-level electric cars.

The lineup currently includes the Volkswagen ID. Polo, ID. Cross, plus the Cupra Raval and Skoda Epiq, all based on the same MEB+ platform.

After launching the lower-priced base versions of the ID. Polo and ID. Cross, starting at €24,995 and €27,995, respectively, Volkswagen is preparing to introduce an even smaller, more affordable EV in 2027.

Volkswagen revealed the ID. EVERY1 concept car last March, a preview of its upcoming entry-level EV that will arrive in 2027 starting at around €20,000.

The ID. EVERY1, which is expected to go on sale as the ID. Up! next year, is “the last piece of the puzzle,” VW brand CEO Thomas Schäfer said, as it aims to offer the widest selection in the volume segment.

With a length of 3,880 mm, the entry-level EV is positioned between the former Up! (3,600 mm) and ID. Polo (4,053 mm). Although that is tiny, VW said it will offer space for four passengers and a luggage compartment volume of 305 liters.

Volkswagen has yet to reveal details, but the €20,000 EV is expected to use the same 37 kWh LFP battery as the base ID. Polo and ID. Cross to keep costs down.

The ID. Polo with the 37 kWh battery has a WLTP range of up to 334 km (207 miles), so the smaller model will likely offer around the same or slightly more range.

Volkswagen said it will offer at least 250 km (155 miles) of range. It will also use a new 70 kW (94 hp) electric motor, enabling a top speed of 130 km/h (80 mph).

The production version of the ID. EVERY1 will be one of nine new models VW plans to launch by 2027.

Volkswagen delivered on its promise to launch the ID. Polo with the 37 kWh battery for under €25,000. Will it do the same with the new entry-level model at around €20,000? We will find out shortly.

It will be produced in Portugal, this has been confirmed ages ago.

According to Spanish auto magazine Autofácil, Volkswagen could manufacture the €20,000 EV in Spain, although Volkswagen has yet to confirm a production site.

The Group currently produces the ID. Polo and Cupra Raval at its Martorell plant, while the ID. Cross and Skoda Epiq are manufactured at the Navarra plant, both in Spain.

Volkswagen’s new Electric Urban Car Family is off to a promising start, securing nearly 150,000 orders after series production began in June.

While those in the US will miss out on the new entry-level EV, Volkswagen is launching the ID. Tiguan in 2027 as a replacement for the ID.4.

#4814. Inside Dodger Stadium’s Fascinating History—Including 5 Facts You Probably Didn’t Know

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Brooklyn Dodgers once played in a small ballpark in the borough’s Flatbush neighborhood. So how did the team end up in the Dodger Stadium of today—a midcentury-modern structure with sweeping views of Los Angeles?

“The city of Los Angeles was the ultimate Plan B for the Brooklyn Dodgers,” Mark Langill, the team’s historian, says in a new installment of AD’s Inside History video series. However, when team president Walter O’Malley saw the freeway access leading to the canyon of Chavez Ravine, just north of downtown LA, the sports exec thought it’d be “the perfect place” for a stadium that would be in a league of its own.

Dodger Stadium, which opened in 1962 and seats a whopping 56,000 fans, has now hosted games in 12 World Series, including in 2024 and 2025 when the Los Angeles Dodgers won the championship. Though the team hoped for a historic three-peat this year—a third consecutive World Series win would have marked the first time a team achieved this since the New York Yankees came out victorious in 2000—they were eliminated by the Milwaukee Brewers in the playoffs.

Ahead of this year’s World Series, AD visits the third-oldest Major League Baseball stadium—behind Fenway Park (home to the Boston Red Sox) and Wrigley Field (home of the Chicago Cubs)—to learn more about its history. Below are a few highlights.

Dodger Stadium is known for its colorful seating, and architect Emil Praeger had a specific reason for arranging the seats in a gradient from yellow at field level to orange, turquoise, and sky blue at the top. “The choice of the colors was selected to mimic the sunset,” says Janet Marie Smith, executive vice president of planning and development for the LA Dodgers.

Every entrance of Dodger Stadium “is marked by something different,” says Smith, adding that landmarks like oversized Dodgers bobbleheads can be especially helpful when getting back to your car after a game. And of course, the stadium wouldn’t be complete without paying tribute to Jackie Robinson, who broke Major League Baseball’s color line in 1947. Alongside a bronze statue of the baseball great are some of his profound quotes chosen in consultation with his widow, Rachel Robinson, and his daughter, Sharon. They are displayed out front because his “power and impact on America wasn’t just on the playing field,” says Smith.

Players heading straight to Dodger Stadium from LAX may think they ended up exactly where they started. A hallway that serves as the entrance to the clubhouse is inspired by the tile mosaics at the airport that have appeared in movies like 1967’s Point Blank and 1997’s Jackie Brown. “We wanted to give honor to Janet Bennett, who designed the mosaics at LAX,” says Smith.

Because Dodger Stadium was in need of advertisement space, hexagonal signs bordering the field were created to “mimic the scoreboard,” says Smith. The six-sided polygon is found in various places at the venue—though some didn’t take shape until 2013, when additions to the venue were crafted from old sketches laid out in 1959. “We’re really pleased to have finally finished things that were authored half a century ago,” says Smith.

The outside of Dodger Stadium is just as important as the inside. In 1963, O’Malley invested in landscaping that would grow into lush botanical gardens just outside of its gates. “We’re so proud of having the only botanist on staff of any Major League team,” says Smith, noting that the green grounds have become one of the stadium’s “defining” features. Outside are more than 100 concrete planters shaped like oversized martini glasses, or champagne bowls, that the Dodger landscaping crew “lovingly restored” over the years.

#4750. Scam alert: The government is not deleting your phone number

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 4:15 PM EDT

The scams are getting a bit meta. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has issued a warning about a new scam currently making the rounds. But this time, the con artists aren’t impersonating a politician or celebrity to trick consumers.They’re claiming that they are the ones sounding the alarm at the FTC.

Real FTC officials explained the general scheme in an alert released on October 22. Scammers call a target to tell them that bad actors in another country have registered a separate phone using their name and number for nefarious purposes. The imposter may even “transfer” you to a fake police officer to file a report. Fake badge numbers and IDs are also offered to make it all sound legitimate, and skeptics may even be directed to verify everything by searching for the police department’s phone number online (also fake). Failure to comply allegedly results in the loss of your personal phone number.

Those who follow through with the instructions provide scammers with their state-issued ID and other personal info for the bogus police report, but it’s not over yet. Some time later, targets will receive another call informing them that they will soon be arrested for those nonexistent crimes committed using their phone number. The solution? Transfer money for a security deposit.

“None of this is true—it’s all a scam,” assured the FTC.

To prevent unnecessary headaches, the FTC listed a few ironclad rules. Actual FTC employees will never contact you about sending money, or transfer you to someone else requesting funds. The FTC will never threaten to disconnect your phone number, or ask you to keep your conversations secret from others. Finally, they will never text a photo of the supposed employee ID as a way to verify their identity to you.

Unfortunately, combatting scammers is a bit like playing a neverending game of Whack-a-Mole. While the FTC has a list of common ways to spot government imposters, they encourage everyone to report the latest con jobs to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

TENERIFE, CANARY ISLANDS — NASA's Artemis II astronauts were just minutes away from a critical engine burn that would send them toward the moon when an unexpected cabin leak warning threatened to change the course of their historic mission.

"If it had happened five minutes later, we may not have been able to have that burn," Artemis II pilot Victor Glover revealed during an Oct. 18 discussion with Canadian former astronaut Chris Hadfield at the Starmus VIII festival here in Tenerife.

The incident unfolded on April 2, the second day of the Artemis II mission, as the astronauts' Orion spacecraft swooped back toward Earth after traveling some 38,000 miles (61,000 kilometers) away. Orion had been completing a highly elliptical orbit around Earth, giving the crew and mission control an opportunity to test the capsule's systems before committing to the journey to the moon. Orion was now approaching its closest point to our planet, roughly 100 miles (160 km) above the surface, ahead of the crucial translunar injection (TLI) burn that would send the astronauts moonward.

"Jeremy looks out the window and says, 'I think we're going to run smack dab into the middle of Earth,'" Glover recalled, referring to Artemis II mission specialist Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. "Because we were getting so low."

The close approach brought additional challenges, as Orion passed through a region populated by orbital debris and satellites.

"The density of things is going up from where we are, and also an increase in radiation," Glover explained.

It was at this particularly demanding point in the mission that Orion issued a warning that indicated a possible cabin leak, around 15 minutes before the critical TLI burn.

"So, determining whether it was real or not was a big deal," Glover said.

The crew and mission control faced a crucial question: Was it safe to continue heading to the moon, or would they have to abandon the planned maneuver and cut the mission very short? "Are we going to do a TLI and burn to go to the moon, or are we putting on our spacesuits to prepare to come back to Earth right now?" Glover recalled.

The timing was particularly critical. The TLI burn was designed to propel Orion out of Earth orbit and onto its trajectory around the moon, marking the first time astronauts had headed toward Earth's nearest neighbor since the Apollo era.

Luckily, as NASA flight director Judd Frieling told us during an April 2 media briefing, the warning turned out to be a false alarm.

So Artemis II received the go-ahead. Orion fired its main engine for approximately five minutes and 50 seconds, successfully setting the four astronauts on their journey around the moon.

"We're watching the timer count down, velocities go up, and the magenta and green numbers coming together," Glover recalled.

"Everything is tracking. It's looking good."

Before long, the crew had broken free of Earth's gravitational hold and were hurtling toward the moon at around 24,500 mph (39,430 kph).

Despite the tense moments before the burn, Artemis II went on to make history. NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman (the mission's commander), Glover (pilot) and Christina Koch (mission specialist), along with Hansen, traveled farther from Earth than any humans before them, reaching a maximum distance of 252,756 miles (406,771 km) during their journey around the moon.

The four astronauts returned safely to Earth on April 10, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of San Diego after nearly 10 days in space.

The Texas-based carmaker has until October 30 to respond to NHTSA about its certification methods for the robotaxi.

Tesla has been very upfront about autonomy being the future of the Texas-based electric-vehicle startup, apart from the occasional dalliance into human-driven sports cars. And that business model shift is apparently about to begin, as Tesla executives said deliveries of the Cybercab robotaxi will start by the end of 2026, according to Reuters—even as the federal government is currently examining the vehicle and its software.

"As Tesla grows, we'll mainly deliver fully designed autonomous vehicles to our customers and Cybercabs will be the first," ⁠Tesla vice president of vehicle engineering Lars Moravy said while reporting third-quarter results, Reuters reports. “We're trying to get Cybercabs to customers by the end of this year—2026. As we start small this year, and we learn ... about delivery and ownership and fleet management of autonomous vehicles in customer hands, we'll continue to scale that."

A small fleet of autonomous Cybercabs began operating in Austin, Texas, earlier this year. Since then, the service has since expanded to two additional Texas cities (Dallas and Houston), three Florida cities (Tampa, Orlando, and Miami), and the San Francisco Bay Area in California.

But the rollout of Tesla’s autonomous rideshare service has not been without issues. A National Highway Traffic Safety Administration inquiry was opened this year after videos showed the initial run of Cybercabs reportedly speeding and driving into oncoming traffic lanes in Austin, Texas. Earlier this month, Tesla faced another inquiry from federal regulators after reports of the autonomous vehicles speeding in school zones. A separate NHTSA investigation was opened into Tesla autonomous operating methodology in September 2026, questioning whether a vehicle without a steering wheel and pedals meets federal motor vehicle standards.

Tesla executives seemingly hope to clear up these setbacks briskly. As Tesla scales its autonomous network, Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla's head of AI, said that the company plans to deploy Cybercabs as fast as they can produce them. The Cybercab is one of the easiest products to scale in terms of manufacturing, Elluswamy explained. “I think the key thing which we are trying to make sure is that we have all the regulatory approvals where we can start deploying the Cybercabs as ⁠fast as we can produce them," he said.

Federal regulators are waiting for a response from Tesla about its certification methodology for the Cybercab, and the carmaker has until October 30 to respond. In terms of the certification process for autonomous vehicles, NHTSA officials recently proposed changes, but the existing standards remain in place—and could stand in Tesla’s way for the time being.

#3833. Fleetwood Mac Announce Fleetwood Mac Documentary

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The story of Fleetwood Mac is classic rock canon at this point, memorialized via an assortment of unauthorized films, loosely inspired TV shows and plays, and Lindsey Buckingham’s recent turn as a reaction influencer for some reason. Now the band themselves are ready to “officially” tell their story, in Fleetwood Mac: The Chain, a Frank Marshall-directed documentary. It’s set to premiere on Apple TV on January 29.

Unsurprisingly, The Chain will keep its focus on the most popular era of the Mac: their 1974-2018 lineup of Stevie Nicks, Buckingham, Mick Fleetwood, John McVie, and the late Christine McVie. (Sorry to Peter Green heads). The Chain, which promises “never-before-seen footage and exclusive new interviews,” bills itself as the first fully authorized documentary from the band, though that’s a bit of a technicality: they all participated in the BBC’s one-hour 2009 doc Fleetwood Mac: Don’t Stop.
