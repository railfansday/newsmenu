The Bayern Munich striker - who returned to his club this morning - became the first Englishman to win football's ultimate individual honour in 25 years, since Michael Owen claimed the prize in 2001.

All the more special was that the 33-year-old - who bagged a staggering 73 goals during the 2025-26 season - was able to do so in front of a home crowd, with the France Football event relocating from Paris to London for the first time since its founding 70 years ago.

But despite Kane's mammoth contributions to football making him a worthy winner, those who might have backed his closest rivals Yamal and Mbappe were quick to seek alternative explanations as to why their favourites had lost out.

Real Madrid legend Guti suggested that the change of venue had played a large part in the timing of Kane's honour.

'It just so happens that, for the 70th anniversary, they move the ceremony to London and someone from London wins it,' the former Los Blancos claimed on Spanish panel show El Chiringuito. 'It's an incredible coincidence that they move it to London and someone from London wins it, isn't it?

#52. US government says hackers are now exploiting critical PHP web bugs

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

U.S. cybersecurity agency CISA has confirmed that hackers are now actively exploiting a pair of critical security vulnerabilities in the widely used PHP web software and has ordered civilian federal agencies within the U.S. government to fix their systems within the next three days.

The urgent new warning to patch comes less than a week after the makers of the decades-old PHP web programming language rolled out patches for the two bugs, which allow hackers to remotely break into a vulnerable system over the internet without the need for passwords. The PHP software is reportedly used by some 70% of web-facing servers, putting major websites and applications at risk. The severity of the bugs prompted some governments and big companies to take their sites offline to fix them.

While the bugs are rated critical, meaning they can be highly damaging if exploited, security researchers said that developing a working exploit to hack into vulnerable systems was initially challenging — but not impossible. Confirmed in-the-wild exploitation now puts extra pressure on organizations to patch their systems to prevent malicious compromises, disruption, and data breaches.

CISA did not provide details about who was exploiting the bugs in its Monday statement, but its statement comes soon after security researchers said they found evidence that the PHP bugs were being exploited.

The bugs, known as PHP2Shell, exploit vulnerabilities in the software that went undiscovered for years. The bugs were initially uncovered two years ago but the nonprofit foundation that maintains the software said there was no practical way for hackers to exploit them, leaving the bugs unpatched. That was until earlier this year, when researchers at security research firm Calif uncovered a way to exploit the two dormant vulnerabilities with the assistance of an AI model.

The two bugs in PHP are among countless vulnerabilities that have not yet been discovered or have been deprioritized for years but now pose a risk due to advances in AI models’ ability to find and exploit vulnerabilities, including by chaining multiple flaws together.

PHP is one of many aging web technologies that help keep the internet and modern technologies running, but that have been under-resourced and under-funded for years, making it difficult for open source projects like PHP to pay for security reviews that might have uncovered flaws sooner.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

United Airlines is willing to accept more empty seats if higher fares produce a better financial result. That may frustrate passengers wondering why a flight with plenty of availability still costs so much, but it is a useful reminder that filling an aircraft and making money are not the same thing.

One more tidbit from United’s October 21 earnings call last week. Chief Commercial Officer Andrew Nocella explained that the airline expects its efforts to recover higher fuel costs through pricing to result in somewhat lower load factors.

Load factor measures the percentage of available seat miles occupied by paying passengers. In essence, it tells us how full the airline’s flights are.

“We do not manage to a fixed load factor target.”

Instead, he explained, United focuses on revenue relative to capacity and its long-term profitability. Here’s his full statement:

“Look, I think it’s obvious where the price of fuel is. It’s obvious where our total CASM is. It’s also, therefore, mathematically obvious what yields need to do. Do I expect there to be some impacts on load factor? There was a tiny bit in Q3. There will probably be a little bit more in Q4.

“But we know where yields need to be for United. We are taking all the appropriate actions to get them there so we can recover 100% of fuel. I do expect that that means slightly lower load factors. I will just reiterate, we do not manage to a fixed load factor target. That’s not how it works. It’s not how it should work.

“We manage to maximize our RASM and our long-term margin goal, and if load factors fall, they are incredibly high, by the way, relative to all of the past in my experience in this business. That’s perfectly okay.”

He had already offered an example: leisure revenue increased 16% in the third quarter while leisure load factor declined 1.3 percentage points. United was comfortable with that tradeoff.

This does not mean United plans to stop discounting or would reject a profitable additional passenger. It does mean management is willing to lose some price-sensitive business if the higher fares paid by the remaining passengers more than compensate.

There’s certainly an argument to be made for selling every last seat. The aircraft is going anyway. The crew is being paid. Once the door closes, an empty seat produces nothing. Why not sell it cheaply and collect at least some revenue?

Sometimes that makes sense. But an airline also has to consider what happens before departure, when it does not yet know who will buy.

Imagine a simplified example, ignoring differences in flight length and the additional costs of carrying each passenger. Selling 150 seats at an average fare of $200 produces $30,000. Selling 140 at an average fare of $230 produces $32,200.

The second flight has more empty seats and more revenue.

Of course, United cannot simply raise every fare and assume nearly everyone will keep buying. The difficult part is figuring out how many passengers will accept the higher price, how many will choose another airline, and how many will stay home.

A cheaper ticket might attract someone who otherwise would not travel. It might also go to someone who would have paid substantially more. If passengers become confident that an airline will slash fares shortly before departure, some will learn to wait (something, by the way, I notice with premium fares at many carriers, though much less likely with economy fares).

Thus, the decision is not always between selling one seat cheaply and leaving it empty. It can involve the effect of that cheap seat on what other customers are willing to pay long before the flight departs.

Without understanding the precise systems running forecasters, I have to imagine that AI has made the ability to predict loads more sharper than ever.

As I’ve been arguing often lately, loyalty is a two-way street and passengers must make their own calculation when considering which carrier or route to fly.

An expensive flight with open seats is not necessarily a pricing mistake and an airline is not obligated to offer a last-minute bargain simply because its seat map looks empty, which is itself an imperfect indication of how many tickets have been sold.

But United should also recognize that customers have alternatives.

I enjoy flying United and appreciate its investments, particularly Starlink. Nevertheless, I compare schedules and prices. A better product can justify paying more, but there is a limit to what I will pay for it, especially becuase American Airlines and Delta Air Lines also have functional free Wi-Fi on most domestic flights.

A traveler who skips one expensive trip may return for the next one. A traveler who tries another airline and has a perfectly good experience may start questioning why he was so committed to United in the first place (raising hand).

That is harder to capture in the revenue calculation for a single flight.

I am not suggesting United has overlooked this: Nocella and his team are very smart. Pricing is considerably more sophisticated than looking at a seat map and choosing a number. But I again caution United to be careful about assuming that customers who accept higher fares today will keep doing so indefinitely.

But generally speaking, I also don’t bet (ever) that the price of a trip will drop. If the fare is too high, I look at other flights, dates, or airports. Sometimes I decide the trip is not worth taking.

United can decide what it wants to charge. I can decide whether the flight is worth buying.

United is comfortable accepting somewhat lower occupancy if higher fares generate better revenue and profitability. I understand the logic, even though we would all rather find a cheap ticket on a flight with plenty of open seats.

For passengers, the practical lesson is that unsold seats do not guarantee an approaching sale. Waiting for a bargain can work, but United is telling us that filling every seat is not its objective. As United continues to try to recoup its much higher fuel expenses, I would not be surprised to see it at least try more aggreqievsly to charge more for seats, even at the expense of load factors.

AI is finding new holes in the infrastructure of the internet. Is anyone around to patch them?

For the past week, software engineers around the globe have been scrambling to update the backend of websites after a team using an OpenAI model discovered two major vulnerabilities in PHP, a critical piece of web software.

PHP, open-source scripting software that underpins as many as 70% of websites worldwide, can become outdated when website owners don’t update to newer versions of the software, making them vulnerable to attacks. Shortly after alerts were issued about the new vulnerabilities, new versions of PHP were issued, and site operators were urged to update. Some government websites went dark in Europe as these updates were applied, but for much of the web, it’s been left to individual website owners to apply the fixes.

No law compels them to do so, although if a website suffers a data breach, the owner could be liable to customers or clients. And for web surfers, it’s not obvious what PHP version a site is running without running terminal commands or using an online PHP checker.

Given that some websites are no longer actively managed — I own a website that was latent and hadn’t had a PHP update or updates of its WordPress software in years, for instance — the PHP story has shown that the web’s aging architecture is proving to be insecure due to neglect and dwindling resources to keep it safe.

One of the most shocking revelations that has come out of the last week’s race to get websites updated and safe is that an organization tasked with making sure security flaws in PHP are addressed is itself on shaky ground.

Reuters reported that the PHP Foundation, a nonprofit formed in 2021 to work with researchers and cybersecurity companies to ensure a crisis like this wouldn’t happen, has only one full-time staff member and lacks the resources to expand.

The Foundation’s Executive Director Elizabeth Barron told Reuters that she’s the sole staff member and works with about 14 part-time contractors and unpaid board members. As AI-generated threats increase, there’s twice as much demand to change PHP code to address them as in the past. “Yet the number of contributors hasn’t doubled,” Barron said. While companies and governments rely on PHP, “It is clear PHP is severely underfunded,” she said.

A representative for PHP Foundation did not immediately return a request for comment.

Nearly all small businesses have some sort of web presence, whether it’s a social media account, a Google listing or a website they own. But small business owners, a survey from Network Solutions found, may not be addressing the PHP issue because they don’t know it affects them.

The report, based on a survey of 121 small business owners with their own websites, found 45% of them had never heard of PHP and 71% didn’t know if their websites use it. A large majority of 71% of these site owners hadn’t heard about the recent PHP flaws.

Rick Radinger, principal systems architect for Network Solutions Group, said that the survey showed awareness and knowledge gaps, but that in addition to not knowing about the current PHP problem, they may not think it’s their responsibility to act on a fix. “An owner might know about the warning but could assume their hosting provider will handle it,” Radinger said. “Others might need additional help but aren’t sure who to contact or next steps.”

Some of the mobilization about the PHP alerts included updates from large web infrastructure companies like Cloudflare and web hosts such as GoDaddy. In a blog post, Cloudflare said it deployed new web application firewall protections to detect and fend off attacks. GoDaddy made updates to its managed hosting and Windows hosting services that didn’t require website owners to take extra steps, but said that domain customers who manage their own sites or run virtual private servers may need to update PHP themselves.

In addition to its security updates, Cloudflare has also introduced a PHP Capture the Flag contest as part of its Bug Bounty Program on HackerOne. “We’ve put a deliberately vulnerable PHP installation behind our (firewall), and we’re inviting security researchers on HackerOne to try to bypass the protections and capture a flag,” Cloudflare said in its blog post. Cash prizes range from $100 to $10,000.

For those who don’t own a website, the PHP vulnerabilities aren’t something you can protect against by updating your passwords; it’s up to websites to stay updated to protect their visitors.

“For users, it is frustrating that they can’t fix this, as it is an issue with the site’s server, not the browser,” Radinger said. “Simply updating your browser doesn’t address the issue, and a site being up and running normally does not mean the site has been fixed.”

The usual advice applies more than ever: Be careful about clicking on unfamiliar website links, don’t share sensitive information with websites you don’t trust and be especially cautious about links that will download software to your device.

The PHP crisis is a good wake-up call about what’s happening to the World Wide Web as it approaches its fourth decade of operation. This latest security scramble is just one of the threats websites face (AI-generated breaches are a huge one). But many of the dangers to come may stem from aging digital architecture; PHP is just one of myriad tools websites use, including JavaScript, SQL databases, content management systems (like WordPress or Wix), and the software that the servers themselves run. If those aren’t regularly monitored, managed and updated across the web, you can expect a lot more vulnerabilities to emerge in the years to come.
