The iPhone Duo started landing in customers’ hands last Friday, and reports of screen damage soon followed. It’s obviously a problem for some owners, but how widespread is it? According to Apple, the issue affects only a “small number” of units.

Some iPhone Duo owners have reported bright white lines appearing on the inner display, sometimes within hours of unboxing. Apple has acknowledged a display issue now, saying: “We are aware of an issue impacting the inner display on a small number of iPhone Duo units. Google DeepMind released its newest world model, Genie 4, in research preview on Tuesday. The latest model generates the most photorealistic and interactive worlds yet, allowing users to type prompts mid-simulation to change the scene. The model also generates real-time audio that matches the simulated world, including music, ambient noises, and interactive dialogue.

The release of Genie 4 comes a couple of months after AI video startup Runway released its second world model, GWM Worlds 2, a playable model that responds dynamically to user inputs and includes audio generation. At the same time, the world model industry is beginning to consolidate. Fei-Fei Li’s World Labs is being acquired by AMD, Amazon is in talks to buy Decart, and Rivian just acquired Third Dimension to bring simulation capabilities in-house for self-driving testing.

Genie 4 is Google’s latest move to stay ahead in the crowded field.

Google released its predecessor, Genie 3, as a research preview a little over a year ago. Aside from being far more photorealistic and featuring audio, Genie 4 allows for free-form edits, like changing the weather or camera angle in real time or adding new people or objects. It can maintain a consistent scene for up to two minutes, double its predecessor’s limit, and offers better physics and more humanlike characters.

Gemini, Google’s AI model, is also more closely integrated with Genie 4, acting as the director that plans steps and checks frames while Genie creates the visuals. In a research experiment tied to Gemini Robotics, a Gemini 4 Argon model broke down tasks into steps that Genie 4 simulated in real time. The experiment shows a possible way to test robot scenarios in simulation before they happen in the real world.

“What we’ll increasingly see in the future are these hybrid world models, where you have a combination of Gemini writing code and text, and Genie ingesting that in real time and simulating reality at different levels of abstraction,” Tejas Kulkarni, research director at Google, said during a briefing.

As with other world models, the pitched commercial use cases include gaming, interactive learning, and robotics. One speculative example of interactive learning would be entering a painting of a historical scene and chatting to the painting’s subjects about the event portrayed.

But “trusted testers” — the people who get first access to Google’s cutting-edge products — are pointing to a possible new use: prototyping films.

“The real time aspect becomes most valuable in the areas where keeping a state of flow in the creativity space becomes incredibly useful,” Diego Rivas, a Google DeepMind engineer, said during a press briefing. “For example, in the ideation phase, in the storyboarding phase — anytime you’re kind of in need of rapid ideation.”

Google also offers Veo, its AI video-generation model that makes high-definition clips with built-in synchronized audio from text, image, or video prompts — a tool that has been growing in popularity among filmmakers.

Rivas added that users could, eventually, rely on Gemini Omni, a multimodal large language model that can generate video from images, audio, and text, to turn those prototypes into creative assets. Though those capabilities, he says, are still a ways off.

Throughout the briefing, Rivas stressed that it’s still early days for Genie 4 and that it’s not yet clear what the end-to-end product experience will be. The team also acknowledged that Genie 4 still has gaps: Details and objects can drift the longer a simulation runs, and they still haven’t quite figured out how to ensure the audio will be accurate and any in-world text will be legible.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

ANYbotics AG, a leading provider of four-legged inspection robots for heavy industry, today announced it’s shipping the world’s first legged robot designed to operate in dangerous environments where a spark or fire could trigger an explosion.

The new robot, named ANYmal X, is a dog-like robot that can patrol and inspect equipment where specialist technicians otherwise move between heavy equipment wearing dampened gear and avoid situations that might trigger an explosion.

The platform is now shipping to several oil, gas and chemical industry leaders, including multinational energy company Equinor ASA and Swiss-Dutch specialty chemical company DSM-Firmenich AG.

“Four years of pioneering engineering with more than ten patents went into ANYmal X,” said co-founder and Chief Executive Péter Fankhauser. “Many experts thought a legged robot could never be made safe for explosive atmospheres. Oil, gas and chemical plants are among the most hazardous and most costly places to operate.”

Energy and chemical industry leaders BP p.l.c., Equinor, Shell plc, Petroliam Nasional Berhad, Petróleo Brasileiro S.A., BASF SE and SLB N.V. began testing the ANYmal robots in 2023 across hundreds of onshore and offshore facilities through ANYbotics early access program. They used the data and knowledge from their participation to refine the platform into the industrial-grade robot being shipped today.

Making a legged robot safe around flammable gases is not simply a matter of giving it a tougher shell. It means controlling potential ignition sources throughout the machine so its electrical components and hot surfaces cannot set off the surrounding atmosphere.

Legged robots are made up of complex components, which must be isolated from the atmosphere. As a result, they are banned from oil, gas and chemical refineries and other hazardous atmospheres where explosions can happen. In these environments, preventable downtime can translate into massive profit losses.

Autonomous inspection robots target a growing industrial market, with research firms estimating global oil-and-gas inspection robotics revenue at roughly $800 million to $1 billion annually, according to Fortune Business Insights. The broader opportunity includes chemical plants and spending on conventional inspection services, although published estimates do not consistently isolate that combined market.

Undetected equipment failures can cost companies roughly $1.4 trillion each year in downtime, according to ANYbotics. In the oil and gas sector alone, Siemens AG reported a single hour can translate to almost $500,000 and the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health reported that historically extraction workers in the United States face dangerous conditions, creating fatality rates seven times the national average.

“We asked one question over and over: Which credible event in the life of a walking robot could create an ignition source, and how do we make sure it never does?” said Chief Technology Officer Andrea Corda.

Companies make fixed equipment compliant by housing it in heavy, explosion-proof enclosures, with limited exposure to the outside environment. Legged robots are a completely different endeavor, with exposed cameras and sensors required for navigation and ports needed for docking. Every component must be certified explosion-proof so it won’t ignite a spark, or, if it does, it will prevent heat sources or combustion from spreading to outside environments — all while maintaining payload capacity, agility and operating speed.

The company said it engineered ANYmal X from the ground up to comply with oil, gas and chemical industry safety standards. ANYbotics performed comprehensive ignition risk assessments and implemented safeguards for every potential ignition source.

“Today, more than 50 power, metals and materials customers use this system to run over 120,000 autonomous inspections every month,” said Chief Revenue Officer Thierry Obédé.

#3672. This '70s Phenomenon Led To The Cancellation Of Several Promising Western Shows

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

When "Gunsmoke" was abruptly canceled after a 20-year run in 1975, it was already somewhat of an anachronism. When the legendary Western series emerged in 1955, the small screen oater was entering a golden age built upon the success of shows such as the late-'40s TV Western featuring now forgotten actor William Boyd, "Hopalong Cassidy." By the mid-'70s, however, the TV landscape was almost unrecognizable. Much of that was due to a phenomenon known as the "rural purge" which saw networks pivoting away from rural-focused shows to deliver urban-based, socially aware entertainment. Viewed from that perspective, it was amazing "Gunsmoke" held on as long as it did. But that wasn't the only Western felled by the rise of urban-focused TV.

By the end of the 1960s the Western genre was already undergoing a major evolution. Gone were the days of John Wayne's simplistic Western heroes. In 1969, films such as Sam Peckinpah's "The Wild Bunch" and Sergio Leone's "Once Upon a Time in the West" were heralding the arrival of the revisionist era, and over the next decade Western films would present a much more irreverent and morally complex vision of the American West.

Meanwhile, TV was undergoing its own revolution. In 1970, there were still several Western series on the air. "Gunsmoke" was one example, but there was also the similarly long-running "Bonanza," as well as James Drury's "The Virginian" and "Death Valley Days." By the mid 1970s, however, they would all be canceled due to a series of factors entirely out of their control.

Leif Erickson's underrated Western series "The High Chaparral" began airing in 1967. Hailing from "Bonanza" creator David Dortort, the show was a hit but lasted nowhere near as long as "Bonanza," wrapping up in March 1971. It was far from the only TV show to get the axe that year.

As Pat Buttram of "Green Acres" famously put it, 1971 was "the year CBS canceled everything with a tree." The network had become well-known for capitalizing on the popularity of rural comedies throughout the 1960s, debuting such beloved shows as "The Beverly Hillbillies," "The Andy Griffith Show," "Petticoat Junction," "Hee-Haw," and Buttram's "Green Acres." CBS wasn't the only network to jump on the trend, either. It started with ABC's "The Real McCoys" which began airing in 1957 and functioned as an almost prototypical "Beverly Hillbillies," focusing on an Appalachian family who moves to the San Fernando Valley. By the end of the 1960s, however, all these shows were put on the chopping block. What happened? Well, the predilections of one CBS executive seem to be a big part of it.

Sara K. Eskridge, author of "Rube Tube: CBS and Rural Comedy in the Sixties" spoke to Woman's World about how much of the rural purge can be traced back to CBS programming chief Fred Silverman, who arrived in the late 1960s and started cleaning house. "He was looking at the data, which was not really something that had been done to any real extent before this," she explained. "He realizes only old people watch those [rural comedy-type] shows. They'd been on the air for more than 10 years, starting to lose favor with the younger crowd, so he cancels them."

Sara K. Eskridge noted to Woman's World how Fred Silverman seemed to have a personal animus toward rural shows, which he'd previously expressed in his masters thesis. When he came to cancel those series en masse, then, many of them were still garnering significant ratings. "These are shows that were in the top 10, most of them in the top 20 when they were canceled," Eskridge said. As far as Silverman was concerned, however, they brought in a "lower class of viewers" and had to go.

Silverman's approach caught on. Suddenly networks were cancelling everything "with a tree in it," to use Pat Buttram's phrase. The mass cull came to a head at the end of the 1970–71 television season, where rural series were suddenly replaced by a new raft shows epitomized by the likes of "All in the Family" and "The Mary Tyler Moore Show." Meanwhile, Westerns found themselves lumped in with the "rural" series, and suddenly "The Virginian," which had only just welcomed Lee Majors as a new addition to its cast, and "The High Chaparral" were gone. "Bonanza" lasted until 1973, before James Arness' Marshall Matt Dillon was told to hand in his badge in 1975 when "Gunsmoke" was canceled.

But TV Westerns didn't die altogether. The same year James Drury and Leif Erickson were told to pack it up, ABC greenlit "Alias Jones and Smith," a show about two outlaw cousins trying to live on the straight and narrow. "Little House on the Prairie" arrived in 1974, and "Maverick" star James Garner began fronting an offbeat Western about a pacifist sheriff in "Nichols." Otherwise, however, TV had purged rural entertainment from the airwaves, and the cowboys were, for the first time since the 1950s, well and truly outnumbered.

When the links to Scott emerged, most were dubious. After all, he’d be a nine-figure signing in pounds, let alone Euros. So, it was always a bit of a dream that no one truly expected – or seriously expected, maybe – to happen.

Now, Milan’s chances have lowered further, with talks of a January departure increasing. Gerry Cardinale plans to help the squad in January, but Scott wouldn’t be a priority.

As Ben Jacobs – a transfer expert based in England – reported on X, it seems the Rossoneri could be outpriced by others in the race, as MilanNews relays.

“Chelsea are increasingly confident of signing Bournemouth midfielder Alex Scott in January. Scott is Chelsea’s main target after Enzo Fernandez left for Manchester City on the final day of the transfer window over the summer.

“Bournemouth want over £100 million and Xabi Alonso’s side are already back in fresh talks after making multiple offers over the summer. Liverpool are also interested in Scott, but are less urgent in adding a midfielder in January.

“Monaco’s Lamine Camara, whom Chelsea failed to sign on the final day of the transfer window, and Roma’s Manu Kone are two other targets.”

Considering that the Englishman had been considered a ‘forbidden’ dream anyway, it’s not exactly shocking news. However, it would’ve been nice to add Scott to the squad, with his undeniable quality and even higher ceiling.
