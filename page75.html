#3447. The reason why some USB ports are orange

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An orange USB port is an indicator, but not set in stone by industry standardization.

Take a look at some of the USB ports throughout your home. If you notice any orange ones, congratulations: there's a chance you have an "always-on" charging option. With these, the USB ports provide power through the power supply or internal battery even if the computer is shut off.

"Chance" is used rather loosely, however, because like many USB colors and symbols, orange in a USB port isn't a matter of industry standardization. There is no electronics rule that defines orange as a port that will charge your phone even if you turn off the computer (or whatever device the port is part of).

The color typically appears on the plastic insert inside a rectangular USB-A socket. A few manufacturers describe orange ports as "always-on," but some of these also support fast USB data transfers. Orange USB ports are often associated with USB 3.0, offering up to 5 Gbps speeds. But since it's not a universal spec, you should view an orange port as a power-related feature that requires confirmation.

This is further complicated by the fact that a manufacturer can highlight a special feature or function without promising a particular charging wattage. Or you might have an orange USB port that doesn't offer 5 Gbps speeds, yet still charges your phone when the power source is off.

Since orange essentially acts as a visual cue that a USB port is not standard, it all boils down to the manufacturer. Break out the computer or accessory's documentation (or check its online specs page), as that will be much more useful than a color chart. Intel and Advantech are good examples of two manufacturers using the same orange insert to highlight entirely different capabilities.

Where Intel is concerned, an orange USB port is an enhanced charging connection on some NUC PCs. Intel specifies up to 1.5 amps from that port, compared with 900 milliamps from the blue ports on those models. Meanwhile, Advantech uses orange ports as high-retention USB connectors, meaning the socket holds on to the plug really tightly. The withdrawal force required is 50 percent greater than a conventional USB connector.

In most color chart comparisons, both orange and blue USB ports use USB 3.0, which supports data transfer rates up to 5.0 Gbps. Given those specs, orange diverges from blue in the "always-on" category alone. Moving a USB cable from a blue to an orange port shouldn't make any difference unless you turn the PC or accessory off and need to charge something.

Blue isn't fully standardized either. According to USB-IF, it's the recommended color for SuperSpeed (USB 3.0, 5 Gbps) USB-A connectors. Even with that recommendation, it never hurts to double-check the specifications for each port individually, especially for non-standard USB colors like purple. Labels can help, but aren't always immediately visible or present at all.

The connected device and cable are important too: not every accessory can match a port's advertised maximum, and the cable has to support those capabilities as well. When you need fast charging, make sure the orange USB port in question is capable. When you need fast transfers, make sure that same orange port isn't going to be a bottleneck. An orange port will offer both in many cases, but you can't tell from the color alone.

#5056. Ripple Wins Final Approval to Establish Its Own U.S. Bank, Placing RLUSD Stablecoin Under Federal Oversight

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ripple has scored a major regulatory victory in the United States after securing final approval from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) to establish a federally regulated national trust bank. The milestone marks a significant step forward for the blockchain payments giant as it expands its footprint in the U.S. financial system and strengthens its push into cross-border payments and asset tokenization.

Ripple CEO Brad Garlinghouse broke the news in an X post late Monday, revealing that the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the federal agency responsible for supervising national banks, had granted approval to Ripple National Trust Bank (RNTB).

The approval effectively gives one of the crypto industry’s biggest names a foothold in the federally regulated banking sector, highlighting the growing convergence between digital assets and traditional finance.

“We’ve always said crypto and banks work better together — this is what that looks like. Real innovation, real accountability, federal oversight,” Garlinghouse wrote.

Under the approved framework, Ripple National Trust Bank can manage reserves backing Ripple’s RLUSD stablecoin, serve as a collateral trustee for stablecoin holders, and offer digital asset custody services to institutional clients.

Despite the new approval, Standard Custody & Trust Company will continue to issue RLUSD, Ripple’s limited-purpose trust company regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services (NYDFS). The national charter will add federal OCC oversight to the stablecoin’s existing state-level regulatory framework.

Launched in December 2024, RLUSD has since reached a market capitalization of roughly $2.58 billion, according to Ripple. The stablecoin is used across the company’s payments infrastructure and as collateral within Ripple Prime, extending its role in Ripple’s growing institutional financial services business.

“The revolution isn’t happening outside the system. It’s happening from within it,” Garlinghouse opined.

Ripple began pursuing the national banking charter in July 2025, receiving conditional approval from the OCC in December that year. The regulator has now granted final approval, completing a process that began more than a year ago.

Despite Ripple’s major banking breakthrough, XRP has yet to mount a significant rally in response to the announcement.

In early trading on October 27, the token traded around $1.30, up only 1.2% from the previous 24 hours. The muted reaction suggests that, for now, investors have yet to translate Ripple’s regulatory milestone into substantial buying pressure for XRP.

Notably, Ripple CTO Emeritus David Schwartz offered a more optimistic view of the link between XRP’s value and network adoption. In a recent exchange on X, Schwartz argued that widespread use of the network for financial applications would likely translate into stronger demand for XRP.

He highlighted several features that could support the token’s value, including its role as a counterparty-free asset that any account can accept and its use in paying transaction fees on the ledger. According to Schwartz, these built-in functions could help drive demand for XRP as adoption expands.

That said, Ripple’s banking approval could strengthen RLUSD adoption and make its broader ecosystem more attractive to financial institutions, including those exploring XRP for cross-border payments. If this expansion translates into greater use of XRP as a bridge asset for international settlements, it could drive higher demand and trading activity, potentially boosting the token’s price. However, the impact will depend on whether institutional adoption generates meaningful demand for XRP itself.

#3136. After another ugly loss, Nikola Jokić sticks to his script: Nuggets fans who boo 'are not real fans'

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Nikola Jokić watched the final three and a half minutes of Monday night's matchup with the Golden State Warriors from the bench. Denver Nuggets head coach David Adelman had pulled his starters after a Gui Santos 3-pointer gave the visitors a 21-point lead — the sort of deficit that not even a three-time NBA Most Valuable Player could erase, even if he and his teammates were playing at the peak of their powers. Which, a week into the 2026-27 NBA season, the Nuggets very evidently are not.

In most cases, you'd expect that a 20-point drubbing at the hands of a Warriors team that had entered winless and mostly feckless would elicit some level of displeasure from the paying public — a smattering of jeers from the upper deck, at least. And yet, as Jokić and his teammates sat idle while the second unit played out the string, the remaining fans in the stands at Ball Arena largely sat on their hands, barely registering any noise above a murmur in the closing moments of what would settle as a 134-114 blowout.

Which, it seems, is the way Jokić would prefer it.

It's been a rocky start to the season for Nikola Jokic and his Denver Nuggets. (AAron Ontiveroz/The Denver Post)

See, on Saturday night, the fans in attendance at Ball Arena had let loose a fusillade of boos after the Nuggets — who'd been roundly beaten by the Oklahoma City Thunder on opening night — trailed the lowly New Orleans Pelicans by as many as 23 points in their home opener before staging a fourth-quarter comeback and eventually winning in overtime. After snatching victory from the jaws of defeat, the greatest player in franchise history took those fans to task for what he saw as capricious, insufficient support.

"I think those fans who booed us, they don't need to come anymore," Jokić said, according to Bennett Durando of the Denver Post. "Because if you guys follow us, you know that we never quit and we came back from much worse or similar situations. If they ask me, they are not welcome anymore."

Two nights later, the Nuggets authored a performance in which they allowed Golden State to shoot 22-for-44 from 3-point land, got outrebounded 45-32 and never held a lead after halftime. From the 4:30 mark of the second quarter through the 6:30 mark of the third, the Warriors blitzed the hosts 33-13, building a double-digit lead that they would never relinquish behind stellar nights from rookie forward Yaxel Lendeborg (31 points, 12 rebounds, 6 assists) and superstar point guard Stephen Curry (24 points in 27 minutes on a perfect 8-for-8 shooting).

Even after another dismal defensive outing to drop Denver to 1-2 on the young season, though, Jokić stood firm in his conviction that Nuggets fans are better served piping down, and don't have standing to let the boo birds fly:

"I just said what I said," Jokić, um, said. "It's not something that … I'm sticking to what I said. I played in a much worse environment [in Europe]. I played in front of 10 people, 50 people, 100 people, 150,000 people, whatever — I didn't play in front of 150,000 people, like, 10,000, 15,000 — and I'm not used to the fans booing their own team [...] I've never heard that. I mean, probably they are. But I think they are not real fans."

Jokić taking Nuggets fans to task comes in the somewhat uncomfortable context of the legendary big man not being under contract beyond next season. Yes, Jokić has repeatedly declared his intention to remain a Nugget for life, explaining his decision to wait to ink his next extension as purely a dollars-and-cents choice; by holding off until next summer, he can sign a five-year deal that would pay him an estimated $359.5 million, and that would stand as the largest contract in NBA history, according to ESPN's Bobby Marks. Even so, with no signature secured, every rumble of discontent from the franchise cornerstone reverberates louder and wider than it otherwise might.

You'd also understand if Jokić chastising the fans for expressing their frustration seems a bit tone-deaf, given the way the last few months have unfolded in Denver.

First, the Nuggets bowed out in the first round of the playoffs for the first time in five years, losing in six games to the rival Minnesota Timberwolves — the team that ended Denver's title defense in 2024 — with three of the losses coming by double-digits. Then, they let top bench scorer Tim Hardaway Jr. walk in free agency, bid farewell to veteran reserves Bruce Brown and Jonas Valančiūnas, traded athletic young wing Peyton Watson to Cleveland for future draft capital and did precious little to fortify the current roster beyond taking a flyer on veteran scorer DeMar DeRozan (though rookie second-round pick Trevon Brazile has shown some encouraging flashes).

The overarching message of the summer: with the Thunder and San Antonio Spurs perched atop the West, and a number of other teams taking big swings to try to combat them, the Nuggets were mostly content to stand pat and pare down their luxury tax bill rather than pushing harder to compete, even with the 31-year-old Jokić still in his prime as arguably the best player in the world. You can understand why fans might not necessarily be thrilled with that … and why, when the team comes out of the chute needing a 23-point comeback against a bad Pelicans team to avoid being 0-3, they might find themselves looking for something more.

The Nuggets are capable of giving it to them. They were without four rotation wings on Monday, with starter Cameron Johnson, Julian Strawther, Spencer Jones and offseason signee Alpha Diallo all sidelined by injury, taxing an already questionable depth chart behind linchpins Jokić, Jamal Murray, Aaron Gordon (who got ejected early in the fourth quarter on Monday) and Christian Braun. With the exception of Murray, those linchpins have all gotten off to slow starts offensively, headlined by Jokić, who's shooting just 41.7% from the field and 21.7% from 3-point land — miles below his typical hyper-efficient marks.

As a team, Denver sits 16th in field goal percentage, 23th in 3-point percentage and 22nd in assists, and has seen its turnover rate and free-throw attempt rate both drop out of the top 10. Add it all up, and an offense that has finished top-10 in points scored per possession for a full decade is now sputtering in 22nd place in the NBA — even with Murray and Jokić averaging 38-plus minutes a game.

For a Nuggets team that even optimistic evaluators would expect to struggle to get to league-average on defense, having an elite offense is a prerequisite for contention — or even competing, most nights. Failing to produce even a facsimile of the kind of offensive unit that Denver fans have become accustomed to seeing … well, that's the kind of thing that might get you booed, however "real" you think the fandom of the discontented parties might be.

That's the rub right now in Denver. Jokić might be justified in thinking it unfair that fans who've watched him deliver a steady stream of 50-win teams, playoff runs and the franchise's first NBA championship are suddenly getting up in arms in the first half of the first home game of the season. Nuggets fans might be justified in thinking they've got the right to correctly identify that what they're seeing to start the campaign is a far cry from the level of play they've come to expect.

But when everybody's got a point, it's not always easy to find a resolution.

"I think he's earned the right to have an opinion. He's had a lot of success here," Adelman told reporters, according to Durando of the Post. "And the big thing for me is the fans have been here for all this time. … They've been here for [lots of winning]. The fans and players, it's communal. They feed off of each other. And just thinking back on all these environments that I've been lucky enough to be part of in this building, it's just, let's not forget that."

One surefire way to remind everyone of the good times? Get off the schneid on a two-game road trip that starts Wednesday with a rematch against the Pelicans … and, this time, maybe don't give up 73 first-half points and need a massive comeback to do it.

The Independent Chief Inspector of Borders and Immigration (ICIBI), which monitors the Home Office’s management of borders, immigration, and customs, has asked digital rights nonprofit Open Rights Group (ORG) to share more information on the tools as it draws up plans for the topics it will inspect next year, campaigner Sara Alsherif tells WIRED. The ICIBI tells WIRED it cannot confirm what topics are under active consideration before it publishes its annual inspection priorities, but it says it is aware of concerns about the use of AI tools and pointed to its findings last year that one of the tool’s “effectiveness and reliability were varied.”

“Asylum decisions are made by trained caseworkers, with AI tools used only to assist in navigating interview records and policy information. They cannot be used in isolation and do not make decisions on claims,” a Home Office spokesperson tells WIRED in an email. “We regularly monitor the performance of these tools alongside our established quality-assurance processes, and have already committed to publishing updated evaluations on their roll out.”

The Home Office uses the Asylum Case Summarisation (ACS) and Asylum Policy Search (APS) tools, built on OpenAI’s 2023 GPT-4 large language model and Microsoft Foundry, to summarize transcripts of interviews with asylum applicants and information about their countries of origin, according to documents obtained by ORG. The immigration and law enforcement authority started piloting these tools in late 2024 as the Labour government tried to clear a backlog of around 100,000 asylum cases, while staying on top of the tens of thousands of new applications the UK receives each year. While the department has made significant progress, halving the number of people waiting on an initial decision in the year through June, the number of appeals against decisions has ballooned.

Researchers are concerned about the accuracy of the AI tools–especially to decide something as high-stakes as whether to send someone back to a country where they fear persecution. ORG found that the percentage of summaries generated by the ACS deemed not fit for use—often because they contain factual errors or misrepresentations—is significantly higher than the published 9 percent error rate calculated in the pilot evaluation. There is no quality control for the summaries produced by the APS, it also found.

Asylum decision-makers use the tools to save time when reviewing asylum cases; when used together, the pilot showed the tools could save around an hour per case, according to government estimates. Caseworkers use the ACS to speed up their review of transcripts of interviews where asylum seekers explain how they were persecuted in their country and why they are afraid to return. They use the APS as a search assistant to find and summarize country policy information, which informs them whether the applicant faces a genuine risk of harm in their home country. In July, a UK judge accused the Home Office of citing an “AI hallucinated” document to refuse a Moroccan woman and her child’s asylum claim on the basis that her home country would be safe for her to return to.

“We’re dealing with people’s lives. We’re talking about people that have faced some of the worst circumstances, and we’re meant to be making decisions based on facts and on understanding harm,” Labour backbench MP Bell Ribeiro-Addy tells WIRED. “I just don’t think it’s appropriate that machines are doing that.”

A Home Office spokesperson says, “Use of Artificial Intelligence is just one of the tools at our disposal to protect our borders, and does not replace or automate the role of human decision-makers.”

Ribeiro-Addy, Alsherif, and the Public Law Project, which has separately been investigating the tools, also worry that well-documented “cultural bias” in GPT models could lead to language barriers and the loss of cultural nuances in the summaries, which could in turn result in discriminatory decisions. An OpenAI spokesperson highlighted to WIRED the company’s ongoing work to improve its models’ accuracy and pointed out that the company will be retiring GPT-4 in January.

In the absence of regulation, the UK’s “AI Playbook” mandates that departments must be transparent about the algorithmic tools they use in decision-making processes and make information on them available to the public, including listing those tools on a public repository. The Home Office’s case summarization and policy search tools are not on that list.

Asylum seekers are also not told when these AI tools are used in their case. “Migrant organizations, legal practitioners, not many of them know about the actual use of this tool,” says Alsherif, who leads ORG’s migrant digital justice program. “So we need to let them know about this, let them know how it works, so they can challenge it.” Not being able to contest an incorrect AI summary of their personal information may breach UK data rights laws, Cloisters lawyers wrote in an independent legal opinion commissioned by ORG. The Home Office states that parts of the decision-making process may involve automation in its privacy notice.

Both groups have sent their findings to UK data regulator, the Information Commission, which is currently investigating the Home Office’s refusal to tell ORG how staff use and were trained to use two AI tools.

Public Law Project senior research fellow Caroline Selman also notes that the ACS and APS tools are just two of the 11 high-risk AI systems that her team has found being used across government but absent from the public repository. She’s concerned there may be more deployed across government that even researchers, let alone the individuals they are being used on, are unaware of.

“Other tools are being used but not being publicized at all,” says Selman, “and you can’t even begin to ask questions about things if you don’t know that they’re being used.”
