#379. Smithsonian scientists just recreated the first 2 billion years of the Universe

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Scientists have created one of the largest and most detailed simulations of the early universe ever developed, offering a remarkable new way to explore how the cosmos evolved after the Big Bang. Known as "Lumina," the simulation was built by researchers at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian to help astronomers interpret observations from powerful instruments such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and Nancy Roman Space Telescope.

The research, published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, reconstructs the universe's first 2 billion years by modeling the interactions between ordinary matter, dark matter, and radiation. By comparing Lumina's predictions with observations from JWST, Roman, and other telescopes, scientists can evaluate how well existing theories explain cosmic evolution and identify discoveries that might challenge their understanding of the universe's fundamental laws.

Astronomers have spent centuries studying the sky and developing explanations for the formation of stars, galaxies, and other cosmic structures. Modern supercomputers now make it possible to turn these ideas into detailed simulations that predict how the universe should behave. When telescope observations agree with those predictions, they strengthen confidence in current theories. Unexpected differences, however, may point toward physical processes that scientists have yet to fully understand.

"With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics," said lead author Oliver Zier, an ITC Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian. "To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions. After several years of work, with plenty of ups and downs, it is incredibly rewarding to see the simulation results and watch our predictions come to life in video."

Recreating the Universe's First 2 Billion Years

Lumina is the largest cosmological radiation hydrodynamics simulation ever produced. In simple terms, it uses powerful computers to calculate how gas, dark matter, and light influenced one another as the young universe developed. By following these interactions across the first 2 billion years after the Big Bang, researchers can examine how the earliest galaxies formed and how their activity changed the enormous clouds of gas surrounding them.

The simulation models a volume of space more than 100 times larger than those covered by earlier comparable projects. Its virtual universe spans a cube approximately 1.5 billion light-years on each side, providing a view of cosmic evolution on a scale comparable to the vast regions that today's most advanced telescopes can study.

Another major advance is Lumina's treatment of helium reionization, a process in which radiation strips electrons from helium atoms. For the first time, a simulation of this kind follows that transformation as it unfolds while simultaneously accounting for how it affects the surrounding universe. This allows scientists to study the relationship between evolving galaxies, radiation, and the gas that fills the space between them.

"We feel incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina," said Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas and an author on the paper. "For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation."

A Massive Supercomputer Powers the Virtual Cosmos

Recreating such an enormous portion of cosmic history demanded extraordinary computing resources. The team used more than 15,000 graphics processing units on Frontier, one of the world's fastest supercomputers, housed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee.

That processing power enabled the researchers to simulate an exceptionally large region of the early universe without sacrificing the fine detail needed to investigate individual galaxies and other important structures. As a result, Lumina can capture the broader evolution of the cosmos while also tracking processes involved in the formation of stars, galaxies, and black holes.

Testing the Mysteries of the Early Universe

Beyond recreating cosmic history, Lumina provides a powerful new framework for investigating how the universe developed. Researchers plan to use it to explore the movement of gas between galaxies, the influence of radiation left over from the Big Bang, and the emergence of regions where stars formed at especially high rates.

By placing these predictions alongside observations from the James Webb Space Telescope, the Nancy Roman Space Telescope, and other instruments, scientists hope to build a more complete picture of the universe's earliest chapters. Any unexpected differences between the simulated cosmos and the real one could provide valuable clues, potentially revealing where current theories need to be refined or reconsidered.

Here's how most coffee professionals make their morning brew.

Coffee inspires passionate debate among its drinkers, including the best way to brew and drink it. There are die-hard espresso devotees, cold brew enthusiasts, and those who prioritize push-button convenience above all.

Among the coffee intelligentsia, a few brewing methods rise to the top. Drip coffee isn’t maligned, but it isn’t how most of the professionals I spoke with make their morning cup, even if most Americans do. Even espresso, the method of choice for most of Europe, falls out of the top spot when asked about the best way to brew.

Nearly every coffee pro I consulted was aligned.

So, then, what is the best process for brewing coffee? According to pros I spoke with, there is an answer, and it’s mostly unanimous. I asked 10 coffee experts — roasters, cafe owners, educators, barista champions, and one former president of the Specialty Coffee Association — to rank seven brewing methods: AeroPress, automatic drip machine, espresso, French press, K-Cup, moka pot and pour-over.

Pour-over earned the top spot by a healthy margin.

While it wasn’t completely unanimous, pour-over was the clear winner for the overall average ranking. The good news is, pour-over is among the least expensive coffee-brewing methods available, requiring little more than a handheld kettle or other pouring device and a funnel dripper with a conical filter that sits on top of the cup or carafe to catch the coffee. (You can certainly spend more money for a serious pour-over kit.) On the other hand, well-executed pour-over coffee can be fussy and isn’t a set-it-and-forget-it kind of method.

AeroPress got high marks from the coffee experts I spoke with.

While pour-over had the highest overall ranking, it wasn’t the only method a pro ranked No. 1. AeroPress — an analog method that combines the agitation of pour-over, the immersion of French press and the pressure of espresso — had a couple of acolytes and came safely in second place in the overall ranking.

Marwyn Garcia, owner of Headcount Coffee, ranked it as number one among the available options (though noted that other, less common methods, such as an immersion brewer or coffee siphon, are also worthy adversaries). The AeroPress steep time is adjustable, allowing for personal preference in the brew, and he also emphasized bean quality, noting its importance in any method.

“Overall flavor is more dependent on how fresh your coffee is,” he says. “As well as the roast level, as it can require a longer brew time or steep to get the desired result.”

Among those who placed it second after pour-over, Howard also notes that AeroPress has definite upside: “It has great clarity and versatility,” he says, “especially for travel or a quick cup.”

Espresso and French press tied for third.

Espresso and French press also received first-place rankings from certain coffee professionals, and effectively tied for third in terms of confidence among c.

Heather Perry, the CEO of Klatch Coffee, a two-time US barista champion and former president of the Specialty Coffee Association, notes that it’s espresso that is used to measure barista performance.

“It’s true that you need some specialized equipment and time to get the best results with espresso, but once you’ve got your machine dialed in, the characteristics of great espresso really shine through,” she says. “Wonderful, intense flavor, beautiful crema, and a great aroma too. Sweetness, acidity, and bitterness are all balanced in harmony.”

Those who favored the French press cited its ritualistic nature and its ability to easily make more than one cup.

“I’m a fan of the French press method because it really captures the relaxed, hands-on ritual of making coffee that I love,” says Bernadette Gerrity, vice president at Café Aroma. “I’ve always preferred old-school brewing methods to new gadgets because you can control everything, from the proportions to the flavor, without any waste,” she says. “It’s really easy to dial in your perfect cup with a French press. Plus, it’s quick, simple and satisfying — just coffee and water, no buttons or special pods needed.”

“It’s true that you need some specialized equipment and time to get the best results with espresso, but once you’ve got your machine dialed in, the characteristics of great espresso really shine through,” said Heather Perry.

“French press hits all of the marks, and it allows for several cups of coffee at a time, which allows the pot to be enjoyed by others,” says former professional coffee roaster Dave Linari of NewSylum Brewing. “I find the coarse grind of the bean and the coarse filter allow all of the flavors and aromas to come through as the bean intended,” he says. “The filter allows the coffee’s natural oils and fine particles to remain in the cup, which creates a richer texture and taste, and the whole system is a very simple and affordable piece of equipment, which you can use to froth milk as well.”

Pour-over brewing lets you control precise variables, including temperature, flow rate, and agitation.

“With pour-over, a slow, deliberate pour allows control of precise variables including temperature, flow rate and agitation,” says Theo Chan, coffee reviewer and managing editor of Coffee Roast, “ensuring even extraction and revealing a coffee’s nuanced sweetness, acidity and aromatics.” (Agitation refers to the water’s ability to circulate among the coffee grounds during the brew.)

“Maximum control over variables equals consistency,” says James McCarthy, coffee roaster and head of education at Colombian coffee brand Devoción, who specifically recommends an origami pour-over dripper. “Since you’re using your own kettle, you can choose the water temperature and vary the flow rate according to preference.”

“For a cup of black coffee, I’d say pour-over is my go-to,” says Joe Howard, co-founder of YAWN Brew. “With a pour-over, you can really highlight the coffee’s unique characteristics of the origins. It’s a clean brew method that gives you good precision of the coffee.”

One coffee expert I spoke with lauded pour-over for its ability to tease out the delicate flavor of certain roasts.

Michael Cramer, founder of Lardera Coffee, notes that roast level is also an important factor in determining which method is favored. “I prefer light-roasted beans, and pouring water by hand produces a more uniform extraction, teasing out their intricate flavors and delicate aromas,” he says. “This requires more time and attention than other methods, but if you care about coffee, it’s a worthwhile investment. You’re giving up ease for the sake of consistency and flavor, which seems a trade-off worth making.”

The ranking also considered the type of filter each method relies on. “Pour-overs use paper filters that remove oils, resulting in a cleaner and more delicate flavor profile highlighting the intricacies in a coffee while being able to represent any roast level well,” says Megan Biolsi, manager of coffee education and Training at Sightglass Coffee. “They typically require a little fine-tuning to achieve a great cup, but allow for intricate adjustments for those who want to experiment and perfect their morning brew.”

Moka pots have a certain charm, but coffee experts rank this brewing method lower on the list.

While the moka pot, a stovetop espresso brewing method, and the automatic drip machine both received middle-of-the-pack votes, coffee professionals unanimously voted the K-Cup the least favored method. While Howard generously noted that it was “quick and tidy,” the consensus was that it “sacrifices flavor and texture,” allows no adjustment, and produces unnecessary waste with every cup.

#5025. Willy Woo Runs 10,000 Bitcoin Simulations and Finds a 2028 Top Near $413,000: How Real Is It?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin news: Willy Woo’s 10,000-path simulation tops at $413K in 2028. How diminishing returns shape it, and what the model leaves out.

On-chain analyst Willy Woo posted 10,000 simulated price paths for Bitcoin on Monday, October 26, from a random-walk model trained on BTC’s history that builds in the shrinking returns of each cycle, to map where the next tops and bottoms could land. The Bitcoin news traders noticed: the median path peaks at $413,000 in December 2028.

Woo himself is more cautious. “She’s optimistic,” he wrote, naming the band between roughly the 28th and 50th percentile as reasonable, a 2028 top between $241,000 and $413,000, and adding: “It’s a simulation, NOT my prediction.”

Bitcoin trades at $79,770 today. So what did the model find, and what does it leave out?

A rising staircase with deep steps. Every percentile line keeps Bitcoin’s four-year rhythm: a top in late 2028 or early 2029, a bear-market low around April 2030, and another peak in February 2033.

The spread is the real result. By February 2033, the 90th percentile sits 33 times above the 10th. Even the gloomy 10th percentile ends 2033 at $120,000, but its 2030 low of $51,900 is about 35% below today’s price.

10,000 simulated Bitcoin price paths. Source: Willy Woo on X

What is the “power-law degradation of returns”? It is the idea that each Bitcoin cycle grows by less than the one before. The bigger the asset gets, the more money it takes to move it.

The history fits. Bitcoin went from about $1,150 at its 2013 top to nearly $20,000 in late 2017, a 17-fold gain. The 2021 top of $69,000 was about 3.5 times that, and October 2025’s record of $126,080 was only 1.8 times.

A power law describes that slide: returns fade steadily over time instead of in random jumps. The “c=0.45” in the chart’s subtitle sets how fast they fade. “Phase-conditioned” means each simulated day is drawn to match where Bitcoin sits in its cycle, whether rally, top, bear market, or recovery, which is why all 10,000 paths keep the four-year shape.

Next to that history, the median still looks generous. A $413,000 top would be 3.3 times the 2025 peak, almost double the last cycle’s gain. Woo’s lower bound, $241,000, is 1.9 times the peak, in line with the 1.8 times of the last cycle.

A lot, which is why Woo calls it a simulation. A random walk trained on past prices assumes Bitcoin will keep moving the way it has, with similar swings and the same four-year rhythm. The market around it has changed.

So the chart is a map of possibilities, with no price target and no timing in it.

Bitcoin trades at $79,770, down 2.1% in a week and 36.7% below its October 2025 record. It sits under the 50-day EMA at $80,765, and buyers defended $79,221 on Saturday and again on Monday.

Resistance comes at $81,480, where last week’s bounces stalled, and then at $82,850. Support sits at $79,221, Saturday’s low, with the 200-day EMA at $76,382 below it.

To reach even the low end of Woo’s band by 2028, Bitcoin would need to triple from here. The Bitcoin news that matters this week is smaller: whether $79,221 holds through Wednesday’s Fed decision.

#535. Best iPhone Duo hidden features thatll make you need this phone

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The iPhone Duo is a whole new type of device in Apple's lineup, and the first thought that came to mind when I got it was, "This thing has to have a bunch of cool new hidden tricks."

As it turns out, I was right. After 10 days with the Duo, I've found myself discovering new ways to do stuff almost every day. And it's not just easter eggs; I'm talking about features and tricks that have since become a regular part of my arsenal. I'm using these to do things faster, make my life easier, and yes, to show off to anyone who'll listen.

These are my favorite hidden features and tips for using the Apple iPhone Duo.

This one is simple, but so very satisfying. One of the features that annoyed me on all of the iPhones I've had over the years (ever since the iPhone X, that is) was the Control Center. It should show up when I swipe down from the top-right corner of my display, but sometimes it doesn't appear, or I accidentally open the Notification Center.

But on the iPhone Duo, you can open the Control Center in a new way. In the top-right of the display, there's a neat new icon that combines WiFi and cell signal strength with battery life. Tap that, and the Control Center immediately slides down (the combo icon also expands into separate icons that display more information about battery, cellular, and WiFi signal).

It took some getting used to, but this is a far better way to open the Control Center. Not only is it easier to tap an icon than to swipe down, but the Control Center opens reliably every single time.

I admit it: I haven't yet found a truly useful case for this one. But it does sound like it could be useful down the road. As noted by Federico Vittici, you can bind a Shortcut to opening and closing the iPhone Duo, which lets you do almost anything with those actions automatically.

Obvious ideas include launching a favorite app, changing the wallpaper, or just invoking the good old creaky door sound for Halloween. Whatever people end up doing with it, it's definitely one trick that you just cannot do on any other iPhone.

The first issue I've had with the iPhone Duo is something I'll call orientation fatigue. The phone is so much fun to use when it's open that I kept flipping it around to see how the apps would adapt, but I constantly found myself lost about where the power, camera, and volume buttons are.

The iPhone Duo has two ways to mitigate the issue. The power button will, in certain cases, get highlighted when it's needed, though this is not always the case. But there's also a setting that dynamically adjusts the volume buttons based on the iPhone Duo's orientation.

The setting is located in Settings > Sounds & Haptics > Fixed Position Volume Controls. By default, it's turned on, meaning the volume buttons will stay fixed no matter how you turn the phone. But if you switch this option off, the volume buttons will change so that the button on the right or on top is always the "volume up" button, no matter how you hold the phone.

Apple highlighted this one, so it's not exactly hidden, but it's slightly more challenging to invoke, making it easier to miss. The iPhone Duo supports "regular "multitasking in horizontal mode, in which you drag two apps to opposite sides of the display to get a side-by-side view.

If you turn the phone to a vertical position, the side-by-side view will vanish. But you can also multitask in a vertical position, though under one condition: one of the apps has to be a video PiP (picture-in-picture) window. To get this, open a video app such as YouTube, start a video, then minimize the video app. The video should stay in a tiny window floating above all other apps on the Duo's display. Now, start a second app, turn the Duo vertical, and drag the little video window to the top of the display. You'll get a cool split-screen view where the video plays on top, and the other app sits beneath it.

On any other phone, this wouldn't be very useful, but the Duo's display is wide enough to make this a viable option.

In its promo materials, Apple mostly highlighted StandBy in tent mode, in which you fold the iPhone Duo halfway, then position it on a flat surface like a little tent. When the phone goes to sleep, it will launch a beautiful StandBy screen, with several customizable views.

But you can also get that when the phone is fully unfolded and positioned horizontally. If you have a case with a kickstand, you can position it upright and get a larger StandBy mode that doesn't have multiple views but is far more customizable in itself, allowing you to populate it with widgets such as Clock, Calendar, Photos, TV+, Stocks, Notes; nearly anything you can think of.

Both StandBy modes are useful. When I go to sleep, I prefer to have the phone in tent mode for a glance at the time. But when I work, I like the larger StandBy mode with a Calendar and a large world time view of the Clock.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Business secretary says focus for November summit will be trade and youth mobility, but talks will also include ‘future options for relationship’

British officials have begun discussions with the EU about a long-term relationship that could include a return to free movement, the business secretary has said while promising the British people “are the boss” when it comes to what the UK will accept.

Jonathan Reynolds said the focus for British negotiations was the EU summit in November to seal an agreement to ease some trading restrictions and a youth mobility deal. But he said the prime minister, Andy Burnham, had made it clear he wanted to see a long-term and stable relationship with the bloc.

“They will be talking to them about what the future options for our relationship might be. But here and now, the focus should be on the summit and the tangible difference improvements we’re going to make,” he told BBC Radio 4’s Today programme.

He said the changes announced at the EU summit, including a food and drink deal and on emissions trading, would make a significant difference. But he added: “What the prime minister has also said, quite rightly, is we face a series of policy changes on the European side that will affect us, and we need to get as little uncertainty as possible.

“We need to have a way to resolve what our long-term relationship will be, and he is going to set that out after the summit. And he is going to be frank with the British people about what the trade-offs are.”

Reynolds said there had been “no assumptions made about the next stage after that. It’s the prime minister quite rightly saying he wants to set out to the British people what the options are to resolve some of the tensions that exist.”

He said no decision would be made without voters being given a say, without specifying whether this would be through a general election or a referendum. “No assumptions have been made on this. The British people are the people who will decide this. They’re the boss on this,” he said.

A number of backbenchers with heavily pro-Brexit seats in the north and Midlands are wary about reopening the arguments for free movement or rejoining the EU.

The Labour MP for Great Grimsby and Cleethorpes, Melanie Onn, was among those urging caution. “I am still not over the kind of very divisive debate that we had when we were leaving the EU post the referendum, and I don’t think that the scars have healed for many people around the country,” she told Today. “So I do think that this is an unnecessary conversation to be having at this time.”

The Reform UK leader, Nigel Farage, told the programme it would be “very, very poor indeed” to begin discussing freedom of movement. “Five months ago, Andy Burnham says to leave-voting Makerfield: ‘I’m not going to reopen these arguments.’ Two and a half years ago, a Labour government got a massive majority in parliament saying we will not be re-entering the single market, the customs union, or opening up the debate on the free movement of people,” he said.

“What I am saying is this: this prime minister has no mandate, and to hear that his officials are negotiating in Brussels already is really very, very poor indeed.”

Burnham made his plea for an “honest discussion” about freedom of movement in an interview with the Sunday Times, making clear that staying out of it would no longer be a red line in talks with the EU.

Burnham also shared his belief that rejoining the single market and accepting free movement could be done without the need for another referendum, if Labour put it in its manifesto. In contrast, he said fully rejoining would need another in-out poll.

The prime minister said: “Freedom of movement had very significant challenges associated with it and I spoke about them in my previous time in parliament. But, at the same time, it was a type of immigration that perhaps wasn’t as difficult [as now].

“Revolving-door migration where people would come and then go back is different. The balance of migration has changed, hasn’t it, since then? I think we’ve just got to have an honest discussion about those things.”
