As Israelis head to the polls on Oct. 27, Americans are increasingly asking: Is Israel a good ally for the United States?

The long-standing bipartisan consensus holds that Israel is the United States’ closest regional ally and among its most important partners globally. However, more and more young Americans on both sides of the political aisle seem to be holding a different view. They see Israel as a genocidal and militaristic state, dragging the United States into unnecessary foreign wars.

In the War Room: The Inside Story of Israel’s Fight Against Hamas and the Iranian Axis, Yonah Jeremy Bob and Elliot Kaufman, Wicked Son, 352 pp., $32.50, September 2026

A new book helps shed light on this question by telling the remarkable story of how the Jewish state redrew the map of the Middle East and enabled Washington to address one of its gravest national security challenges. Absent the bravery and ingenuity of Israel’s armed forces, the United States could very well be living under the threat of a nuclear-armed Iran today.

Written by Yonah Jeremy Bob and Elliot Kaufman, In the War Room chronicles Israel’s national security policy and decision-making starting from Hamas’s attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, to the Gaza peace plan going into effect on Oct. 10, 2025. As leading journalists (Bob works at the Jerusalem Post and Kaufman works at The Wall Street Journal), the pair gained enviable high-level access to senior decision-makers in both Washington and Jerusalem and have provided a gripping first draft of this remarkable period in world history.

According to a quote apocryphally attributed to Vladimir Lenin, “There are decades when nothing happens, and there are weeks when decades happen.” This quote applies to the decades of history that transpired in the 104 weeks covered in Bob’s and Kaufman’s 300-page book.

So much has changed in this time that it is almost hard to remember what the Middle East looked like back in September 2023.

Iran was enjoying its greatest level of global power and influence since the time of the ancient Persian empire. It was a near-nuclear power with a proxy network projecting malign influence throughout the region. Experts at the time estimated that Iran’s breakout time to the bomb was essentially zero.

The Islamic Republic was a terrorist puppeteer pulling the strings on Hezbollah in Lebanon and Hamas in Gaza. Hezbollah alone possessed more than 100,000 rockets, essentially holding Israel hostage. The Assad regime in Syria was an Iranian ally serving as a transshipment hub for weapons and training from Tehran to Beirut.

In their detailed yet highly readable book, Bob and Kaufman provide a blow-by-blow account of how Israel almost single-handedly disarmed Iran and its “ring of fire” and literally cleared the way for U.S. bombers to degrade Iran’s nuclear threat by the summer of 2025.

According to Bob and Kaufman, Israel was caught unprepared on Oct. 7 because the national security establishment was so fixated on the greater threats posed by Iran and Hezbollah. Jerusalem underestimated Hamas, and it paid the price.

After briefly considering a preemptive blow against Hezbollah, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his war cabinet decided that a full-scale invasion of Gaza would be necessary to eliminate the threat from Hamas.

Bob and Kaufman are mostly critical of Israel’s strategy in Gaza, which they describe as achieving “mixed results at a great cost in human life and global opinion.” There was no off-the-shelf plan for a full-scale invasion of the enclave. Instead of clearing and holding territory, as the United States eventually learned to do in its counterinsurgency campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan, Israel cleared areas of Hamas fighters only to depart and allow Hamas to return and regroup.

Instead of invading all of Gaza simultaneously, Israel segmented its operations regionally and temporally, starting with Gaza City in the north and continuing south to Khan Younis and, eventually, Rafah. The segmented strategy meant the war dragged on, casualties mounted, and public opinion in the United States and the world turned against Israel.

Still, in the end, Bob and Kaufman credit Israel with decimating Hamas as an effective fighting force.

The most captivating part of their account, however, covers Israel activating secret war plans to eviscerate Hezbollah and Iran. Here, Bob and Kaufman describe the effort as “spectacular and decisive” with “shockingly little diplomatic cost.”

Hezbollah struck first, raining down daily rocket fire on Israel shortly after Oct. 7 and causing Netanyahu to clear inhabitants from their homes in Israel’s north. By summer 2024, Israel was ready to strike back with a trap that had started to be laid in the mid-2000s. Hezbollah’s longtime leader, Hassan Nasrallah, feared that cell phone communications were not secure and ordered his fighters to communicate through beepers and walkie-talkies instead. Through years of careful work, the Mossad set up front companies and secretly became the key supplier of Hezbollah’s communication devices.

Placing explosives in beepers made them bulky, so the Mossad’s front companies took out advertisements touting the beepers’ enhanced security and durability. The ruse was so successful that orders came pouring in from other eager customers from around the world. On Sept. 17, 2024, the beepers beeped their last. Hezbollah commanders removed the devices from their pockets only to have them explode in their faces, “wounding some 3,500 Hezbollah fighters in a matter of seconds.” The next day, Hezbollah walkie-talkies self-destructed, injuring another 500 fighters. What is less known is that Iranian officials used the same communication devices and more than 100 fighters in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps were also injured in the blasts. The effect on Hezbollah as an organization, however, was much broader as Hezbollah fighters, not knowing what might blow up next, trashed electronic devices and avoided communications.

Next, Israel targeted Hezbollah’s massive weapons caches. On Sept. 23, Israel hit an astounding 1,300 targets in a single day. The authors quote Netanyahu boasting, “In six hours, we wiped out most of the ballistic missile stockpiles Hezbollah has amassed.”

Four days later, Hezbollah’s leader was removed from the battlefield. Israel dropped 85 bombs on Nasrallah’s underground compound in Beirut, eliminating the person that Netanyahu called “the axis of the axis.” The strike was aided by targeting systems that Mossad operatives had planted inside the building a month earlier and by the distraction of Netanyahu’s speech at the United Nations General Assembly, timed to finish just before the strikes began.

Next came Israel’s invasion of Lebanon, in which the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) seized 85,000 military items and killed or wounded more than 10,000 Hezbollah fighters. By late November, Hezbollah was decimated and signed a one-sided cease-fire on Israel’s terms.

Netanyahu did not plan to overthrow President Bashar al-Assad in Syria, but Hezbollah’s demise left the Syrian dictator naked. Hezbollah propped up Assad in his long-running civil war, but with Hezbollah decimated, Ahmed al-Sharaa and his Turkish backers saw an opportunity. In an 11-day blitz, rebels stormed Damascus and toppled Assad on Dec. 8, 2024.

Amid the confusion in Damascus, Netanyahu saw an opportunity to neuter a longtime rival and the IDF sprang to action. Israel’s 480 strikes destroyed 70 percent of Syria’s most important military capabilities. Almost overnight, Syria was transformed from a potent pro-Iranian proxy to a weakened and staunchly anti-Iranian regime.

Of course, the font of all these other dangers was the biggest target of them all, the Islamic Republic of Iran.

Israel got in some early shots to disable Iran’s air and missile defenses following large-scale Iranian attacks in April and October 2024. The major action, however, occurred during the 12-day war in June 2025. The new Trump administration pursued negotiations over Iran’s nuclear program, and when talks deadlocked on June 12, Israel enforced U.S. President Donald Trump’s redline.

Starting on June 13 and continuing for nearly two weeks, Israel unleased 4,600 bombs on approximately 900 targets, decimating nuclear facilities and killing at least 30 top Iranian officials and nine nuclear scientists. Israel’s air force was supported by hundreds of Mossad agents and special forces soldiers on the ground inside Iran, launching swarms of drones on critical targets. As Bob and Kaufman explain, “with tens of millions of Iranians who detest the regime—Persian, Azeri, Kurdish, or otherwise—there is a nearly endless supply of Iranians for the Mossad to recruit.”

The big outstanding question was, would the United States help finish the job? It was not a foregone conclusion. Trump was sympathetic. Where U.S. President Joe Biden warned Netanyahu not to strike Iran’s nuclear program back in October 2024, then-candidate Trump asked, “Isn’t that what you’re supposed to hit?”

Others in the Trump administration, including U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance, were more skeptical. But Bob and Kaufman tell the captivating story of how Eyal Zamir, Israel’s chief of the general staff, briefed Vance on the generational opportunity for U.S. security. He told Vance, “We are already flying over Fordow freely. There is a straight-line aerial lane open.” The United States could waltz in, destroy Iran’s nuclear program, and depart with very little risk to U.S. forces. Vance was persuaded.

On June 21, Operation Midnight Hammer began—more than 125 planes, seven B-2 bombers,12 30,000-pound bunker-busting bombs. Zero U.S. casualties. Trump crowed the next morning: “Iran’s key nuclear enrichment facilities have been completely and totally obliterated.”

Did Israel drag the United States into this war? In Trump’s own words prior to the strikes, “like I’d go in unwillingly. … If we don’t make a deal, I’ll be leading the pack.”

The Biden administration repeatedly asked Israel to restrain itself to prevent escalation. Notably, when Iran’s first direct attack on Israel failed to cause much damage in April 2024, Biden urged Netanyahu not to retaliate and to “take the win.”

Jerusalem mostly ignored this advice and often only informed Washington of the next major escalation after the operation was already underway. Had Netanyahu followed Biden’s advice, Tehran’s ring of fire might still be standing, backed by nuclear arms.

But Biden eventually came around and claimed credit for Israel’s aggressive stance. In December 2024, he said, “Our approach has shifted the balance of power in the Middle East.”

Similarly, as late as May 2025, it was not at all clear that Trump was willing to employ U.S. military power to strike Iran’s nuclear program. It was only Israel’s effective military campaign against Iranian leadership, air defenses, and above-ground nuclear plants that convinced Washington to join the fray.

According to Bob and Kaufman, “Jerusalem needed to be daring enough to act first, so that the opportunity would be too good for President Trump to pass up.”

So, is Israel a good ally of the United States? To be sure, peace in the Middle East has not yet broken out. Iran is stubbornly resisting the United States’ naval blockade. Iranian-backed Houthis are targeting Saudi Arabia and shipping in the Red Sea. And Hamas, Hezbollah, and the Iranian nuclear program may one day be reconstituted.

But still, Iran is much further from achieving its nuclear ambitions today than it has been in more than 20 years. Hamas, Hezbollah, and Assad’s Syria—the ring of fire protecting Iran’s nuclear program—are no longer relevant factors in Middle Eastern security.

As Bob and Kaufman argue, “From these blows, the anti-Israel and anti-America proxy axis that Iran spent decades building might never recover.” For the sake of international peace and security, let’s hope they are right.

Anthropic’s planned IPO raises questions over whether it can maintain its safe and responsible AI priorities.

Anthropic wants to have it both ways. With its impending IPO, it wants to tap the vast riches of the public markets and insists it doesn’t want to compromise its core mission of developing AI safely and for the benefit of humanity. But greater model capabilities bring greater risks, and shareholders eager for returns may have little patience for costly safety measures that slow products on their way to market.

Sam Altman, the CEO of Anthropic’s archrival, OpenAI, has cited investor pressures as one reason the company is not pursuing an IPO this year. Anthropic hopes it can resist investor pressures with a mind-boggling governance structure.

A “unique and complicated governance structure that has not been used before…creatively designed for this founder-led and mission-driven company.”

University of Pennsylvania law professor Elizabeth Pollman told The Verge that Anthropic has proposed going public with a “unique and complicated governance structure that has not been used before” and that has been “creatively designed for this founder-led and mission-driven company.” Experts say it should give Anthropic freedom to pursue profits without letting shareholders force it to cut corners on safety or rush systems out before they’re adequately tested. But even the strongest legal protections can only go so far, and no corporate structure, no matter how well designed, can completely insulate a public company from the demands of the market.

Foremost, Anthropic is a public benefit corporation. Simply put, it is legally required to focus on more than just maximizing shareholder returns, balancing these with its stated social mission and the interests of stakeholders, such as employees and specific communities. Patagonia and Kickstarter are among the better-known examples, with missions centered on environmental conservation and supporting creative communities, respectively.

Another key safeguard aims to protect Anthropic’s mission by limiting shareholder influence over one of the biggest levers of corporate power: the company’s board of directors. Directors oversee the company and appoint its leadership, including the CEO. Directors can also hold executive roles, as is the case with Anthropic, with CEO Dario Amodei and president Daniela Amodei both sitting on the board.

Shareholders typically have considerable sway over who sits on the board, giving them a means to replace directors or push for changes if they are unhappy with how the company is being run. For example, if shareholders are unhappy at what they perceive as unnecessary delays to market for optional safety testing, they may remove directors and replace them with ones more aligned with their interests, such as making money and being less cautious.

“Anthropic is entirely insulated from profit seeking pressures, in the sense that ordinary shareholders have no ability to select the board or make business decisions.”

Under Anthropic’s proposed governance structure, ordinary shareholders won’t have much of a say over who is on the board at all. Instead, four of the board’s seven directors will be chosen by a special body called the long-term benefit trust, explains Jens Dammann, a professor in business and commercial law at the University of Texas School of Law. It is a highly unusual setup for a major technology company.

The trust is made up of “five financially disinterested voting trustees” with expertise in “AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.” Through a special class of share called Class T stock, the trustees can appoint or remove directors in pursuit of their stated purpose: ensuring “that Anthropic responsibly balances the financial interests of stockholders with the interests of those affected by Anthropic’s conduct and our public benefit purpose.” The trust is designed to operate independently of the company’s commercial pressures, allowing it, in theory, to put Anthropic’s mission first.

Ordinary shareholders technically have a say in the board’s three remaining seats. However, Anthropic has created another safeguard that keeps power firmly in the hands of its founders, called “Founder LLC,” which will control 50.1 percent of the voting power on shareholder matters. Given that many shareholder votes are decided by a simple majority, Founder LLC basically gives Anthropic’s founders the ability to block or force through decisions, regardless of how other shareholders vote. This means that they will effectively choose the remaining three directors, Columbia Law School professor Dorothy Lund told The Verge.

The prospectus says Founder LLC will “initially” be seven of Anthropic’s founders: Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, and Christopher Olah. “Our seven co-founders will initially be the sole members.” Notably absent is another cofounder, Ben Mann, who co-leads the company’s experimental Anthropic Labs arm, as reported by The San Francisco Standard.

All in all, the arrangements would give Anthropic’s founders and the trust “tremendous power” over the company’s direction, shielding it from many of “the pressures associated with being a public company,” Dammann told The Verge. “Given the unusual governance structure Anthropic has chosen, I don’t see how going public, by itself, would undermine its ability to prioritize safety over commercial interests.”

Ann Lipton, a professor at the University of Colorado Law School, agreed, telling The Verge that, legally speaking, “Anthropic is entirely insulated from profit seeking pressures, in the sense that ordinary shareholders have no ability to select the board or make business decisions.” But Lipton cautioned that the founders themselves have financial stakes in the company, which could influence their decisions.

“Investors may not mind their lack of voice as long as things go well. But if things go sour, they may become quite frustrated.”

And there’s a big difference between legal protections and immunity from market pressures. Anthropic, like all AI companies, “desperately needs capital,” Lipton said. “It’s not clear to me that any governance structure will protect against the market reality that unless Anthropic takes advantage of profit making opportunities at a faster pace than its competitors, it will not be able to fund its operations.” Lund expressed similar concerns. Anthropic will still “need to stay in the good graces of their investors,” she said, adding that “competitive pressure and capital needs can erode safety commitments over time.” Anthropic did not respond to The Verge’s request for comment on whether it felt the company could actually resist shareholder pressure to massively accelerate AI at the cost of safety.

Another potential vulnerability emerges if Anthropic’s founders start leaving. In its prospectus, the company says its success is “highly dependent on the continued service” of its senior team, particularly its founders. Dario and Daniela are singled out as being especially “critical” to advancing the company’s research and mission. But beyond the loss of leadership, their departure could trigger significant changes in how the company is structured, potentially stripping away some of the safeguards preventing Anthropic from pursuing profit over safety.

Under the proposed structure, Founder LLC would “sunset” if a majority of its members leave or the group chooses to dissolve it. Once that happens, ordinary shareholders could gain the power to weaken the trust’s protections, even against the wishes of the trustees, and without the support of the founders or employees, who hold different classes of stock, Dammann told The Verge. Other means of subverting the trust also exist, he explained, including coordinated action by the public shareholders, employee stockholders, and founders.

For now, it seems, Anthropic can probably have it both ways. But the protections designed to preserve its commitment to AI safety are far from ironclad, and much depends on market pressures, whether its founders stick around, and directions of the trust. Dammann said he sees a “fundamental dilemma that cannot be resolved in a fully satisfactory way” between Anthropic’s mission and investor demands. As long as things are going well, this tension may be manageable. “Investors may not mind their lack of voice as long as things go well,” Dammann said. “But if things go sour, they may become quite frustrated.”

#3405. Ghostty 1.4.0

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Release notes for Ghostty 1.4.0, released on October 27, 2026.

Ghostty 1.4.0 is a major release that includes large performance and memory improvements, a 100% complete Kitty graphics implementation including animation and relative placements (the first non-Kitty terminal to do so), SSH integration improvements, and hundreds more improvements and fixes across all platforms.

This release features 7 months of work with changes from 172 contributors over 2,889 commits. Thank you to all the contributors, maintainers, community moderators, translators, packagers, and users who each helped make this release possible.

These release notes are painstakingly and lovingly hand-crafted by human Ghostty maintainers, including the full changelog. They take a combined 16+ hours to write, so please enjoy. 🥰

Note: I requested CVEs for the above advisories weeks ago, but GitHub hasn't assigned them yet. If/when they're assigned, I will update the above with proper CVE numbers.

PRs: #12999 #13069 #13393 #14413 #14414

Ghostty 1.4 supports macOS 27 (Golden Gate). Most of the work was compatibility fixes:

Ghostty 1.4 remains fully compatible with prior macOS versions back to and including macOS 13 (Ventura). See the system requirements for more details.

Ghostty 1.3.1 works on macOS 27, but it has some of the visual issues listed above, such as the gray tab bar.

PRs: #13943 #13939 #13935 #13934 #13893 #13933 #13389 #13270 #13281

Ghostty now supports Kitty graphics animation and relative placements. These were the last major features of the Kitty graphics protocol that Ghostty didn't support.

Programs that display animated images with the Kitty protocol, such as an animated GIF shown with kitten icat, now animate in Ghostty instead of showing a single still frame. Frame transmission, composition, and playback control are all supported, and animations pause while the terminal isn't visible. Relative placements let a program position one image relative to another image's placement, so the child image moves along with its parent.

We also did a full conformance pass against the specification and Kitty itself, fixing dozens of behavioral differences. For example, images now scroll with text inside scroll regions (#4323 ), retransmitting an image ID replaces the old image (#6711 ), and invalid image data now gets an error response instead of silence, which could leave clients hanging. The full list is in the terminal capabilities section.

Kitty graphics are faster, too. Image payloads are now parsed in bulk with SIMD instead of one byte at a time, making large image transfers about 16x faster to parse in our benchmarks (64 MiB of image data, x86_64 Linux), and drawing images costs much less per frame.

Our Kitty graphics implementation now covers 100% of the published specification, making Ghostty (and libghostty) the first terminal to do this outside of Kitty itself.

PRs: #13264 #13473 #13240 #13245 #14130 #14137 #14138 #14017

Ghostty now transparently compresses scrollback that isn't visible, reducing the physical memory those pages use by 70% to 90% for typical text-heavy history. Compression only runs while the terminal is idle and pages are decompressed on demand when you scroll back or search, so it has no measurable impact on I/O throughput.

Thanks to compression, the default scrollback limit has been raised from 10 MB to 50 MB per terminal. On platforms that support compression, a full scrollback still generally uses less physical memory than the old 10 MB limit did.

Compression lowers resident (physical) memory, not virtual memory, so look at resident memory when measuring it. It is currently available on macOS and 64-bit Linux.

PRs: #13209 #13220 #13226 #13227 #13537 #13827 #13587 #14011 #13722 #14035 #12760

There are performance improvements across the board in Ghostty 1.4 that yield significant gains compared to prior Ghostty versions.

The biggest win came from a surprising discovery: on macOS, the kernel caps every read from a pty at about 1 KiB no matter how large the read buffer is. Previously, Ghostty processed each read as it arrived, so per-read overhead was paid every kilobyte, and the program writing output sat blocked while we parsed. Ghostty now drains the pty on one thread while parsing on another, raising end-to-end I/O throughput by 25% to 55% on macOS (M4 Max, plain and Unicode text) over Ghostty 1.3.x.

VT processing itself is also much faster. In parser benchmarks, plain ASCII text went from 128 MB/s to 725 MB/s (5.7x), CJK text got 3.4x faster, and a 2.6 GB dataset of real-world terminal recordings from asciinema is processed about 25% faster. Together, these changes took cat-ing a 150 MB text file on macOS from 1.5 seconds to 566 milliseconds.

The GTK app has a new rendering pipeline. It no longer uses GtkGLArea. Instead, the renderer draws with EGL on its own thread, exports each frame as a DMABUF, and hands it to GTK to display. Rendering is now triple-buffered, which should greatly reduce tearing, and no OpenGL calls run on the main thread anymore, which let us remove a number of workarounds.

The most visible result is crisp text under fractional scaling. Text and box-drawing characters are no longer blurry with fractional display scaling such as 125% or 150% (#1938 ). We fixed this once before, but it came back after upstream GTK changes. Ghostty now renders at the window's true fractional scale, so every rendered pixel maps to exactly one pixel on screen.

The new render surface needs a system EGL library (see Changes for Package Maintainers). On GPU drivers that can't export DMABUFs, Ghostty falls back to copying frames through the CPU, which is slower but works.

PRs: #13962 #13993 #13995 #13998 #14002 #14061

Ghostty now implements the Kitty clipboard protocol (OSC 5522) on both macOS and GTK. Ghostty 1.3 could parse the protocol but didn't implement it.

OSC 52 can only read and write plain text. The Kitty clipboard protocol lets programs running in the terminal read and write any kind of clipboard data, such as images or HTML, and lets them pick among the formats on the clipboard. Programs can also enable paste events (mode 5522), which tell the program what formats a paste contains so it can read the one it wants. This is how a terminal program can accept a pasted image, for example.

Clipboard access follows the existing clipboard-read and clipboard-write configurations. By default, reads prompt for permission and writes are allowed. The permission prompt shows a preview of the clipboard contents, including images. Programs that send a password can have your answer remembered for the rest of that terminal session. A new clipboard-write-limit-bytes configuration limits the size of a single write (default 64 MiB). A write over the limit fails and leaves the clipboard untouched.

You can now rearrange splits on GTK by dragging them, matching the macOS feature introduced in Ghostty 1.3. When a tab has two or more splits, each split shows a drag handle at the top. Grab the handle and drop the split onto any other split, including splits in other tabs and windows. An overlay shows which side of the target split it will land on. The terminal contents, working directory, and running processes all move with it.

A new drag-handle configuration controls when drag handles are shown on both macOS and GTK: auto (default), always, or never. With never, splits can't be rearranged with the mouse. Hiding the handles was a common request from users who found them in the way.

On GTK, splits can only be dropped onto other splits. Dragging a split out to create a new tab or window is macOS-only.

PRs: #14454 #13494 #14483 #14538 #14673 #14375 #14144

Ghostty 1.4 includes dozens of improvements to core terminal emulation. We now run esctest, Thomas Dickey's terminal conformance suite, against Ghostty's terminal core in CI. Alongside that, we fixed many differences from xterm in cursor movement within margins, tab stops, character sets, mode reports, and more. You can find the full list of related changes in the terminal capabilities section.

Ghostty also supports several new sequences, including soft terminal reset (DECSTR), extended cursor position reports (DECXCPR), backarrow key mode (DECBKM), and screen size reports in characters (CSI 19 t). Programs can also now ask whether the terminal is visible using visibility reports (mode 2033), so they can stop rendering while hidden.

Keyboard encoding also matches xterm more closely, with fixes for modifyOtherKeys mode 2 and legacy Alt-as-Escape encoding of non-ASCII keys.

Ghostty 1.2 introduced SSH shell integration features as a work in progress. In Ghostty 1.4, they're backed by a new ghostty +ssh CLI action: a drop-in ssh wrapper that prepares the remote session for Ghostty and then runs the real ssh with your arguments forwarded unchanged.

Before connecting, +ssh installs Ghostty's terminfo on the remote host so the proper xterm-ghostty TERM value can be used, and forwards COLORTERM, TERM_PROGRAM, and TERM_PROGRAM_VERSION. If the terminfo install fails (for example, tic isn't available on the remote), it falls back to TERM=xterm-256color. Flags such as --terminfo=false, --forward-env=false, and --verbose control each step.

The ssh-env and ssh-terminfo shell integration features (still opt-in) now call +ssh too. Previously, each shell integration carried its own copy of this logic, which made up roughly a third of our shell integration scripts. Now bash, zsh, fish, elvish, and Nushell all behave identically, and you can get the same behavior from scripts or anywhere else by calling ghostty +ssh directly.

The terminfo install itself is also more reliable. Ghostty now always installs its own bundled terminfo rather than accepting whatever xterm-ghostty entry the remote already has, and it reinstalls automatically when a Ghostty update changes the terminfo. It also no longer needs infocmp or Ghostty's terminfo on the local machine. Installs are cached per host and port, so two machines behind one address no longer share a cache entry. And ssh-env no longer silently overrides SetEnv entries in your ~/.ssh/config (#11518 ).

The +ssh-cache command that manages this cache now takes positional destinations (ghostty +ssh-cache user@example.com) and has a new --prune=30d flag. See the breaking changes if you script against +ssh-cache.

Over the past 6 months, the C API for libghostty-vt has grown from a parser and a few encoders into a complete terminal emulator API, adopted by dozens of free and commercial terminal emulators used by millions of people. It's hard to quantify exactly, but the number of people using a libghostty-based terminal is confidently several times the number of people using the Ghostty GUI.

libghostty-vt now builds for macOS, Linux, Windows (MSVC and GNU), FreeBSD, Android, iOS, and WebAssembly, and CI continuously verifies every one of these targets. The goal is for libghostty-vt to be generally usable everywhere, within reason, so if you want more target support, let us know!

It's also easier to integrate libghostty-vt into existing build systems. There's CMake support, Nix packaging, an xcframework, and a source tarball, in addition to the existing pkg-config file. libghostty-vt no longer needs libc++, and it can build freestanding.

The API is still marked as a work in progress and broke compatibility several times during this cycle, so expect more breaking changes before a tagged release. The API documentation and the Zig, C, C++, Swift, and WebAssembly examples (now over 30) are kept up to date with main.

Taking a team who finished in the bottom six and getting to 80 points, we just felt a real injustice at the end of it all.

“I understand that it’s not going to be great for me tomorrow, but I get it. At Hearts, we were hurting. Last season was almost like we were mourning. That might seem a bit exaggerated, but that’s how it felt.

“We were all clinging to each other during the first part of the close season. It was a time when we felt we should all be sticking together. I felt the guilt about the decision over whether I stayed or left.

“I had felt something similar a couple of weeks earlier with Lawrence. I was golfing with Graeme Jones (sporting director) and chewing the fat about the season, the plans for the next season and having a post-mortem of everything when we got the call that Lawrence wanted to go to Rangers.

“I remember how I felt then. I felt a bit let down and flat about the whole thing. For me to make a similar move a couple of weeks later, I understood that. I totally get it if the treatment I receive tomorrow is what it is. You could say that maybe I deserve it, but nothing will change how I feel about my time at Hearts.

“I think there was a real injustice and a grievance towards the end of the season, we felt we should have won the title. I wanted to be a manager who won a title outside the Old Firm. But for a few contentious decisions going against us, I think we would have won it.”

Only goalkeeper Beau Reus will be unfamiliar to McInnes on Wednesday night, with the Rangers boss revealing that he spoke to many of the Hearts summer signings in the weeks leading up to his departure.

“I think only the goalkeeper is a player I hadn’t signed or worked with,” continued McInnes. “Although I hadn’t worked directly with a lot of the new signings, they were players I had numerous Zoom calls and conversations with about bringing in.

“Even at the end of last season, during the first week of my holiday, I was speaking to Tom Renaud, Malachi [Fagan-Walcott], MJ Kamara and [Amadou] Ba-Sy. The work means you feel as though you have a wee in with those guys.

“Obviously, it’s now somebody else’s job at Hearts to bring those boys on. They’ve got their job to get on with, but they’ve shown enough encouragement this season to suggest they will be absolutely title contenders again. There’s no doubt about that in my eyes. They are such a well-run club. They’ve got a brilliant chairman in Callum [Paterson] and they are really well run and they have the influence of Jamestown, so I only expect them to get better.

One of McInnes’ former charges in Claudio Braga spoke highly of McInnes last weekend and the coach reciprocated the warm words for a striker who won player of the year last season.

“Clearly, Claudio is such a talented player and his influence on the team has been clear,” said McInnes. “You would like when you sign a player the type of person that they are, but he’s just a good soul from top to bottom. He’s a proper person.

“He’s somebody I really enjoyed working with, somebody I still keep in touch with and someone I would like to help going forward. Good things will happen for him. Hopefully we can keep him quiet tomorrow night, along with some of his teammates, but he should be proud of what he has done in such a short period at Hearts.”

Hearts never lost a home match under McInnes’ watch and they remain unbeaten at Tynecastle since May 2025. Rangers will require a near complete performance to come away with all three points, according to their boss.

“My concern is Rangers and for us to improve. It is up to us to improve enough to be successful and win trophies. There are still a lot of familiar faces there who I’ve worked with, but it’s about the game. It’s not going to be about anything else.

“I think every component comes into it. Sometimes you think you have time at a place like Tynecastle, but you actually don’t. You need to be on your game and ready for that, so that will be part of the message.

“Both teams have a lot of good players. Both sets of players can play at tempo and speed. They can press properly and move the ball quickly in possession. The speed on the pitch will be there and the environment off it will be there too, so it all lends itself to a cup-tie atmosphere. You’ve got to be ready for it.”
