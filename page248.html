Khvicha Kvaratskhelia will undergo surgery for the ruptured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury he suffered in Paris Saint-Germain's 4-2 win over Barcelona on Tuesday, the French champions have announced.

Kvaratskhelia went down heavily on his right leg following a challenge from Xavi Espart in the 59th minute of the match at the Parc des Princes, fears of a major injury immediately fueled as he had to be carried off the pitch. Though no timeline has been set for his return after an operation, which PSG confirmed will take place "in the coming days," such a major injury will fuel doubts as to whether Kvaratskhelia will play again this season.

ACL injuries may not often be the career enders they once were but a rupture to the knee ligament remains one of the most significant a player can face, usually incurring an absence of at least nine months. It could take even longer before Kvaratskhelia is back to the brilliant form that helped propel PSG to two straight Champions League titles, the Georgian being named player of the tournament for the latter of those wins and also earning a nomination for the Ballon d'Or.

PSG will also be without forward Ousmane Dembele and center back Marquinhos for a "few weeks" after the injuries they suffered in an attritional win over Barcelona. It is, however, Kvaratskhelia's absence that will be giving Luis Enrique sleepless nights.

It is not, however, the case that PSG want for options in attack. This summer saw the Parisians make significant changes to their attacking depth, bringing in combined fees in excess of $350 million for the sales of Goncalo Ramos, Lee Kang-in, Randal Kolo Muani, Bradley Barcola and Ibrahim Mbaye. In their place came the triumvirate of Maghnes Akliouche, Ferran Torres and Mika Godts, each costing around $50 million.

Akliouche would seem the most likely to step in. After all, it was the former Monaco man who got the starting spot against Manchester City over Kvaratskhelia, whose form had faltered in the early weeks of the season. The 24-year-old has two goals and an assist so far in 11 appearances for his new club and has won praise from Luis Enrique for his versatility and ability to play across the frontline. The latter is a critical quality for PSG, whose starting front three of Kvaratskhelia, Dembele and Desire Doue interchange positions almost at will.

It is easy to see how Ferran Torres might be able to pick up that role too. Generally used as a center forward in his final years at Barcelona and so far at PSG, the 26 year old first broke through as a winger with Valencia and Manchester City. Torres has already started brightly with seven goals to his name for Les Parisiens. The same cannot be said for Godts, whose only start of the season so far came against Le Mans earlier this month.

So PSG do not lack for options even if none of them are quite at Kvaratskhelia's level. The trouble for Luis Enrique might be how many more minutes he has to spread around. A driving force in the back-to-back triumphs has been the opportunity to rest key players in Ligue 1 games, ensuring they are fresh for the critical Champions League games at the back end of the season. With doubts as to whether Kvaratskhelia can make them and Dembele clearly requiring careful management throughout the season, it looks tougher than ever for PSG to keep the rest of their attack fresh and firing.

#3574. Chrome warns you about suspicious websites now – how to turn on Google’s new scam protection

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google’s browser will flash two types of warnings based on whether the site is deemed dangerous or just suspicious.

Scammers are alive and well on the web, and avoiding their tricks seems like a never-ending battle. Now, Google has beefed up the security in Chrome to further protect you from becoming a victim of online scams.

Also: 5 ways to improve your Chrome browser’s security

In a blog post published Tuesday, Google outlined three new measures designed to help Chrome users browse the web more safely and securely. The new measures apply to Chrome both on computers and mobile devices.

First up is protection against suspicious websites. Chrome has long warned you about dangerous sites that are known to be malicious. Now, the browser will also notify you if a site is deemed suspicious. The warning that appears on the screen may specifically tell you that the site doesn’t seem right, as it’s similar to other sites associated with phishing, malware, and scams. You can click a Learn More link to delve further.

If you’re concerned about the site and want to follow Google’s advice, just close it or click the Return to safety button to go back to the previous page. If you’re certain the site is safe, you can mark it as safe and use it normally.

Google rates a website as suspicious by comparing it with other deceptive or misleading sites. Gemini also plays a role here, as the AI analyzes the site’s content, the brand it represents, the site’s intent, the domain’s reputation, and other factors to derive a scam likelihood score.

With this update, Chrome can now distinguish between suspicious sites and dangerous sites by flashing different warnings. The alert for a dangerous site paints the screen red and strongly recommends that you leave the page. The alert for a suspicious site is more subtle, giving you the option to still visit and use the site if you’re certain it’s legit.

“Suspicious site warnings notify users when a page exhibits deceptive patterns commonly associated with known malicious sites,” Google said in its blog post. “These new warnings are distinguished by format, iconography, color, and text, to make it easy for users to quickly and confidently make the best choice for themselves based on the context. By tailoring the warning UI in this way, we are able to safely expand our coverage to display a higher volume of protective warnings.”

Google has also beefed up Chrome’s overall scam-detection capabilities. Last year, the company added Gemini’s AI smarts to Chrome to thwart tech support scams. Here, the scammer behind a malicious site tries to convince you that you’ve been infected by malware, only to try to sell you security software or gain remote access to your computer.

Now, this same type of AI-powered security will alert you against a wider range of online scams, including fake antivirus and phony browser updates. For this, Google uses a server-side Gemini model with more advanced capabilities. The model analyzes the content of the page, the reputation of the domain, and other signals to reach a final conclusion.

Also: How Google’s AI combats new scam tactics

To protect your privacy, the company promises that the amount of data shared with the back-end server is minimized and is shared only when the site triggers high levels of scam signals. Any page content sent from your computer or mobile device isn’t logged, remembered, or used to train the AI, Google added.

ClickFix is a type of social engineering scam that tricks you into manually copying and running malicious commands on your own computer, ostensibly to fix a technical issue that doesn’t exist. Because you carry out the actual steps yourself, existing security defenses in Chrome and other products won’t catch or block the attack, at least until now.

Also: Inside Google’s faster Chrome patch strategy to block AI attacks

With the latest update, Chrome will now detect if you’re tricked into copying suspicious commands. After you copy them but before you have a chance to paste them, the clipboard is wiped of any personal data. Chrome then analyzes that data and displays a warning if the activity is deemed dangerous.

To take advantage of the new security defenses in Chrome, you need to be running the browser in Enhanced Protection mode. This is the strongest level available in the program’s Safe Browsing process. To enable this in Chrome on the desktop or mobile, head to Settings and select Privacy and security. On the desktop, click the option for Security; on mobile, tap the option for Safe Browsing. Then choose the setting for Enhanced Protection.

You should also turn on Enhanced protection for your Google account. For this, sign in to your Google account page on the web. Select the option for Security & sign-in. Scroll down the page and select the option for Enhanced Safe Browsing for your account. Then just turn on the switch.

“As scammers find new ways to take advantage of people, Chrome and Safe Browsing will continue to develop smarter, real-time defenses to make the web safer for everyone,” Google said in its post. “To make sure you’re the first to get the latest protections against phishing, scams, social engineering, and malware, enable Enhanced Protection on Chrome and your Google Account to help you stay one step ahead of attackers.”

#801. A train was delayed by a solar storm. But something didn't add up.

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A mysterious train delay nearly 180 years ago has led scientists to uncover a surprising mistake in the history of space weather. An international research team has solved a 178-year-old mystery involving one of the earliest documented cases of solar activity interfering with electrical technology.

The incident dates back to the 1840s, when electric telegraph networks were beginning to transform long-distance communication. As these early systems expanded, operators occasionally encountered strange electrical disturbances that could disrupt their equipment. Scientists now understand that some of these problems were caused by changes in Earth's magnetic field triggered by activity on the Sun.

Researchers led by Lancaster University have revisited a historical account of a train delayed in Exeter, England, when solar activity interfered with the railway's telegraph system. The investigation involved scientists from RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University, and the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space.

By piecing together historical documents and scientific observations, the team uncovered an error that changes the timeline of the earliest known technological disruptions caused by space weather.

How Solar Storms First Disrupted Technology

One of history's most dramatic examples of space weather affecting technology occurred in 1859. Known as the Carrington Event, the powerful solar storm involved a massive solar flare and a coronal mass ejection, an enormous eruption of charged particles and magnetic fields from the Sun.

The resulting disturbances produced spectacular auroras, commonly known as the northern lights, that appeared as far south as Hawaii and Central America. In some locations, the sky glowed brightly enough for people to read newspapers at night. The unusual illumination even fooled gold miners into thinking morning had arrived, prompting them to prepare breakfast shortly after midnight.

The storm also wreaked havoc on telegraph networks around the world. Electrical surges caused equipment failures, sent sparks flying from wires, shocked operators, and even started fires. In some cases, the electrical currents generated by the disturbance were so powerful that telegraph systems continued operating without batteries.

Although the Carrington Event remains the most famous example from this period, evidence of solar activity interfering with electrical systems stretches back even further.

In 1871, an anonymous author published an account in the scientific journal Nature describing a "very intense magnetic disturbance" that supposedly occurred on October 18, 1841. According to the report, the disturbance interfered with railway signals in Exeter and delayed the scheduled 10:05 p.m. train departure by 16 minutes.

If the date had been correct, the event would have represented the earliest known instance of space weather disrupting human technology.

However, a new investigation published in the American Geophysical Union journal Space Weather has revealed that the original account contained a significant historical error.

The Railway Mystery That Changed Solar Storm History

Associate Professor Brett Carter, a space weather researcher at RMIT University, helped investigate the historical records. During the investigation, the team noticed a problem that immediately called the original timeline into question.

The Exeter-to-Starcross railway line mentioned in the report did not open until 1846, almost five years after the event supposedly occurred.

"In this study, we effectively investigated what had to be a typo in a Nature paper from 1871. We know it was a typo because the Exeter-to-Starcross train line mentioned in that paper didn't exist until 1846, which is 5 years afterwards," he said.

To establish when the train delay actually happened, the researchers examined a wide range of historical evidence. They compared railway timetables and newspaper reports with observations of solar activity, accounts of auroras, and digitized measurements of Earth's magnetic field.

Together, these records pointed to a different date: October 18, 1848, exactly seven years later than originally reported.

The revised timeline resolves the apparent contradiction and places the incident during an important period in technological history, when electrical communication systems were becoming increasingly widespread.

Carter explained that the 1840s marked a turning point in humanity's relationship with space weather. Solar disturbances had always occurred, but the emergence of electrical technology meant that their effects could now interfere directly with everyday human activities.

"Understanding this historical event is important because the 1840s mark the intersection between the rise of our technological age and space weather, which has always been around," Carter says.

"Since this line was crossed, humans have not looked back."

The findings also change the incident's place in the historical record. Although the Exeter disruption remains one of the earliest credible examples of space weather affecting technology, it can no longer be considered the first.

That distinction now belongs to an earlier documented incident involving telegraph interference on Britain's Midland Railway in March 1847.

Professor Jim Wild of Lancaster University, who led the study, said the findings demonstrate that the dangers associated with space weather began affecting society long before the development of modern power grids, satellites, and electronic communication networks.

"What this research highlights is that space weather is not a new threat but a long-standing natural hazard. Society has been experiencing the effects of space weather on technology for almost as long as electrical technologies have existed," Wild said.

The Exeter incident is especially significant because it occurred when electrical technology was still in its infancy. Early railway operators were beginning to rely on telegraph systems, creating new opportunities for solar disturbances to interfere with essential infrastructure.

"The Exeter train delay is a fascinating story because it sits right at the point where emerging technologies first began to encounter the realities of the space environment. By combining historical archives with scientific observations, we've been able to show that the event almost certainly happened in 1848 rather than 1841.

"Although this means it is not the earliest recorded space weather impact, it remains one of the first clear examples of solar activity disrupting critical infrastructure," he said.

The investigation also shows how historical scientific records can be used alongside newspapers and other archival materials to reconstruct events that occurred long before modern monitoring instruments became available.

"It also demonstrates the value of combining scientific records with contemporary newspaper reports and archival documents when reconstructing historic space weather events."

Dr. Mike Hapgood, a Visiting Scientist and space weather expert at the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space, described the research process as something resembling a detective investigation.

"It highlights the importance of preserving these older records, which give us the evidence base we need to interpret past events and strengthen future predictions," he said.

Modern Technology Still Faces the Threat of Solar Storms

Almost two centuries after the Exeter train delay, solar storms continue to pose risks to railways and other essential infrastructure. The technologies involved have changed dramatically, but they remain susceptible to disturbances originating from the Sun.

Today, space weather can interfere with electrical power systems, railway signaling equipment, satellite navigation, and communication networks. Powerful solar events can disturb Earth's magnetic field, potentially generating unwanted electrical currents in infrastructure and affecting systems that depend on reliable satellite signals.

Scientists now have far more sophisticated instruments and forecasting methods than were available in the 19th century. However, Hapgood emphasized that modern society's growing dependence on interconnected electrical and space technologies also creates new vulnerabilities.

Understanding how past solar storms affected infrastructure can help researchers assess the dangers posed by future events and develop better ways to reduce potential disruptions.

"Deepening our understanding of these events is essential for preparing for, and mitigating, the impacts of space weather - especially as we look forward to a decade of exciting space developments that will face the challenge of a new solar cycle in the 2030s," he said.

#4586. RFK Jr.’s Revised Vaccine Schedule May Cause 83% Surge in Hepatitis B Infections

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s proposed changes to the childhood vaccine schedule have raised alarm among public health experts and researchers.

Concerns include a rise in preventable infectious diseases such as measles, polio, and hepatitis B.

A new study suggests that delaying or foregoing the hepatitis B shot for newborns could increase chronic infections among children.

In December 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) ended its recommendation for universal hepatitis B vaccination at birth for infants whose mothers test negative for the virus. Instead, the federal health agency recommends individual-based clinical decision making.

After reviewing the evidence, the CDC, which is overseen by the HHS, concluded that newborns “face little risk of contracting hepatitis,” which deemed the vaccine, typically administered at birth, unnecessary.

Experts say this shift in public health policy wasn’t based on science, and researchers warn of rising infection rates.

“Hepatitis B immunizations have been proven to protect individuals against a serious and significant disease,” said Graham Tse, MD, pediatrician and chief medical officer of MemorialCare Miller Children’s & Women’s Hospital in Long Beach, CA.

“The hepatitis B vaccines have several decades of history and have been proven safe and effective,” Tse said. “There has been no new data or evidence presented or uncovered to support delaying the newborn dose of hepatitis B.”

Here’s what parents should know about making informed decisions with their child’s healthcare team.

A U.S. modeling study published in Annals of Internal Medicine on October 20 projected that delaying the first vaccine dose until 2 months could increase chronic hepatitis B infections among infants by 19% under one scenario.

With shared decision making and lower vaccine uptake, projected increases ranged from 83% to 225%, compared with universal birth-dose vaccination.

The researchers also estimated that up to 1.8 million young adults could remain susceptible to hepatitis B by age 20 under shared decision making, compared with 232,000 under universal birth dosing.

Another study published in JAMA Pediatrics in late April projected hundreds of additional chronic infections annually if the hepatitis B birth dose were delayed or omitted.

However, these figures are model-based projections, rather than verified outcomes. Actual results will depend on vaccination rates and other factors.

In December 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) adopted a recommendation shifting away from universal hepatitis B vaccination at birth for infants whose mothers test negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).

Under the new approach, parents and healthcare professionals can decide together whether to give the first dose at birth or defer it until the baby is 2 months old.

The change followed an 8 to 3 vote by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), marking a departure from longstanding U.S. guidance.

However, infants born to mothers who test positive for hepatitis B or whose infection status is unknown should still receive the hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) within 12 hours of birth, according to the updated guidelines.

Hepatitis B is a viral infection that affects the liver.

Although some infections resolve, newborns and infants face a higher risk of developing lifelong hepatitis B infection compared with adults.

Up to 90% of infants infected during their first year develop chronic hepatitis B.

“We have actual world evidence that vaccinating newborns for hepatitis B prevents hepatitis B infections later in life and the serious complications of that disease,” Tse said.

Over time, chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, or liver cancer.

“The danger of delaying the newborn hepatitis B shot is that individuals do not get vaccinated and thereby do not have protection against hepatitis B,” Tse said.

Early vaccination helps protect young infants before potential exposure occurs, including situations in which maternal screening misses an infection.

“Newborns can be infected with hepatitis B from a mother who is not aware they have a hepatitis B infection or from exposure later in life,” Tse said.

“The risk is developing a hepatitis B infection, which can potentially lead to liver cancer, fulminant hepatitis, or severe end-stage liver disease.”

In contrast to the CDC, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) still recommends a universal hepatitis B vaccine dose within 24 hours of birth, followed by additional doses during infancy.

The leading pediatric medical organization has raised concerns about the potential consequences of reduced vaccination rates.

The hepatitis B vaccine has decades of safety and effectiveness data.

Tse said the AAP’s vaccine schedule and recommendations are based on decades of study, use, and expert input, and provide the best protection against vaccine-preventable diseases.

Parents who have questions about potential side effects, timing, or their baby’s individual risk should discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional.

“Parents are confused, given the recommendations from CDC, HHS, and Kennedy differ from established and respected groups such as the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Infectious Disease Society of America, and several states,” Tse said.

“Parents desire to keep their children safe, and having differing opinions and confusion will lead to reduced vaccination rates as parents experience doubt as to whom to listen to or believe.”

While the updated CDC policy offers more flexibility for some families, lower vaccination uptake could lead to an increase in chronic hepatitis B infections.

Ask your pediatrician about the benefits of vaccination against hepatitis B at birth, the risks of delaying the first dose, and the recommended schedule for completing the series.

You can also ask how maternal screening results affect your baby’s protection.

An informed conversation with your healthcare professional can help you weigh the evidence and choose a vaccination plan for your baby.

“I see no argument whatsoever for changing the established vaccination schedule for hepatitis B,” Tse said.

“There have been no new studies, no data, nor evidence to justify Kennedy’s proposed changes to the childhood immunization schedule. Delaying vaccinations or skipping vaccinations puts your child at risk for serious and significant diseases, which are all preventable,” Tse said.

However, he was also quick to make clear that he won't be disappearing from game development, announcing plans to form a new studio that he's now offered a bit more insight on.

"I’ll be back at it again soon and will share more about my plans for a new studio," Sundberg wrote as part of his LinkedIn post about Liquid Swords' impending closure. Yet its use of cover mechanics, unit customisation, and onscreen violence felt distinctly new compared to other games I was playing at the time. As someone who'd grown up on Age of Empires and Command & Conquer, seeing units pair off and take part in animated scuffles brought the battlefield to life, especially after spending years of Saturday afternoons sliding static Warhammer figurines around a tabletop.

Then, in Dawn of War 2, Relic dropped traditional basebuilding and pushed the camera in to focus the action on commanding just a few squads. It upended the tried and tested mechanics of the genre I was used to. The hard unit cap meant it simply wasn't possible to assemble a vast army with which to overwhelm the enemy. The only way to succeed was to use each differently-specced squad as it was designed: heavy bolters supplying cover, assault marines launching into the fray to break up the enemy's defensive positions, recon units circumventing the frontline to attack a vulnerable flank. It was a bold reinvention when they could have just offered more of the same.

Admittedly, I played much less of Dawn of War 3, but it too was responding and reacting to what was happening in the RTS genre at the time. Dota 2 and League of Legends were in their ascendency and, rather than ignore the former mods of RTS games, Relic tried to thread their DNA back into the Warhammer-y battlefield. Hero characters were beefed up and each unit gained special abilities to be used in combat. Armies were made both larger and weaker, meaning there was a much higher death toll in each mission. And base building returned, but cover was ejected, changing the dynamics we had got used to in the previous two games.

I run through all this history so you can see why Dawn of War 4 is both a good game and an unexciting one. From what I've played of the Orks and Adeptus Mechanicus campaigns, it can produce enjoyable afternoons of pushing virtual soldiers around a sci-fi battlefield, but all the while it feels too familiar to what came before it. King Art Games have made no secret that of the Dawn of War games, they've mostly closely been cribbing from the original. The battles aren't as large as Dawn of War 3, but the unit cap is significantly larger than Dawn of War 2, and both base building and cover make a return. As a result of this deference to the 2004 original, m first impression – and this is a preview rather than a review – is that Dawn of War 4 is a competent RTS that immediately feels dated.

I went back to the original Dawn of War to compare the two. Dawn of War 4 doesn't entirely miss the opportunity to expand: your units, for instance, have far more customisation options, allowing you to swap out their weapons and equip them with additional abilities. Plus, there are more tools available for grouping and selecting your units, making it a little easier to manage your forces. And, in all the ways you would expect, it looks like a more modern game, with sharper looking textures, more detailed models, and a camera that lets you peer even closer upon the violence. But while the original Dawn of War is fun to play today, it has decades-old issues that nonetheless crop up in Dawn of War 4 as well.

Despite your being able to group units, select all on screen, and select all units of single type, the larger your army grows, the less your ability to make meaningful tactical choices becomes. The unit toolbar, which Dawn of War 4 places at the bottom of your screen, fills with squashed unit portraits that eventually become indistinguishable. The Orks suffer from this more even than the Adeptus Mechanicus, as their roster is so cheap to recruit. With the growing inability to be selective, in mission after mission, I just grabbed as many units as I could and threw them at the enemies' defences. It was effective and I don't want to complain too much about the victories, but they weren't satisfying when attaining them involved so few of my own, meaningful choices. This was also the weakness of the original Dawn of War's design and why the move to smaller squads to focusing on squads was so effective in its sequel. The ability to roll over your enemy with a large army was removed entirely.

The Adeptus Mechanicus, at least, make a much better first impression. Unlike the Waaargh-obsessed boyz, it's hard to grab a lump of soldiers, chuck them at the enemy, and expect results. I was more inclined to play around the specialisations of certain units, like the Skitarii Rangers, excellent long-range anti-infantry snipers who - when plonked into cover - will whittle down any greenskins or Necrons that march on their position. Meanwhile, the Skitarii Vanguard, armed with radiation-emitting ammunition, can vapourise the more heavily-armoured troops. However, both Skitarii ranged units are truly awful in melee, so to keep them effective, I paired them with units of Sicarian Ruststalkers. These blade-armed cyborg assassins are brutal in close combat, and can hold back infantry while the Rangers and Vanguard finish them off. Combine and position your units well, and you'll scythe through enemy troops, but do it haphazardly and they'll crumble just as quickly.

The early missions in the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign introduce you to Potentia Delta-9, a Technoarcheologist exploring the surface of Kronus to learn about what stirs in its depths, and Skitarii Marshal Sek-IX-23, who is doing his best to put down the Ork uprising. It was the opening where the Adeptus Mechanicus were most enjoyable to command., the shape and smaller scale of the battlefields giving a lot more opportunities to embrace the tactics of managing their extreme strengths and weaknesses.

In one early defence mission, Potentia finds herself surrounded by newly-awoken Necron troops. (If you've seen Dawn of War 4's trailers or know the history of planet Kronus from the previous games, you won't be surprised to learn it's Necrons that are stirring from a decades-long slumber, like Sleeping Beauty if she were clad in self-repairing battle armour.) You must hold out against waves of the immortal robots with their fizzing green eyes and even fizzier laser cannons. It's a set up that pressures you into thoughtfully applying your resources, ensuring that each entrance to your base has a firm defensive line composed of mixed troops. Then, as the waves against you grow stronger, and the attacks come against you from different angles, you must relocate your forces to bloody the nose of the attackers. Complicating things is the appearance of trapped Adeptus Mechanicus units around the map; if you can break out from your base and rescue them, they'll bolster your army, but at the risk of depleting your defences.

It's a mission design I've seen before in many RTS games, which does undermine the enjoyment, but it hints at the tactical play that's missing in the Ork campaign. Sadly, the further I get into the Adeptus Mechanicus campaign, the larger the battles become, with fewer opportunities to focus on unit-specific tactics. The result is that these too are won by treating your forces as a bolter-armed blob that just rolls over the enemy. It doesn't matter whether you're fighting Necrons or Orks, they all fall down before the bolter-blob.

As I say, I may have set my expectations for a new Dawn of War game too high. Over the past year, I've spoken to the team about their attention to detail in capturing units in the Adeptus Mechanicus and Necron armies, like the perpetual motion machine walkers that can't stand still, or the mega unit you can summon who makes no secret of the fact they hate you. Those conversations only stoked my hopes, which also might point to why I'm feeling a little deflated after what I've played.

I will still be playing through the full four campaigns when I get the opportunity (the preview build is limited to two of the four factions and I'm only able to talk about the opening six missions in each of those stories). And one change I will be making will be to whack the difficulty up. If I can make whomping the enemy harder, perhaps it will push me into thinking more tactically.

Still, while that may make Dawn of War 4 more enjoyable, it won't hide the fact this feels like a missed opportunity in a series that has marked itself by its reinvention, especially as it's recreated weaknesses of the original game which were addressed in its sequels.

#4843. Distant space ‘hamburger’ hides a baby gas giant

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 21, 2026 12:04 PM EDT

A cosmic Quarter-Pounder is providing us with a near-perfect view of a distant gas giant’s earliest moments. Known as Gomez’s Hamburger (or GoHam for short), the massive protoplanetary disk surrounds a young star located in the Scorpius-Centaurus stellar region of our home galaxy about 453 light-years away from Earth. As researchers explained in a study recently published in The Astrophysical Journal, GoHam’s vantage from Earth is virtually unparalleled for anyone hoping to glimpse the subtle layers of dust and particles that will one day coalesce into a humongous gas giant.

Early observers first spotted GoHam in 1985, but initially pegged it for a planetary nebula as far as 6,500 light-years from Earth. Astronomers have since revised their assessments with data gathered by the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in northern Chile. As its full name implies, ALMA measures millimeter-and-smaller wavelengths, allowing scientists to map the miniscule dust grains and gaseous molecules that compose distant objects. GoHam is a particularly unique subject for ALMA, because its position makes it a new benchmark example for testing models of how planets evolve.

“GoHam gives us a rare and clear view of the vertical and radial structure of a very large, nearly edge‑on disk,” explained study co-author and University of Minnesota astronomer Charles Law.

According to Law, its mixture of cosmic winds, large formational asymmetries, massive disk size, as well as the potential for planetary formation makes GoHam a “perfect laboratory.” With ALMA, Law and his colleagues analyzed GoHam’s layers of carbon monoxide, and sulfur-bearing molecules. As predicted, the lightest gas is in the highest layer above the formation’s midplane, while the heavier sulfur components lay beneath it. Meanwhile, a thin but concentrated section of millimeter dust is visible in the midplane itself.

Comparisons to GoHam’s fast food namesake only go so far, however. Its dust clouds lean north–south, with one side containing brighter and wider emissions. This likely stems from a disturbance such as a cosmic vortex, which may capture additional material for planetary formation. Dim, thin carbon monoxide emissions along the disk’s northern section also suggest GoHam’s central star is blowing gases into deep space as what’s known as photoevaporative wind.

Astronomers also noted that GoHam is one of the largest protoplanetary disks ever observed. At nearly 93 billion miles wide and with an exponentially larger dust mass than the typical protoplanet disk, GoHam may ultimately host not just one gas giant, but an entire planetary system.

#817. Astronauts’ hearts just passed a surprising test for Mars

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A journey to Mars may be less challenging for the human heart than scientists once feared. A new study suggests that astronauts can adapt remarkably well to months of weightlessness, offering encouraging evidence that humans could endure the long trip to the Red Planet and function under its weaker gravity.

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston monitored the hearts of 13 astronauts who spent approximately six months aboard the International Space Station. Their findings showed that although spaceflight initially affected heart performance, the astronauts gradually adapted, with intensive daily exercise playing an important role in maintaining their cardiovascular health.

One of NASA's major concerns about sending humans to Mars is how astronauts' hearts and circulatory systems will respond when they leave the weightless conditions of spaceflight (known as microgravity) and encounter Martian gravity, which is only about one-third as strong as Earth's.

Encouragingly, the researchers found that the astronauts showed no significant signs of cardiovascular strain when exposed to simulated Martian gravity after returning to Earth.

"With our findings, there is now reason to think that we would be able to traverse to Mars, get there and actually work on the surface of Mars," said study senior author Dr. James Thomas, professor of medicine in the division of cardiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine.

The study, published last month in the journal Circulation, is the first to investigate how the heart responds to simulated Martian gravity following an extended period in space.

How Daily Exercise Protects Astronauts' Hearts in Space

Living without gravity creates unusual challenges for the human body. On Earth, the heart constantly works against gravity to circulate blood, while muscles must support the body's weight. In space, those demands decrease substantially, allowing muscles and even the heart itself to shrink over time.

Returning to a gravitational environment can also be difficult. Astronauts on previous missions have frequently experienced dizziness or lightheadedness when standing after landing. This happens because gravity pulls blood toward the lower body, sometimes causing blood pressure to drop suddenly. The condition is called orthostatic hypotension. (If you've seen Sandra Bullock crawling after returning to Earth in "Gravity," you get the idea.)

However, the astronauts involved in the new study generally handled this transition well, potentially because they had maintained demanding exercise schedules throughout their missions.

As part of NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program, astronauts exercised for up to two hours each day. Their routines included 30 to 45 minutes of endurance training, which strengthens the cardiovascular system, along with 60 to 75 minutes of resistance training to help preserve muscle mass and strength.

"You lose muscle mass and cardiac mass when you're in a weightless environment because you are working less up there," explained Thomas, who also is a Northwestern Medicine cardiologist. "You're floating around in the air, and you give a little tap with your foot, and you can sail over to say hello to your buddy."

"To counter that, exercise is crucial when you're in space," Thomas added.

Heart Scans Reveal How the Body Adapts to Weightlessness

To understand what happens to the heart during prolonged spaceflight, the researchers collected echocardiograms before, during, and after each astronaut's mission. These ultrasound scans use sound waves to produce moving images of the heart, allowing doctors to examine its structure and measure how effectively it pumps blood.

While aboard the International Space Station, astronauts were trained to perform these scans on one another every few weeks. The recordings were transmitted to Earth, where researchers could monitor changes in heart function throughout the missions.

Thomas worked alongside Dr. Benjamin Levine of UT Southwestern Medical Center and Dr. Michael Bungo of the McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

"It would be typically about 3 a.m. for me because the space station runs on Greenwich Mean Time," Thomas said. "I would watch the recordings come in live and coach the astronauts in real time to improve the images."

The scans revealed that the astronauts' hearts initially struggled to adjust to their new environment. During the first two weeks in space, researchers observed declines in cardiac filling, stroke volume, and several other measures of cardiovascular performance.

Cardiac filling refers to how much blood enters the heart between beats, while stroke volume describes the amount of blood pumped out with each contraction. Both measurements decreased early in the missions, indicating that the astronauts' hearts were temporarily moving less blood with each beat.

As the astronauts spent more time in space, their cardiovascular systems adapted. Cardiac filling and stroke volume gradually improved, approaching the levels recorded before launch.

These results suggest that the heart can adjust to prolonged weightlessness more effectively than early measurements might indicate, particularly when astronauts maintain regular physical activity.

Astronauts' Hearts Pass a Simulated Mars Gravity Test

The researchers also wanted to answer a particularly important question for future Mars missions: What happens when an astronaut who has spent months in weightlessness suddenly encounters gravity again?

Mars has roughly 38% of Earth's surface gravity. Although this is considerably weaker than what people experience on Earth, it would still force astronauts' hearts to work differently after months of floating in space.

To reproduce some of these effects, the researchers conducted a simulated Martian gravity test after the astronauts returned to Earth.

Each astronaut was tilted upward by 22 degrees from a lying position. This posture caused blood to shift toward the lower body, approximating the circulatory effects of Martian gravity.

The results were encouraging. After spending about six months in space, the astronauts' hearts performed just as well under simulated Martian gravity as they had when standing upright on Earth before their missions.

This suggests that astronauts who maintain their cardiovascular fitness during spaceflight may be capable of transitioning to the weaker gravity of Mars without experiencing major cardiovascular difficulties.

Although the findings come from a relatively small group of astronauts and a simulation rather than an actual Mars landing, they provide valuable evidence for planning future missions.

Combined with the benefits of daily exercise, the heart's ability to adapt to weightlessness and respond well to simulated Martian gravity offers another reason to be optimistic about sending humans to the Red Planet.

The study is titled "Impact of Prolonged Spaceflight on Cardiac Structure and Function: An Exploratory Longitudinal Observational Study." #4408. "AI is a technofascist, anti-human and anti-art project", says creator of lefty tech satire Future? No Thanks!, before explaining why he used it anyway

Molleindustria called on ChatGPT and Claude for a "tiny fraction" of code

Prominent capital-p Political game developer and left-wing academic Paolo "Molleindustria" Pedercini has revealed that he used generative AI tools for aspects of recent ecosocialist satire Future? No Thanks!. According to Pedercini, "a tiny fraction of its code was written with AI support" towards the end of the project, specifically to assess and fix bugs.

The game also uses text-to-speech voice-acting for a couple of characters, generated with ElevenLab software, with Pedercini noting that the characters in question are intended as a spoof of wonky artificial intelligence. He insists that none of the game's art or writing is AI-generated. These insights arrive weeks after the game's Steam release, which was delayed due to Valve's concerns about sexual material. Back then, there was no genAI disclosure on the game's storepage. There is one now.

Pedercini fessed up about using generative AI on his blog yesterday. "Every independent gaming festival and showcase in the year 2026 requires some kind of generative AI disclosure," he begins. "That is great! Slop and vibe-coded games are a scourge, AI is a technofascist, anti-human and anti-art project, and it’s imperative to create spaces of resistance.

"Future? No Thanks! is a game that tackles the impact and limits of artificial intelligence head-on," Pedercini continues. "It satirizes generative AI and the careless ways it can be used, even in an enlightened ecosocialist world. It also dramatizes beneficial uses, such as coordinating a planned economy, voice recognition, and talking robots. And yet, I can’t say it’s a 100% AI free game."

In a breakdown of development of Future! No Thanks, stretching back to 2021, Pedercini comments that "99% of its code base was written before AI was even viable as a coding tool". As for why he decided to use it during the closing stages, he argues that "ChatGPT or Claude" can be "a powerful semantic search engine" when fixing bugs.

"Search engines have been enshittified to death, specific technical queries don’t yield meaningful results anymore," Pedercini writes. "Most search engines produce low quality AI responses anyway. Such coding assistance was used for things like fixing a shader I didn’t write which broke after an update, or diagnosing a one-frame loading glitch when loading sequences. Finishing touches and minor debugging.

"Unlike assets that can be easily swapped, AI generated code is hard to remove once introduced," he goes on. "I wouldn’t be able to tell which lines I wrote, which were generated, and which were somewhere in between."

Regarding the game's voicework, Pedercini argues that "text-to-speech used to be an uncontroversial technology and an early application of neural networks. Then the AI bros started commercializing AI voices as a way to replace human actors, so it’s now problematic."

Apparently, Future? No Thanks! features "about two minutes" of generated voicework "for an automated toilet and a personal assistant that mis-translates Roman idioms". Pedercini spells out that "the humor of these scenes relies on uncanny voices doing their job poorly". The Future? No Thanks! team experimented with human voices for these roles, he adds, but found "they just weren’t working as well". He includes a clip of the PA character for reference.

Early demos of the game also featured some AI-generated "fake doo-woop tunes" with "AI" in the lyrics that "were supposed to make fun of losers who listen to slop". The referencing of AI in the lyrics was "conceived as a kind of mandatory watermark this future society imposes to AI-generated music," Pedercini explains. "I thought they were hilarious, but over time the association with a plagiarist tech company started to bother me so I replaced them with royalty free loops. By the way, royalty free samples that are certainly AI-free are increasingly hard to find, but I found an old site untouched since 2016."

Pedercini ends his blog (as of writing, anyway) with some thoughts on discussion around generative AI in games, commenting that the "100% no-AI stance/requirement" adopted by a fair few devs and players "is very recent, and the game was being developed before a consensus emerged". He adds that "as an educator I have to look into these tools which most of my students use, and I was trying to figure out if there are non-brain-rotting ways to do so. An older version of the blog had some lengthier thoughts about genAI, in which Pedercini argued that being "AI-free" is "the only truly ethical position" for game developers, given the technology's association with "energy and water use, IP theft, cheating, unemployment, spam, and so on".
