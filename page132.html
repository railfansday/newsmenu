#5422. Airplanes Got ABS Brakes In The '40s — Production Cars Didn't Get Them Until The 1960s

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anti-lock braking systems are one of the most important safety innovations in modern cars. They play a huge roll in the reduction of stopping distances, and they have been legally required as standard equipment on all new cars sold in the United States since Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126 became law in 2013. The massively beneficial technology has been on cars since 1966, but the idea of anti-skid brakes was already around for about two decades prior to its first automotive implementation.

As airplanes became jet-powered and grew faster and more powerful during the aviation boom of World War II, the tasks of safely landing and stopping those planes became more challenging. The Boeing B-47 Stratojet was the first mass-produced aircraft to ever employ an ABS system in 1947 using a system called Hydro-Aire Hytrol ABS, and Dunlop introduced its Maxaret ABS system in 1952.

Mercedes-Benz, known for its various safety innovations, is widely credited with producing the first mass production car with anti-lock brakes, but according to Hagerty, the first car to ever come fitted with such a system was actually the ultra-low production Jensen FF that was built from 1966 to 1971. It used an adaptation of Dunlop's Maxaret ABS system, but only 320 Jensen FFs were ever built, and none were officially exported to the U.S. The first application of ABS in a mass-production car eventually came from a U.S. manufacturer.

For the most part, credit goes to the 1971 Chrysler Imperial and its optional Sure-Brake system as being the first production car to offer an anti-lock braking system, since it prevented all four of the Imperial's wheels from locking up under panic braking. It was, however, what's called a three-channel anti-lock braking system, which means it used one brake-pressure modulator for each front wheel, and a single modulator to control brake pressure for both rear brakes.

Popular Science put a Sure-Brake-equipped Imperial to the test in 1970, and found the system to reliably prevent wheel lockup, even on ice. Road Test magazine tested a Sure-Brake-equipped Imperial in 1971, and noted how alarming the rapid clunking sound caused by the system's pressure actuation was, and complained about how it may further startle a panicked driver in an emergency situation, but again, the system prevented lockup. Sadly, Chrysler offered the system as an extra-cost option on 1971-to-1973 model year Imperials, but less than 5% of buyers opted for the system, so Chrysler canned it for 1974.

Though Chrysler's Sure-Brake was a computerized system, Mercedes and Bosch were the first to introduce a digital controller and implement the use of digital microelectronics in the 1978 S-Class, which paved the way for modern ABS. There are claims that the 1978 Mercedes-Benz S-Class was the first car to implement ABS, but to phrase it most accurately, it was the first four-wheel multi-channel anti-lock brake system to use digital microelectronics.

From their early roots stopping jets in short order, to safely stopping Jensen FFs, Chrysler Imperials, Mercedes-Benz S-Classes, and now every new car sold in the U.S., anti-lock braking systems have left a mark on transportation safety history. The technology has also paved the way for other pivotal safety innovations like electronic stability control (ESC), as well as performance and off-road features like torque vectoring and brake-based LSDs.

#143. How OpenAI turned its coding tools into dots, an always-on personal assistant

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

The always-on AI personal assistant, which keeps working in the background even when you aren’t asking it to, is this year’s answer to the question of how people will actually use deep learning models. The startup Instinct became an overnight sensation, and Meta put the full force of its marketing behind its assistant, Muse.

OpenAI wasn’t going to be left out. The company released dots, its assistant offering, at its developer day in late September. Alexander Embiricos, who leads OpenAI’s enterprise product team, joined us at TechCrunch Disrupt this month to explain where the product came from — starting with the question of why so many companies seem to be turning to cutesy mascots as avatars for their trillion-parameter matrix multiplication machines.

“There’s kind of this convergent evolution happening,” Embiricos said of the efforts to build AI assistants. “As we were working on the product, we realized if we could frame this as, it’s an entity and it’s a single conversation, not many different conversations….it starts to become intuitive.”

Embiricos points out that you could achieve much of dots’ functionality with Open AI’s Codex app, its coding tool, released at the beginning of this year. But that requires more work and hand-holding. He recalled celebrating when his wife started leaving her computer on all night so that her agents could keep working.

“On the product team, what I think about is, how do we make this intuitive and useful to people?” he told Disrupt attendees. “A lot of that isn’t building some new piece of tech into the product; it’s how you frame it to the user and how it feels.”

Making the technology feel intuitive meant repackaging the functionality of the company’s models into something more accessible, a path it was already on as it spun ChatGPT Work out of the agentic coding tools it had built for software engineers. Even the name — a play on the negative space inside of OpenAI’s logo — is intended “to feel like just something that could eventually be ubiquitous.” (Asked when he learned that rival AI entrepreneur Elon Musk owns the dot.com URL, Embiricos said, “I found out very late.”)

The idea of a persistent agent is that it doesn’t need prompting. For OpenAI, that means more token usage, and for users, a more pro-active assistant “that’s sort of thinking about you and thinking how to help you,” as Embiricos put it.

In our testing, the assistant’s ability to think ahead is apparent and often useful, but it isn’t quite there yet. It will flag calendar conflicts and upcoming deadlines, but not necessarily in the most timely fashion.

“The first time it told me about one of those [conflicts] — this is like early in development — it told me three minutes before the thing,” Embiricos says of his dot. “It’s like, ‘Hey, this thing is coming up. You might want to do something about it.’ I only have three minutes!”

Embiricos expects the timing to improve over time, but the proactive approach is key to the product. “I was hearing about an engineer at a drone delivery startup, and they were saying, ‘We had this outage, and by the time I noticed that the outage was going on, my dot had already noticed the outage was happening, had already analyzed the outage, and had come to me with useful information for how we could resolve the outage.’”

As with most AI agent use cases, there’s a tension between the personal information an AI model needs to be useful and exactly how much personal data you are exposing to a software company. Muse has been criticized for the profiles it builds of your family and friends, which the software uses to know, say, what kind of restaurant you might want to go to with a specific pal, but could also add incredible value to Meta’s advertising business. Does dots collect similar data?

“Yes, we have a thing that we built into dots, which we call proactive research,” Embiricos said. “On a cadence, it will think about what it knows about you, what is the conversation that it’s had, and then what have you given it access to that it can think about, and then it will go and research, ‘What could I do to be more helpful to you?’ And then, basically, it will propose that.”

It’s difficult to separate the data needs of the agent from that of its parent corporation. Hark, a startup developing its own AI personal assistant, is explicit in pointing out that it doesn’t collect data to fuel an advertising business the way that OpenAI and Meta do.

With dots, OpenAI says that the agent has the same privacy controls as ChatGPT itself: Users can turn off model training (stopping OpenAI from using their conversations to improve its AI), and control which resources it accesses within your computer and how. Still, those controls aren’t granular and the app can be pushy. For example, it refuses to accept read-only access to cloud drives.

“What I think we need to do is basically build out super, super, super granular controls,” Embiricos said, but he pointed to the model’s intelligence and discretion as the other side of this coin.

“This is actually something we kind of got some flack about when we launched,” Embiricos said. “We had really conservative defaults over what the agent would do, and it would ask for permission a lot. I remember knowing that we were going to see that feedback, but thinking, ‘This is okay, we can tune that over time.’”

The other big question about AI agents is whether they can scale. They’re fun when only a few people have them, and stories of agents snagging hard-to-get reservations abound. But businesses don’t necessarily like dealing with agents and might not be equipped to. People certainly don’t like dealing with them (note: stop having your AI agents pitch me), and Embiricos is among those people. He also has a human assistant because OpenAI’s corporate partners want to interact with a person, he said, adding, “I personally never intend to have a meeting with someone else’s agent.”

If the AI industry is right that agents are our future, can we get there without overwhelming the infrastructure that our world currently runs on, from businesses and websites to payment networks?

#4882. Keystone breach deepens: Hackers stole key used to verify wallets as genuine

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Keystone initially disclosed a leak of customer information but has since disclosed that hackers also stole a key that could allow counterfeit wallets to pass authenticity checks.

Hardware wallet maker Keystone said an attacker who breached its website last week also stole a key used to verify the authenticity of its devices, widening the scope of an incident initially disclosed as a customer data leak.

In a statement Monday, Keystone said the stolen authentication key could allow attackers to produce counterfeit wallets that pass its authenticity checks.

“These devices could then be sold or shipped to customers, who might unknowingly use them as genuine products, potentially resulting in asset theft,” Keystone said.

The incident adds to a series of attacks targeting hardware wallet manufacturers this year.

Earlier this month, Ledger asked resellers across Southeast Asia and Hong Kong to suspend sales following reports that devices sold in the region contained unauthorized implants capable of capturing recovery phrases.

In late July, Coldcard manufacturer Coinkite issued a warning to users after attackers exploited a bug that caused its devices to generate wallet seeds with less entropy than intended, resulting in the theft of millions of dollars in crypto.

In its latest update, Keystone said the attacker exploited a vulnerability in a third-party monitoring tool used by its website to escalate privileges.

“This enabled the attacker to obtain some customer information and a key used to verify the authenticity of our devices.”

Keystone said it plans to destroy 18,835 devices in its inventory that contain the compromised key and has suspended production shipments of all devices using it.

The company said shipments would resume once it begins producing devices with a new authentication key.

Keystone said devices manufactured and delivered before the Oct. 20 incident should be considered safe and that their authenticity checks remain trustworthy.

However, it could not guarantee the authenticity of devices delivered after that date and urged affected customers to contact its support team for additional verification.

Keystone also disclosed the extent of the customer data breach, saying its website’s Shopify store had generated 51,083 buyer records.

Of those, 25,119 had been deleted per its 180-day data retention policy.

However, the remaining 25,964 records dating from 2024 to 2026 may have been stolen by the attacker. Keystone said the older entries were not deleted due to a failure in its automated tool used to handle bulk erasure of data.

“The tool did not operate reliably. Some erasure tasks failed, and we did not detect and address these failures in time, resulting in certain personal information being retained beyond the permitted period,” Keystone said.

In addition, the attacker may have also had access to 12,873 customer checkout records retained by Shopify — information from purchases that had not been completed.

Keystone said it had contacted Shopify to request the deletion of personal information in checkout records retained for more than 30 days.

The incident echoes a June 2020 data breach at Ledger that exposed more than 1 million email addresses, along with customer names, phone numbers and physical addresses. The leaked information fueled years of phishing attempts targeting affected users.

Other hardware wallet manufacturers have also suffered breaches involving customer information and third-party services this year.

In August, Trezor disclosed a breach at its shipping partner that exposed customer information, with the number of affected users expanding to more than 80,000 in September.

SafePal also reported an order-tracking vulnerability in August that exposed data belonging to nearly 40,000 customers, while BitBox was targeted in a September phishing campaign involving a compromised third-party email provider.

#3755. Sunrise On The Reaping Director Francis Lawrence Has Secrets About The Hunger Games Prequel's Key Relationship [Exclusive]

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

"The Hunger Games: The Sunrise on the Reaping" is coming to theaters soon, and fans immersed in the world of the Hunger Games — specifically, the transformed North American continent known as Panem under a totalitarian regime — will finally get to see Suzanne Collins' 2025 book play out on screen. I recently spoke to Francis Lawrence, the director of every single Hunger Games adaptation aside from the first one (which was helmed by Gary Ross), about one of the most essential bonds in this story: namely, the one between our protagonist Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) and his fellow District 12 tribute Maysilee Donner (McKenna Grace).

As Lawrence revealed, he and his actors kept some things close to the chest. "There were a lot of conversations with both Joe and McKenna because we started with the games, and so a lot of our lead-up to shooting was rehearsals of fights, then action sequences and things in Spain where we were shooting," the director said. "And so I had the two of them a lot. And so we would just talk constantly about the two of them." He continued:

"Quite honestly, I think the three of us have some secrets about their relationship. I've just always found it really interesting that when she sees him, she slams the door in his face and they've known each other their entire lives. And I think there's probably other elements, too, to their story that Suzanne hasn't written about. So we all have our own secrets that we use to get underneath what the characters are and how they relate to one another and why they may have conflict and why they bond and all of that. So that stuff's really fun. And they're great at it because they're both great actors."

If you're about to yell "spoiler alert!" at me, calm down; the relationship between Haymitch and Maysilee is actually revealed in Suzanne Collins' first literary sequel, "Catching Fire," which was published back in 2009. As that trilogy's heroes and District 12 tributes Katniss Everdeen and Peeta Mellark (played on-screen by Jennifer Lawrence and Josh Hutcherson) prepare to head back into the arena for the Third Quarter Quell — a super-evil edition of the Hunger Games, which puts former victors back into the arena — they watch footage of the 50th Games, where Haymitch and Maysilee formed an alliance against double the number of tributes. That's how they watch Maysilee's bitter end: "Haymitch runs for her, anyway. He arrives only in time to watch the last of a flock of candy pink birds, equipped with long, thin beaks, skewer her through the neck. He holds her hand while she dies."

Maysilee is also important to Katniss in a fully unexpected way. As Peeta points out, her mockingjay pin came from the Donner family, and Maysilee's abandoned twin Merrilee (the mother of Katniss's friend Madge), who "spends half her life in bed immobilized with terrible pain, shutting out the world." Katniss continues:

"I think of how I never realized that she and my mother shared this connection. Of Madge showing up in that snowstorm to bring the painkiller for Gale. Of my mockingjay pin and how it means something completely different now that I know that its former owner was Madge's aunt, Maysilee Donner, a tribute who was murdered in the arena."

The entire premise of the Hunger Games is disgusting on several levels, but something particularly insidious about them — and something that Suzanne Collins explores in each and every book she writes — is the fact that the kids pitted against each other in the arena end up forming bonds. With four District 12 tributes in Haymitch and Maysilee's games instead of two, Haymitch initially tries to avoid any alliances. But the youngest of the group, Louella McCoy (Molly McCann in the upcoming film), already has a long-standing relationship with him characterized by Haymitch's protective feelings for the girl. When Louella ends up at the center of a tragic accident, the sickest thing in the entire Hunger Games franchise to date happens, Louella is replaced by a brainwashed lookalike, Lou Lou (Iona Bell), who's forced to compete in her stead.

Francis Lawrence told me that he "[mapped out] the relationships" between Haymitch and his fellow tributes, and because Haymitch and Louella were playground "sweethearts," he made sure to sketch that out. Beyond that, though, he pointed out the parallels between Louella and Katniss, both of whom are young brunettes that favor braids and find themselves in the arena. "There's the connections that if you know the other stories and movies, that you might make of Louella with the braid and Katniss, calling her sweetheart, and calling Katniss sweetheart and all of those kinds of things, which are a bit Easter eggy if you don't really know the books and things, but all that stuff is fun," Lawrence mused.

"The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping" arrives in theaters on November 20, 2026 — and you'll see all of these relationships play out on the big screen then.
