#194. Instagram Is a Streaming Service Now

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I’ve never listened to a full episode of the Happy Wife Happy Life podcast, hosted by Jordan Myrick and Kendahl Landreth, but I’ve sure seen all the clips.

My Instagram Reels algorithm recently served me, along with 5 million other viewers, a brief rant from Myrick about feeling bamboozled by the health care system. Then, I spent the next hour scrolling through the show’s other snippets on Reels, loving the couple’s snarky banter. Still, I never followed the duo to a different platform to watch the full-length podcasts.

On Monday, Instagram announced plans that might have turned me into a regular listener of the show. Instagram will expand tests in the US that let creators upload long-form content up to 90 minutes and flip a video’s orientation to horizontal. Creators can also link their short clips directly back to full-length videos.

“Our belief here is that entertainment and creativity are not limited to a three-minute video,” Tessa Lyons, vice president of product at Instagram, tells WIRED. She wants to build the “best place for social entertainment” with this overhaul of Instagram video. The social media platform may soon feel like a streaming service, as more viewers watch longer videos on their big screen.

Instagram is also expanding its creator tools for turning Reels into episodic content. “We want to be able to say, ‘Wow, Reece loved episode 3. So, as soon as episode 4 drops, we've got to make sure to show it to him,’” Lyons says. This Reels feature, called “Series,” is already available for some creators in the app, and it's being tested for TV viewing as well.

Instagram is focused on the experience of watching videos on your TV. It's hunting for ways to make that TV experience feel social, like showing your connections what you’ve been watching via the Friends tab. The company also plans to turn your smartphone into a TV remote.

Lyons sees the video-focused update as avoiding one of IGTV’s core mistakes—IGTV was the company’s previous attempt at more expansive content. In that instance, many viewers desperately wanted to flip the screen. IGTV failed to connect with users, in part, because it only allowed vertical videos. Meta discontinued the app a few years after its 2018 launch.

While Instagram’s latest changes challenge YouTube’s TV dominance, they also try to leverage its current strength as a platform for clips. Bite-sized clips, often of two people sitting behind podcast microphones, feel inescapable on social media right now, with many creators using them to boost visibility. Even WIRED’s own podcast now records videos for social media clipping.

Instagram is streamlining a viewer’s potential fandom journey. Rather than viral clips and long-form content existing on different platforms, Instagram is positioning itself as a centralized experience where I can stumble on Myrick’s health care rant in the morning and then open the app on my TV later that night and get a nudge to stream the full podcast episode.

“What we're seeing right now is that a lot of great short-form content is vertical, and a lot of great long-form content is horizontal, and we have the design challenge of figuring out how to make that all work cohesively in one product,” Lyons says. “TV becomes a great destination for watching long-form, horizontal content, and mobile becomes a great place for discovering that content.”

#5308. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/university-texas-austin-unveils-new-academic-health-system-it-builds-ai-native-hospital" hreflang="en">UT Austin unveils UT Dell Medicine as it bets big on new AI-native hospital</a>

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
UT Austin unveils UT Dell Medicine as it bets big on new AI-native hospital" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The University of Texas at Austin unveiled Tuesday a unified academic health system, called UT Dell Medicine, that brings its clinics, Dell Medical School and the upcoming UT Dell Medical Center under one banner.

UT Dell Medicine marks the evolution of Dell Medical School and its clinical practice into a full academic health system, bringing its existing care operations and future growth under a unified identity, according to Claudia Lucchinetti, M.D., executive vice president for Medical Affairs, UT Austin and the executive leader of UT Dell Medicine.

The University of Texas at Austin is the academic and operational anchor of the UT Dell Medicine health system.

UT Dell Medicine will operate as Austin’s fourth major health system, and its only academic health system, Lucchinetti said, bringing the 10-year-old medical school, its clinical practice and future healthcare facilities under one name. Three health systems currently operate hospitals in the Austin area: HCA Healthcare (operating locally through the St. David’s HealthCare partnership), Baylor Scott & White Health and Ascension Seton.

Beyond serving patients in central Texas, UT Dell Medicine aims to become a destination academic health system that combines state-of-the-art clinical care, cutting-edge research and next-generation medical education, Lucchinetti told Fierce Healthcare.

"We're bringing together the University of Texas at Austin and all its great capabilities across everything from AI, engineering, humanities, the sciences and discovery. It's also bringing together the University of Texas MD Anderson, which will be bringing forward world-class capabilities in cancer care, and then it brings in Dell Medical School and now UT Dell Medical Center, which will complement those cancer capabilities with additional capabilities across predominantly cardiovascular, brain and neurosciences transplants, and complex advanced critical care capabilities," she said in an interview.

UT Dell Medicine plans to expand significantly over the next four to six years. Once fully operational, UT Dell Medicine plans to double its clinical capability through physicians and advanced providers and more than triple its patient volumes.

Anchored by Dell Medical School, UT Dell Medicine brings together patient care, research and education, system executives said. Its existing clinical practice and ambulatory surgery center, previously known as UT Health Austin, will adopt the new name.

The announcement on Tuesday comes as site clearing begins this month to build a new AI-native medical center in North Austin, slated to open in 2030 and with an estimated price tag of about $5 billion. UT Dell Medical Center, developed by the University of Texas in partnership with The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, is anchored by a landmark $750 million gift from the Michael & Susan Dell Foundation. The Dells were also integral in launching the university's medical school with a $50 million donation in 2013.

The planned 27-acre anchor facility, which will be located within the larger 300-plus-acre UT Dell Campus for Advanced Research, will feature a 300-to-500-bed specialty hospital, an outpatient clinic and a full emergency department.

UT Dell Medical Center will center its clinical strategy on five areas: cardiovascular, cancer, neurological and transplant care, plus advanced intensive care. The medical center will integrate cancer services from UT MD Anderson Cancer Center, expanding access to advanced treatments and clinical trials.

"There still remains a significant outmigration for complex care out of Austin, not because we don't already have excellent doctors and also places for care delivery, but complex care requires a level of integration and multi-specialty coordination that, frankly, is difficult to navigate in many health systems," Lucchinetti said. "Here in Austin, it is a highly fragmented system. There are multiple systems and multiple different electronic health records. There are multiple different groups across anesthesia and radiology."

Mike Ryan, chief healthcare technology, digital and data strategist at The University of Texas at Austin, added, "We studied this market deeply. We know where the gaps in care are. The reason there's so much outmigration in these specialties is that once a patient's needs get to a certain level of complexity, we see a lot of those people leaving the market, if they are able to, and not everybody is able. It's really only in an academic medical center that you can bring this level of integration and complex care together to meet the needs of those patients."

UT Dell Medicine plans to organize care around a patient-centered, integrated and multidisciplinary care model, Lucchinetti noted. The academic health system aims to make complex care easier to navigate by organizing specialists and services around the patient. The medical center will also share a single digital infrastructure, medical records and billing systems with MD Anderson to better coordinate care for cancer patients.

"Many patients are forced to navigate these very complex systems during the most vulnerable, difficult times in their lives, and to be able to have the system orbit them is a fundamental priority, and at the core of the vision of bringing integrated medical care to the patient," she said.

"That is fundamental to improving outcomes, keeping patients in Texas, and also bringing new capabilities that frankly don't exist that patients have to leave to get elsewhere," she added. "We have a priority to address the needs of our community, but also to do it in a way that thinks about what's different in this era. How do you build that system from the ground up?"

The new medical center project integrates artificial intelligence and advanced tech directly into patient care and clinical training. UT leaders believe they have a unique opportunity to redesign the academic health system for the next 50 years and to build AI and other advanced technologies into the hospital's infrastructure from the ground up. Executives are future-proofing the hospital, making decisions today on clinical offerings, spatial adaptability, robotics and AI readiness years before launch.

The architectural firm leading the design is Skidmore, Owings & Merrill. The project is past 50% completion of the schematic design phase as the architectural team locks in flexible spatial design and capital equipment strategy for a 2030 opening, executives said.

"We've stepped back and studied, 'How can we do this in a way that takes advantage of technological advances and do it in a way that makes the experience better for patients, that is more connected to patients, but also doesn't lose the human connection that's so important?'" Ryan said.

A hospital built five to seven years ago might have cameras in patient rooms. UT Dell Medical Center, by contrast, expects to have 20,000 to 30,000 sensors throughout the facility, Ryan said.

"Those sensors will be capturing data that we will use to improve experience, to make care teams more efficient, to be able to alert care teams to patient needs that we might not be able to get to otherwise," he said.

The health system is designing its facilities to support extensive use of robotics, including automated transportation of medications, supplies, linens and food.

Health system leaders declined to name specific vendors or tech companies they plan to work with to integrate technology into the medical center.

The construction of an AI-native hospital built for robotics and other technologies raises interesting challenges about how to build a "future-proof" facility as tech advances so rapidly.

"We're being cognizant of the design, knowing that some of these technologies will change over time, and we're being cognizant of the infrastructure that we build directly into the facilities so that we can adapt it," Ryan said. "We'll be collecting data, but the algorithms that we run against that data will change over time. We're designing it for future biomedical equipment advances. We're talking with those vendors to understand their roadmaps and where they're going."

For a tech-forward hospital, physical design considerations include elevators and hallways wide enough to accommodate robotics and separate corridors versus shared corridors, Ryan noted.

"There are a number of things in there around how the building controls would work to get to this idea. The building is part of the care team, which is both a clinical concept and an experiential concept. For example, automated dimming of lights and temperature controls based on patient needs or activities, and then clinically capturing data to do some of the interventions, the alerting. We have to think about how do we actually orchestrate care that we're building into this," he said.

The system is designing the facility and its infrastructure to accommodate future technologies, including AI tools "that haven't even been invented yet," Ryan said. "We think we're setting the infrastructure up right to be able to do that," he said.

But a more technological future is not necessarily a more humane one, Lucchinetti countered. "Technology is there in service of the patient, and if it's really doing its best job, it's invisible to the patient; it recedes into the background, and what comes forward is the care model, the compassion, the empathy, the connection that still is fundamental to medicine," she said.

The vision is for the hospital to "become an intelligent member of the care team," she added.

There are opportunities to use AI and data to focus upstream on broader population health and preventive care, health system leaders said. UT Dell Medicine plans to use AI, predictive tools and data to shift care toward earlier detection and prevention, with the goal of helping people stay healthier longer.

"How can we use technology to anticipate, to predict, to intervene, to act, to deliver better outcomes? How do you wire prevention truly in everything we're doing—whether that's at home, whether that's virtually, whether that's through the outpatient capabilities we're building, whether that's through the complex hospital environment," Lucchinetti said.

UT Dell Medicine is exploring how to bring UT Austin’s supercomputing capabilities into clinical care, allowing clinicians to analyze multiple streams of patient data in real time and make faster decisions. The Texas Advanced Computing Center at The University of Texas at Austin is one of the leading academic supercomputing centers in the world. The medical center wants to integrate supercomputing, edge computing and automation and digital twin technology to predict patient risks and expedite physician decision-making.

The system is laying the data and computing foundation for AI at scale, prioritizing safety, security, privacy and validation while connecting UT Dell Medicine with UT Austin’s broader technology capabilities, Lucchinetti noted.

UT Dell Medicine and tech leaders are also thinking about the patient experience. The aim is to make advanced technology largely invisible to patients, replacing intrusive equipment and routine disruptions with integrated digital tools and sensors. "There's a significant amount of thought going into how can we make this an environment that really promotes healing," Ryan said.

"It's been hard to see dear friends lose their jobs, and this beloved franchise seemingly vanish forever"

The gradual drip of details about and glimpses at what BioWare were looking to cook up with the cancelled Mass Effect 5 continues, this time thanks to a composer who was in talks to come aboard to put together the RPG's soundtrack. Said songsmith, Austin Wintory, has opted to share around 20 minutes of tunes he put together just as an attempt to explore where he might take things if he did end up being hired to score Commander Shepard's next adventure. Naturally, with BioWare's closure, that hasn't come to pass.

The eight tracks in the YouTube video Wintory, whose compositions have graced the likes of Journey and Assassin's Creed Syndicate, has put together range from a main theme, to intense battle tunes, and the sorts of 'ooh, there's something happening' songs that usually accompany key story moments. The opener, dubbed The Legacy of Shepard, blends pensive piano tinkling with bombastic sci-fi swells. The final track's a funkier remixed version of it. I think my favorite might be track seven, Forever Calibrating, just because it's the sort of relaxed and atmospheric backing tune I tend to put on for ambience while working.

"Working on Mass Effect 5 was my career's biggest dream project, my white whale," Wintory wrote in the video's description. "It is my favorite franchise of all time, and for the last couple years I'd been in serious conversations with BioWare and EA to jump in. My suggestion was to treat the original game's masterful Jack Wall/Sam Hulick score as a starting point, and to build on it from there with deeper use of physical, analog synths and a more tightly interwoven use of the orchestra. But a love letter first and foremost (not unlike my approach to the Aliens franchise with Fireteam Elite).

"As part of the ongoing dialogue, I decided last year (2025) to surprise the folks with an elaborate production: nearly 20 minutes of music, fully recorded and produced with synths, vocals and a large orchestra here in LA. I'd not yet learned any narrative details of what they were planning so this was just pure musical exploration. Receiving the news alongside everyone else that EA had decided to not only cancel the project but shutdown BioWare entirely was a deeply felt loss. It's been hard to see dear friends lose their jobs, and this beloved franchise seemingly vanish forever."

The composer closed by thanking BioWare and EA's music department for giving him the chance to put together these experimental Mass Effect tracks.

While this outpouring of info about what BioWare were up to with Mass Effect 5 is likely all we'll ever get of it, given the folks who were working on it have been scattered to the winds, some fans of the series have begun petitioning for Shep's space adventures not to end up rotting in EA's big shed of disused game IP. Though, surprisingly, they appear not to be teaming that call with the assertion that it should ideally be the same devs who were working on the series prior to BioWare's closure that get first dibs on any opportunity to carry on making Mass Effects.

#247. Coca-Cola hikes earnings outlook, even as it sees lackluster demand in North America

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Coca-Cola on Tuesday raised its full-year outlook for the third straight quarter, despite lackluster demand in its home market.

For the full year, Coke now expects comparable earnings per share to grow at least 11%, up from its prior forecast of an increase in the range of 9% to 10%. The beverage giant also anticipates that its organic revenue will climb at least 5%, a slight increase from its previous outlook of approximately 5% growth.

CEO Henrique Braun attributed the company's strong results to its "speed and discipline" despite the "uneven environment," he said on the company's earnings conference call.

Looking ahead to 2027, the company expects consumer behavior to remain unchanged, CFO John Murphy said. In recent quarters, Braun and other executives have said North American shoppers are under pressure, stretched thin by inflation and economic uncertainty, and looking for value. But the beverage giant has also said consumers are still buying its drinks, from premium brands like Fairlife to its namesake soda.

Shares of the company fell less than 1% in morning trading on its gloomy forecast for the consumer environment.

Here's what the company reported compared with what Wall Street analysts surveyed by LSEG were expecting:

Coke reported fiscal third-quarter net income attributable to shareholders of $4.01 billion, or 93 cents per share, up from $3.7 billion, or 86 cents per share, a year earlier.

Excluding transaction gains and other items, Coke earned 91 cents per share.

Net sales rose 7% to $13.4 billion. Coke's organic revenue, which strips out acquisitions, divestitures and foreign currency, also climbed 7% in the quarter.

For the second straight quarter, the company's unit case volume increased 5%, helped by higher demand outside of North America.

Coke's home market reported flat volume. However, the company's North American business would have reported volume growth if not for the timing of the Fourth of July holiday and the monthslong shortage of Topo Chico, a sparkling water brand Coke owns, Murphy told CNBC.

"The underlying momentum remains quite strong," he said. "Consumer sentiment is largely unchanged in the sense that, wherever you are on the income scale, you're looking for value, and I think our system here has done a good job interpreting that."

In North America, Coke's at-home business is faring better than its away-from-home segment, which includes restaurants, movie theaters and amusement parks. The restaurant industry is still under pressure, and Coke expects that to continue through the rest of the year, Murphy told analysts on the company's earnings conference call.

Globally, Coke's sparkling soft drinks business saw volume increase 5% during the quarter, lifted by growth of Coke, Coke Zero Sugar and Diet Coke.

Diet Coke is having a "resurgence," Murphy told CNBC. Gen Z consumers in the U.S. have nicknamed it a "fridge cigarette," while consumers in India threw Diet Coke parties as shortages tied to the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran have made the drink scarce.

"It's a brand that's almost reinventing itself with the next generation of consumers," he said.

Coke has also gained market share as rival PepsiCo sees its soda business struggle, according to Murphy. Still, some of Coke's soda brands, like Sprite, have "some work to do."

Coke's water, sports, coffee and tea division also saw volume climb by 5%. Increased demand for its water, which includes its premium Smartwater brand, fueled the growth. Demand for its tea and coffee was flat, while sports drinks declined.

The company's juice, value-added dairy and plant-based beverages business reported flat volume, despite growth in brands like Fairlife and Minute Maid.
