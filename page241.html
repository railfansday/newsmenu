#398. American Airlines forecasts bigger loss in 2026 on fuel spike, plans to slow growth next year

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

American Airlines forecast deeper losses this year and said it will slow its capacity growth next year as record revenue failed to help the airline recover from a spike in fuel costs.

American said Thursday that it expects to lose between $1 and $1.60 a share, on an adjusted basis in 2026. In July, it forecast adjusted full-year earnings per share between a loss of 65 cents and a profit of 65 cents.

The Fort Worth, Texas-based airline is the fourth major U.S. carrier to cut its forecast for 2026 due to a jump in jet fuel following the start of the Iran war. Rising jet fuel prices have driven up airfare more than 20% from last year, according to airline reports and federal data. Delta Air Lines, United Airlines and Southwest Airlines also slashed their outlooks this month.

Jet fuel is airlines' biggest expense after labor, and the spike has added billions to costs this year. American's fuel bill was up 67.5% in the third quarter alone, from last year to $4.64 billion.

"American delivered record third-quarter revenue and the strongest year-over-year revenue growth in my career, with premium performance leading the way," American CEO Robert Isom said in a news release.

Despite the lower expectations for deeper losses in 2026, Isom said on an earnings call Thursday that he has "great confidence" that the company will return to profitability next year.

The CEO has been under pressure to boost profits at the carrier, which has dramatically trailed profitable rivals Delta and United. American has been investing in new lounges and revamped cabins with more premium seats and said this year that it plans to add seat-back screens again to its narrow-body fleet.

Earlier this month, the airline named a new network chief. It was the latest move in a management shake-up that has included new hires in its operations department and its commercial team. Last year, American brought in a new commercial chief, industry veteran Nat Pieper.

International and premium-travel demand has remained especially robust, executives said. Late last month, American said it would deepen its partnership with Alaska Airlines as the Seattle-based carrier would join American's trans-Atlantic and trans-Pacific joint businesses.

The airline is also closing in on an order for new wide-body airplanes, executives said. CFO Devon May told CNBC on Thursday that the airline has considered the Boeing 787 Dreamliner, Airbus A330 and Airbus A350.

American forecast it will lose between 50 cents and $1.10 a share, adjusted, in the fourth quarter, with revenue expected to be up as much as 22.5% from a year earlier and capacity up between 5.5% and 6.5%.

#735. NASA’s Curiosity rover reveals a spectacular new view of Mars with a mystery on the horizon

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA's Curiosity rover has captured a spectacular new panorama of Mars, providing its clearest look yet at a series of mysterious cliffs shaped by wind. Scientists have been eager to examine these distant formations up close, and the latest images offer an exciting preview of what lies ahead. Taken at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time, the scene reveals striking blue tones across the foreground while morning sunlight illuminates rugged cliffs known as yardangs along the horizon.

Curiosity recorded the panorama using its Mastcam on August 11, during the mission's 4,982nd Martian day, or sol. Six separate photographs were transmitted to Earth and combined to create the sweeping view. Unlike most Mastcam photographs, the images were processed without white balancing, allowing the scene to retain the distinctive colors and lighting of the Martian morning.

Curiosity Approaches Mysterious Martian Cliffs

The yardangs stretch approximately 10 miles (16 kilometers) across the northwestern portion of Mount Sharp, a 3-mile-tall (5-kilometer-tall) mountain that Curiosity has been climbing since 2014. The rover recently achieved a major milestone, gaining 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) in elevation, a greater climb than any other rover has completed on Mars.

As Curiosity continues toward these unusual formations, researchers anticipate increasingly detailed views of the cliffs. More importantly, they hope that examining the rocks firsthand will help explain how the remarkable landscape developed.

What Mars' Ancient Rocks Reveal About Its Past

Mount Sharp contains numerous layers of sedimentary rock, each preserving evidence from a different chapter of Martian history. Investigating these layers has helped scientists reconstruct a time billions of years ago when lakes and streams existed in this region of Mars.

As the planet's environment changed, those bodies of water eventually disappeared, leaving deposits of salty minerals behind. Once additional material stopped accumulating on the mountain, powerful winds may have gradually eroded portions of the existing rock, carving out the distinctive yardangs visible today.

Yet the unusual appearance of this particular layer has raised questions about its origins. Its color differs from the surrounding terrain, and its rock layers are tilted in an unexpected direction. Scientists suspect that processes beyond ordinary wind erosion may have contributed to its formation.

"The yardang layer looks out of place. It's the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on," said Ashwin Vasavada, Curiosity project scientist of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "But that's what makes it exciting to reach. No one is sure exactly what created this layer, but one idea is it may be ash deposited by ancient volcanic eruptions."

NASA Plans to Investigate the Cliffs in 2027

Curiosity is now in the second year of its fifth extended mission, which began with an investigation of unusual spiderweb-like boxwork ridges. Over roughly the next year, the rover will continue traveling through rock layers containing abundant sulfates and carbonates, minerals that provide evidence of ancient drying conditions on Mars.

Mission scientists aim to reach the bottom of the yardang formations sometime in 2027. Once there, Curiosity should be able to examine the mysterious cliffs using its robotic arm, gathering unique measurements that could help determine how they formed and reveal more about the environmental changes that shaped Mars billions of years ago.

#437. David's Bridal plans to spin off AI platform Pearl into publicly traded company

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

David's Bridal is planning to spin off its artificial intelligence platform, Pearl, into a stand-alone publicly traded company targeting a valuation of roughly $350 million, CNBC learned exclusively.

The company expects the deal to close in the first half of 2027.

David's Bridal filed a nonbinding letter of intent on Thursday to separate out Pearl. The AI-centered business would own David's Bridal's intellectual property and look to build on its growing AI model, while the original company would try to strengthen its physical retail business.

"We have a fast-growing, high-margin business generating positive cash flow right now in Pearl, and going public now will help us attract the talent and give us more optionality to fuel the growth that we believe that we have," David's Bridal CEO Kelly Cook, who will take over as CEO of Pearl after the deal closes, told CNBC in an interview.

Cook said the companies will work hand in hand to serve customers throughout the wedding-planning process, adding that the split is "positioning both companies for strategic growth."

Pearl's services include an agentic AI-powered wedding-planning platform, a proprietary tool to streamline appointments and communications and a digital content network with original programming. It uses AI to personalize its suggestions based on its data collection for each client.

David's Bridal expects Pearl to generate an EBITDA margin of more than 30% this year and is projecting revenue growth of more than 100% in 2027, exceeding $85 million. It's expected to be debt-free after the deal closes and have positive free cash flow over the next two years, according to the company.

"Pearl has earned the right to stand on its own because it's created more value than we've expected," Cook said.

Following the transaction, Pearl will own David's Bridal's intellectual property and license it back to the chain.

"It's a very healthy business model," Cook said.

She added that the demand remains strong for both of the businesses. Grand View Research estimates the U.S. wedding services market will top $96 billion in 2026 — and both Pearl and David's Bridal want to be at the forefront of that segment.

The companies will continue to have a "relationship built on reciprocity," she said, sharing proprietary data and pointing customers to the other business for the next steps in their wedding planning. The split will allow each company to refine its focus, she added.

Still, the plan to go public comes at a time when AI stocks have been struggling. The initial public offering market for consumer companies has been lackluster, more companies are postponing their public debuts and investors are beginning to lose confidence in the AI boom.

Cook said the differentiator for Pearl is that the company is "not AI for AI's sake."

"Most AI companies have to find a customer," she said. "We already have her ... we have her information, and it's less about the AI side and more about modernizing this entire ecosystem for her that desperately needed modernization."

David's Bridal is no stranger to financial struggles. The company filed for bankruptcy protection in 2018 and emerged a few months later. It then filed a second time in 2023, citing a significant business slowdown from the pandemic and high inflation. To save itself, the company then sold its business to asset manager Cion Investment Corp. to avoid a shutdown.

As part of the spinoff deal, Cion has proposed to invest $25 million as an anchor investor in the separate company.

Cook, who took over as CEO last year to help it transition from a legacy retailer to a more cohesive online and in-store experience, said the company has "been through a hard chapter," but those struggles led to the decision to split the business.

"That's no secret, we've talked about that openly," she said. "What's extraordinary is what came out of that process is a leaner David's, a more focused David's ... I think it's a move from strength."

#4795. What night is van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’? New analysis says it’s in July.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

The swirling bluish-white mistrals, towering cypress trees, and glittery stars in Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” (1889) have captivated our imaginations for generations. Despite never selling a painting during his lifetime, van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” has since inspired a hit song in 1972, draws visitors to the Museum of Modern Art in New York City, and adorns everything from journals to scarves and tote bags.

But what particular night above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France, did the troubled post-impressionist painter actually put on the canvas? A new analysis points to July 22, 1889. During the early morning hours at that time of year, a waning crescent moon, the planet Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in Taurus appeared close together—all of which are seen in the painting. A study published today in the journal Scientific Reports details the findings.

According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, van Gogh was inspired by the view from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in southern France. He spent twelve months there 1889–1890, seeking reprieve from his mental illnesses and was also diagnosed with a form of epilepsy.

Like many of his paintings that year, “The Starry Night” was created in several sessions during the day, under different weather conditions. The village nestled below the hills was also based on other views, and could not be seen from his window. Although van Gogh reconstructed some of the features in the night sky as he saw them, he also altered the celestial shapes and added a sense of glow.

“Van Gogh assigned an emotional language to night and nature that took them far from their actual appearances,” the museum writes. “Dominated by vivid blues and yellows applied with gestural verve and immediacy, ‘The Starry Night’ also demonstrates how inseparable van Gogh’s vision was from the new procedures of painting he had devised, in which color and paint describe a world outside the artwork even as they telegraph their own status as, merely, color and paint.”

Using astronomy to pinpoint which day inspired the painting began with UCLA art historian Albert Boime in 1984 and Harvard University astronomer and astrophysicist Charles Whitney in 1986. The two scholars reached conflicting conclusions, and neither of them displaced the prevailing religious and symbolic interpretations of the painting.

Historically, the painting has been dated based on a letter van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18, 1889. However, the waning gibbous moon from mid-June does not account for the slender crescent seen in the painting. By July 22, 1889, the Hyades star cluster rose in the eastern sky alongside Venus, and the moon’s shape looked closer to the crescent on the canvas. This new study uses observational geometry to find an answer.

“To an observational astronomer, the cluster of stars in ‘The Starry Night’ is the Hyades in Taurus,” said study co-author Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim.  “But the moment I read the earlier studies explaining it as Aries, I heard the painting calling to me.” Kim is the CEO at South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a PhD candidate at Kyung Hee University.

For this research, the team combined astronomical simulations and measurements of star positions, with historical weather records, and van Gogh’s own letters. They also used earlier sketches of the landscape to reconstruct the narrow field of view represented in “The Starry Night.”

According to the team, the crescent moon, Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus would have been visible around dawn on July 22, 1889. This configuration generally corresponds to what is portrayed in the painting. Weather records suggest that the night sky around July 22 would have been clear enough for this astronomical arrangement to be visible. Additionally, the frequent mentions of cicadas in van Gogh’s letters suggest that the region was seeing a period of hot weather, which matches the recorded temperatures in July 1889 better than in June.

The team also verified that the painter’s field of view in another painting titled “Starry Night over the Rhône” (1888) with photographs taken on site was painted when the sky was in Aries. Using the same method, the team also believes that the stars in “Café Terrace at Night” (1888) may be the Summer Triangle.

“I hope this work prompts a renewed, multidisciplinary look at the sequence and working methods of the Saint-Rémy paintings, and at Van Gogh’s movements that summer,” Kim said. “Our conversation with Van Gogh’s other skies is still ongoing. We plan to tell those stories one by one.”

#5649. Redwood and Rivian set to expand their EV battery recycling partnership

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Redwood Materials and Rivian are expanding their existing EV battery partnership, adding a fresh battery material recycling effort to the EV startup’s Normal, Illinois, vehicle factory and, potentially, other Rivian-owned sites.

As Tesla co-founder JB Straubel’s second act, Redwood Materials had a lot to live up to. Its plan to give used EV batteries a second life as energy storage for public- and private-sector buildings, construction sites, micro-grids, and (sigh) data centers makes plenty of sense, though. Enough sense, anyway, that Rivian decided to put the idea to work as it built out its R2 production line at the former Diamond-Star Mitsubishi plant in Normal. Back in April, the upstart EV brand signed on for 10 MWh worth of energy storage using repurposed Rivian battery packs – but that deal was just the beginning.

The new, expanded partnership between the two companies will see Redwood collect, sort, and recycle Rivian’s battery production scrap materials, putting them safely back into the manufacturing process with minimal waste and at a fraction of the cost of virgin material. What’s more, the new deal will see retired Rivian batteries put to use in other stationary energy projects before eventually recycling some 99% of those batteries’ critical materials (nickel, cobalt, copper, and lithium).

With the recent launch of what is sure to be the more popular, more affordable R2, that’s suddenly a much bigger deal than it might have been last year.

“Every Rivian battery pack that comes back to us is a potential source of domestic grid energy storage in addition to critical materials,” explains Straubel. “Redwood’s unique ability to integrate and redeploy Rivian batteries with our technology is an opportunity to enable fast, flexible, and more cost-efficient energy storage. As the demand and cost for energy keep rising, working with companies like Rivian is how we help meet these challenges.”

Rivian is the latest major EV brand to enter into a formal battery recycling partnership with Redwood, with the list of others including BMW, Ford, Toyota, and Volvo.

#5228. Coffee calories have jumped nearly 40%. Sugar isn't the biggest culprit

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Americans are getting more calories from their daily coffee, even though the amount they drink has barely changed. Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University have identified a surprising reason for the increase: non-dairy creamer, which has overtaken sugar and milk as the largest contributor to added calories in coffee.

The study, published online in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, examined dietary information from 30,000 American adults aged 20 and older. Between 2003 and 2018, average coffee consumption stayed relatively constant at approximately 13 ounces per day. However, the average number of calories consumed from coffee increased from 43 to 60 per day, a rise of nearly 40%. By 2018, the leading sources of added coffee calories were non-dairy creamer, sugar or syrup, and dairy milk.

"Coffee consumption has been associated with several health benefits. However, coffee is often accompanied by the addition of sugar and cream, which can contribute to an increased consumption of calories from added sugar and saturated fat," said Fang Fang Zhang, senior author of the study and Neely Family Professor at the Friedman School. "The more we know about what people are consuming, the better we can understand the link between nutrition and overall health."

How Researchers Tracked Changes in Coffee Habits

To investigate how Americans' coffee habits evolved, the research team examined eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), covering 2003 through 2018. The nationally representative dataset included 30,000 adults aged 20 and older, each of whom completed two 24-hour dietary recalls.

Researchers separated coffee beverages into two broad groups: non-combination beverages and those containing additional ingredients. They then classified the additions into several categories, including dairy milk, half-and-half, dairy cream, non-dairy milk, non-dairy creamer, sugar/syrup, sugar substitutes, and other ingredients such as alcohol and cocoa powder.

Some participants reported consuming pre-mixed coffee beverages/powder, making it necessary to estimate the amounts of individual ingredients they contained. To do this, the researchers used the USDA Food and Nutrition Database for Dietary Studies corresponding to each survey cycle, along with linked Standard Reference codes.

Non-Dairy Creamer Consumption More Than Doubled

The analysis showed that Americans' overall coffee intake, including coffee consumed without additions, remained largely unchanged between 2003 and 2018. What changed was the amount and type of ingredients being mixed into those beverages.

In 2018, dairy milk was the most commonly added ingredient by weight, averaging 21.4 grams per day. Non-dairy creamer followed at 8.1 grams per day, while sugar accounted for 4.4 grams per day. Consumption of dairy milk, sugar, and sugar substitutes showed no statistically significant changes over the study period.

Non-dairy additions, however, followed a very different pattern. Their average consumption rose from 3.65 grams per day in 2003 to 10.5 grams per day in 2018. Much of this growth came from non-dairy creamer, which increased from 3.37 to 8 grams per day. Non-dairy milk consumption also climbed sharply, rising from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

Other coffee additions increased slightly as well, with an average gain of 0.5 grams per day.

Why Non-Dairy Creamer Now Adds More Calories Than Sugar

Although dairy milk remained the most common coffee addition by weight, non-dairy creamer had become the leading source of added calories by 2018.

On average, non-dairy creamer contributed 18.5 calories per day, compared with 16 calories from sugar and 11 calories from dairy milk. In 2003, non-dairy creamer had contributed just 7.71 calories per day, illustrating how dramatically its role in Americans' coffee consumption had changed.

"We found that non-dairy creamer being added to coffee has now passed both sugar and dairy for its contribution to the total energy consumed from coffee. Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense. This could explain the increased calories that non-dairy creamer is contributing," said Yongyi Pan, the study's lead author and a Ph.D. candidate at the Friedman School. "Even though it has been on the market since the 1950s, non-dairy creamer has increased in popularity in recent years as consumers seek plant-based or dairy alternatives."

Sleep, Work Hours, and Coffee Consumption

The researchers also investigated whether coffee consumption differed across groups of adults based on their daily routines and overall diet quality.

They found that people who slept fewer hours generally drank more coffee than those who slept longer. A similar pattern emerged among adults who worked longer hours, who tended to consume more coffee than those with shorter work schedules.

However, coffee consumption was comparable among people with higher and lower overall diet quality, suggesting that drinking more or less coffee was not strongly associated with how healthy their broader diets were.

The researchers acknowledged that the study relied on participants recalling what they had eaten and drunk, which can introduce measurement errors. Because food and beverage consumption can vary considerably from day to day, self-reported dietary information does not always perfectly reflect people's usual habits. The research team took steps to improve the reliability of its estimates.

Co-author Bingjie Zhou contributed to the research while pursuing a Ph.D. at the Friedman School.

Norwegian forward might look to Harry Kane for inspiration on the pitch, but when north London derby comes around, she needs no lessons on what the rivalry means

“It means more than just three points. It’s for the whole club. It’s for the whole place. It’s for the whole of Tottenham.” One only needs to be in the presence of Tottenham Cathinka Tandberg for a few minutes to feel the passion that emanates from her when it comes to this weekend’s north London derby. As a lifelong Spurs fan, the forward understands more than most the intensity of the rivalry that exists between her side and Arsenal, one that she cannot wait to experience once again on Saturday.

“It will just be an incredible feeling,” she tells Moving the Goalposts. “I think you will feel a few nerves that you maybe don’t feel in a normal game but that’s good.

“All the Spurs fans hate Arsenal and Arsenal hate Spurs. That’s how it is. You want to show that you’re the best team – simple as that. You just want to fight for the club, the badge and for the people around you.”

It has been a significant 13 months for the 22-year-old who made her dream move to London in September 2025. After catching the eye as a goalscorer in the Damallsvenskan at first Linköping and then Hammarby, the Norwegian was signed by Martin Ho on deadline day to bolster the team’s attacking options. She made her mark on her first start, scoring a spectacular 40-yard goal at Everton.

“I settled really well,” she reflects. “I’m really enjoying it and want to push myself forward. It’s been a tough start [to this season] and I haven’t really got what I wanted. But there’s loads of games left. I’m still young. I’m still improving and learning. I just have to trust the process and trust what I’m surrounded by.

“It’s a big difference from Sweden,” she continues. “You meet World Cup players week in, week out, so you have to perform every week if you want results. I feel like my physicality has changed a lot. I’ve got stronger and fitter. Playing under Martin has made me think more away from the goals. I think more about how I play with the ball, how I press and all of those things that I maybe didn’t focus on before … it’s a bit like Harry Kane. The older he gets, he has developed his game as well. He needed to be deeper in the pitch because that’s how they wanted to play and then he has to be better with the ball. That’s a bit how I also need to be. I can’t only just stand in the box. I need to be good everywhere else.”

Spurs have been transformed under Ho and finished fifth last season. However, with success comes expectation, ambitions that Tandberg clearly shares but with a sense of perspective.

“Tottenham has always been a Champions League club for me,” she says. “But I’m a fan so I can’t really talk for the other players. When I came to the club, I said that I want to get top three. But you also need to be realistic and to see where the team has been before. The transformation that Martin has done in a season is unbelievable. I don’t think anyone else could do that. That tells you everything about him. He’s one of the greatest coaches I’ve worked with and I think everyone that comes here, one of the reasons is because of him. We all trust him and the process.”

That faith is currently being tested. After an unbeaten start, Tottenham have suffered back-to-back defeats, conceding six goals to both London City Lionesses and Chelsea.

“You can call it a dip in form but I still feel like we also showed our qualities in those games,” Tandberg says. “It’s just about ourselves. We maybe need to change our mentality a bit, find that fire and dig a bit deeper when we play those top teams. I feel like maybe it’s good that it’s come because it had to at some point. It’s about how we respond.

#4577. Why Living with Endometriosis May Double Your Odds of Migraine

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

In a new analysis, researchers found females with endometriosis were 2.3 times more likely to also have migraine, and they said the two conditions may share a pain pathway involving nerves and inflammation.Recognizing that connection earlier could lead to coordinated care for both conditions, the researchers said.The analysis, presented at the American Neurological Association (ANA) Annual Meeting in San Diego on October 20, included more than 13 million females. #5166. To Sell or Rent? How To Choose the Best Path for Your House.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Selling your house is a big step, and you want it to go smoothly. And with houses taking a little longer to sell these days, renting it out might start to sound like a nice backup plan.

But the reality is, while renting can work well for some people, it’s also a big commitment. So before you decide, it helps to see the whole picture first.

Lately, some sellers are choosing to hang on to their houses and rent them out instead. According to Parcl, around 9% of homes for rent right now were previously up for sale.

Some homeowners put their houses on the market expecting a faster process than they got or a certain price buyers didn’t respond to. And when things didn’t go exactly as planned, they decided to rent instead. As Kara Ng, Senior Economist at Zillow, explains:

“Bargaining power is tilting toward buyers and homes are taking longer to sell, making renting out a property one way to buy time rather than compete aggressively on price.”

But here’s the important distinction. Just because some sellers are choosing to rent doesn’t mean selling isn’t working. In many cases, the difference comes down to how well a house is priced, prepared, and marketed for today’s buyers.

Parcl data shows where these “accidental landlords” – owners who planned to sell but decided to rent instead – are most common (see graph below):

They make up around 17% of homes for rent in places like Sherman and Houston, TX, but less than 4% in markets like Des Moines, IA and Bloomington, IN.

That’s a pretty big difference. And it shows why you have to know what’s happening in your neighborhood. Your market, your asking price, and your strategy all play a role in how your sale goes.

A local agent can show you how houses like yours are selling, what strategies work best, and help you weigh your options – not to push you to sell, but so you at least have the full picture based on today’s market. And part of making an informed decision is understanding what renting your house would actually take.

While renting might sound like easy passive income on paper, in practice, it’s a hands-on job with rules to follow. As Invest West Management puts it:

“Even if you never intended to become a landlord, the law still sees you as one — and you must meet all legal obligations that come with that role. This often includes registering the tenancy, providing tenants with certain disclosures, and ensuring the property meets safety standards.”

You’d also be the one chasing missed rent payments, fixing damage between tenants, and covering unexpected repairs. Not to mention fielding weekend calls about leaky dishwashers or broken AC units.

There are expenses beyond regular maintenance and repairs, too. According to Bankrate, these are a few of the biggest:

Insurance premiums (landlord insurance typically costs about 25% more than a standard homeowners policy)

Management fees (if you hire a property manager, they usually charge around 10% of the rent)

Gaps between tenants, when you pay the mortgage without rental income

If you want to be a landlord and you’re prepared for the responsibility, renting may be a great fit. But if you’d really rather sell, don’t let today’s market convince you that you can’t.

A great agent can make it happen. The key is having an open conversation up front with your agent about pricing and how to market your house. Nailing those details from day 1 helps your house make a great first impression, so renting doesn’t have to be your fallback.

#746. Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.
