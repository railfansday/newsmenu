#20. EU proposes giving Frontex border agency new major powers

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

The European Commission has unveiled a new plan to give Frontex more resources to ensure the return of irregular migrants, including by supporting non-EU countries. The move comes after the tragedy in Ceuta.

The European Commission on Tuesdayunveiled a new proposal to beef up the powers of the EU border agency in order to empower it to support the return of irregular migrants.

The proposed legislation gives the agency, called Frontex, more resources to act beyond EU territory.

The plan would give the agency the power to support non-EU countries to return migrants to their countries of origin.

The proposal comes as EU member states increasingly focus on returns, saying too many people who are asked to leave the bloc remain.

The new role for Frontex comes after the humanitarian crisis in Ceuta this summer.

The tragedy in the Spanish exclave was seized upon by some EU member states as an example of where the EU could do more to block people from reaching the EU.

Italy, for example, placed some temporary border controls on travel from Spain.

Spain's prime minister at the time criticized several EU member states for their "selfish" response to the influx of migrants to Ceuta.

"Today we propose that Frontex becomes the central EU actor on returns," Magnus Brunner, commissioner for internal affairs ​and migration, told reporters.

"It will be able to act faster, with a quick reaction force that can ⁠deploy within ⁠24 hours and spot risks ⁠early, before an ​event becomes a crisis," Brunner added.

The ability to deport migrants from non-EU countries means that Frontex could, for instance, deport a Bangladeshi national from Libya, where many migrants start the final leg of their journey into the bloc.

The plan envisages gradually expanding Frontex's standing corps to 30,000 officers by creating a new "European border and return reserve" of 17,000 officers within the next 15 years.

Those officers would remain at the disposal of member states and could be deployed when needed, the commission said in a statement.

The proposal would also create a new EU Return Coordination Center within Frontex and expand the agency's role across the entire deportation process.

It would also include a new online monitoring center to track border-related developments on social media and support action against content linked to border security risks.

Frontex has been responsible for guarding the external borders of the EU as well as ensuring the rights of new arrivals.

It has been criticized for its lack of accountability in the past.

The planned reform shrinks the scope of the agency's internal rights watchdog, with complaints to be referred to national authorities.

"The Commission is pandering to populism and the far right on migration," said Tineke Strik, a Dutch MEP from the Greens/EFA group.

"We need a human rights-based approach to border management and Frontex plays a key role. But instead the Commission ​risks turning Frontex into a European version of ICE," she said.

#1527. How Ferguson saved himself from the brink of the sack at Man Utd

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

When Manchester United were drawn away to Nottingham Forest in the third round of the FA Cup in January 1990, there was a sense that this was the end for the young Scottish manager Alex Ferguson.

It remains as true now as it did then that you cannot predict the future, and that very often attempts at doing so fall miserably short of the mark.

Yet at that time you would have been as close to certain as possible that the writing was on the wall for Ferguson.

If you were able to time travel and went to Manchester, January 1990, walked into a pub and started to explain how Ferguson would lead United into an unmatched era of English footballing dominance, winning the treble in nine years time with a core group of local boys and then cementing himself among the greatest managers of all time you’d be at the very least laughed at.

In pure titles alone it is beyond impressive, an almost impossible record of success: 13 Premier League titles, five FA Cups and two Champions Leagues. That is before considering the way in which he won them and where the club was before his arrival.

But things got much worse for Ferguson and United before they got better. Much, much worse.

You’ve doubtless heard it mentioned before how he was almost fired, but you may not appreciate just how close he came. Here’s the story of one goal in Nottingham, January 1990.

When Ferguson arrived at Old Trafford in November 1986, he was considered one of the brightest young managers in the game, having led Aberdeen through an unprecedented period of success culminating in their European Cup Winner’s Cup triumph in 1982-83.

He remains the last manager to win the Scottish Premiership with a club other than Rangers and Celtic.

The United job was no small challenge. Ferguson replaced Ron Atkinson, the most successful post-Matt Busby manager who, despite winning two FA Cups and finishing in the top four every season, was never able to bring the First Division back to Old Trafford.

With a third of the season gone, United lay in 21st and Ferguson began uprooting things straight away with his first target the drinking culture at the club.

“I had to get rid of the idea United was a drinking club,” he said to David Meek, Author of Sir Alex Ferguson.

“I had enormous difficulty with Norman Whiteside and Paul McGrath.

“[Drinking] created conflict with my concept of a Manchester United Player.”

In his first season, Ferguson dragged United to an 11th placed finish despite their poor start and things began looking up.

Then, in 1987-88 Ferguson led United to a second-place finish. They ended up nine points behind leaders Liverpool but there was not much of a substantive title challenge with Liverpool leading nearly all season.

Either way, Ferguson’s revolution was underway with Viv Anderson, Steve Bruce and Brian McClair brought in by the Scotsman.

Yet the winds of change turned the following season. Far from the title challenge expected, United finished 11th in 1988-89. No doubt a sackable offence today, the old First Division was much more volatile in terms of positions – but regardless, there was concern amongst the Old Trafford faithful.

The new summer window meant more players for Fergie’s arsenal; out with Whiteside and McGrath, in with Gary Pallister, Paul Ince and more.

But hopes of a title challenge once again collapsed in on themselves as the season progressed. Despite a 4-1 home win on the opening day of the season to champions Arsenal, United’s form was inconsistent and worsening.

It culminated in a 5-1 destruction of the big-spending Red Devils at Maine Road by Manchester City in September and the knives began to be sharpened.

It’s the 30th anniversary of City beating United 5-1 in the Maine Road Massacre.

“Ferguson said he drove home and went straight to bed, putting his head under the pillow in complete shock,” Meek writes.

And well he might have; the media smelt blood and the fans had had enough.

Infamously, a banner reading “3 years of excuses and it’s still crap… tara Fergie” could be seen at Old Trafford in the following months.

With pressure building, the general consensus was that Ferguson would be sacked when, not if, United lost to Nottingham Forest in the FA Cup.

“Martin Edwards [United’s former chairman] and Sir Bobby Charlton have tried to rewrite history by claiming that wasn’t the case,” former United board member Michael Knighton said in The Mirror.

“But when we played QPR at Old Trafford on New Year’s Day, I was stood in the boardroom at half-time when Martin sidled up behind me and whispered, ‘We’ve got to be talking about the manager’.”

The stakes, then, for the match against Forest could not have been higher.

A cup run would provide Ferguson some much-needed breathing space and earn him a distraction from United’s league form.

Forest was no easy task: a decade on from their European Cup double, Clough’s side were still an impressive unit from which the FA Cup was the only thing missing.

The match was a tight and nervy affair, The City Ground packed to the rafters.

Watching footage of that game back, so much of is feels old and unusual, namely, the pitch, which was just a field of mud. The ball bobbled along it, occasionally bouncing up unprovoked.

But in an act that proved to be timeless, Ferguson managed to deliver a victory when all the odds seem stacked against him.

The goal came in the 56th minute when Mark Hughes, stunning hair and all, found himself in what we’d now call a half-space on the outside of Forest’s box. He spotted the run of young Mark Robins, an Oldham boy who had developed in the United youth system and the youngest player on the pitch.

With the outside of his boot, Hughes curled the ball perfectly past two Forest defenders, bouncing straight onto the head of Robins. Stuart Pearce desperately tried to stop him, but it was too late and Robins dutifully nodded home.

It was the one moment of quality from either side all game. Several times Forest came close to equalising, the ball at one point seemingly heading for goal before the bumps in the pitch in front of United’s goal, almost as if by some divine intervention, sent the ball wide.

Later, the ball was in the United net and the Forest fans celebrated but even before VAR such celebrations were cut short, the linesman’s flag was up to suggest that United’s keeper was impeded and the referee agreed.

In the end, there was nothing anyone could do to stop it: United won and Ferguson kept his job.

“Some joy at last for Alex Ferguson,” the commentator cries out. He didn’t know the half of it.

Currently, those with a Bilt credit card can earn 4% back on spending in the form of Bilt Cash, in addition to the points that they earn. Starting on your next card account anniversary, Bilt credit cards are being moved to what’s being described as a “simpler” set of benefits.

The most significant change is that by default, everyone will earn up to 1.25x Bilt Points on their rent or mortgage payment, as Bilt claims it’s simplifying the program and retiring Flexible Bilt Cash mode, which earns 4% back in Bilt Cash on everyday spend (in addition to points).

If that’s your preference, you just have to opt out of the new benefits in your card settings before your card anniversary date, and then you’ll keep any eligible annual credits, and won’t be able to switch between Flexible Bilt Cash and housing-only mode.

Coffee inspires passionate debate among its drinkers, including the best way to brew and drink it. There are die-hard espresso devotees, cold brew enthusiasts, and those who prioritize push-button convenience above all.

Among the coffee intelligentsia, a few brewing methods rise to the top. Drip coffee isn’t maligned, but it isn’t how most of the professionals I spoke with make their morning cup, even if most Americans do. I asked 10 coffee experts — roasters, cafe owners, educators, barista champions, and one former president of the Specialty Coffee Association — to rank seven brewing methods: AeroPress, automatic drip machine, espresso, French press, K-Cup, moka pot and pour-over.

Pour-over earned the top spot by a healthy margin.

AeroPress got high marks from the coffee experts I spoke with.

#599. These tiny snakes roll four times faster than they slither

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Tiny snakes found in Southeast Asia have developed an extraordinary way to escape danger. Instead of relying on the familiar side-to-side motion of slithering, dwarf reedsnakes can rapidly roll across the ground, reaching speeds about four times faster than their usual movement.

Scientists have now uncovered how these small reptiles accomplish such an unusual feat. By studying video recordings captured from multiple angles, researchers discovered that the snakes coordinate their movements with remarkable precision. While the rear portions of their bodies rotate rapidly, their heads remain relatively steady, facing forward and staying at a nearly constant height.

[See link to video after the end of the article.]

The findings reveal how an animal can achieve an unexpected form of movement through specialized behavior and body control, even without the physical structures normally needed for rolling.

Rolling might seem like an effective way to travel quickly, but it presents a major challenge for living creatures.

A wheel can spin continuously around a fixed axle, allowing it to rotate without disturbing whatever it supports. Animals, however, have no comparable mechanism separating their rotating bodies from their heads.

If an animal's entire body spins together, its head and sensory organs must also rotate. This can make it difficult to maintain balance, adjust body position, and steer in the intended direction. As rolling speed increases, these problems become even harder to manage.

Yet dwarf reedsnakes belonging to the genus Pseudorabdion have found a way around these limitations.

These small Southeast Asian snakes are among the few animals known to deliberately roll as a means of escaping potential predators. Their unusual behavior has appeared in field guides and was previously documented during a field observation, but scientists had not fully understood the movements that made it possible.

Tiny Snakes Roll Four Times Faster Than They Slither

To investigate this remarkable escape strategy, researchers filmed dwarf reedsnakes from several angles and carefully examined how different parts of their bodies moved during rolling.

The results revealed impressive speeds. While rolling, the snakes reached peak forward velocities ranging from 1.72 to 3.69 meters per second, approximately four times their maximum speed when moving through lateral undulation, the familiar side-to-side motion used by most snakes.

Despite traveling so quickly, the snakes were able to follow a narrow racetrack measuring just 20 centimeters across, demonstrating that their rolling movements are not simply uncontrolled tumbling.

Closer examination of the recordings revealed a surprisingly intricate sequence of movements.

Rather than spinning their entire bodies together, the snakes alternate between rolling on one side and swinging a curved section of their bodies downward beside their heads. The researchers called this downward sweeping movement a "stroke."

During each successive cycle, the side touching the ground switches, and the stroke changes direction from left to right or right to left. By repeating this carefully timed pattern, the snakes generate continuous forward rolling motion.

The Remarkable Secret Behind Their High-Speed Escape

One of the most surprising discoveries involved what happens to the snakes' heads during these rapid movements.

Although the rear portions of their bodies undergo substantial rotation, the snakes keep their heads pointed in the direction they are traveling. Rather than spinning along with the rest of the body, the head remains comparatively stable.

The researchers also found that the snakes maintain a relatively consistent head height above the ground. This stabilization becomes especially noticeable at higher speeds, when the snakes tend to hold their heads elevated and minimize upward and downward movements.

Keeping the head steady while the rest of the body rotates may help the animals maintain control and navigate effectively during their rapid escapes.

This ability is particularly remarkable because the snakes accomplish it without a mechanical separation between the rotating and stabilized portions of their bodies.

What Rolling Snakes Reveal About Evolution

The discovery demonstrates how sophisticated coordination can allow animals to overcome physical limitations that might otherwise make certain types of movement impractical.

Although dwarf reedsnakes lack anything resembling a wheel's axle, they effectively divide their bodies into regions with different functions. Much of the body performs the powerful rotational movements needed for propulsion, while the head remains stabilized to support controlled travel.

This unusual strategy allows the snakes to combine speed, directional control, and rapid body rotation in a way that would be difficult to predict from their anatomy alone.

More broadly, the findings highlight an important principle of animal evolution: physical structure does not necessarily determine the full range of movements an animal can perform. Innovative behaviors and precise muscular coordination can open up entirely new possibilities, allowing creatures to accomplish feats that their body shapes might initially suggest are impossible.

#771. A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

The E-Sauce is based on Priority’s existing Sauce, a chunky-tired, belt-driven, single-speed bicycle that was originally expected to appeal to a fairly narrow group of riders. (Fun fact: it also comes in an 8-speed internally geared hub variant, known as the Hot Sauce.)

Instead of living out its product life in obscurity as a bike between niches, Priority says the bike found a much wider audience than they originally expected thanks to its relaxed riding style, distinctive looks, and ability to handle everything from city streets to trails.

And so, naturally, now they stuck a motor on it.

Unlike many of the e-bike companies we cover, Priority didn’t spring up during the e-bike boom with a catalog of imported electric bicycles. The Manhattan-based company was founded by legit cyclists and spent years building traditional bicycles before increasingly embracing electrification. Its calling card has long been bike-shop-quality construction paired with unusually low-maintenance components, particularly Gates carbon belt drives, which the company features on every model it offers, right down to their cute little kids’ bikes.

That same formula carries over to the E-Sauce, which uses a single-speed Gates belt drive rather than a derailleur and chain. Power comes from a 750W rear hub motor, and there’s a throttle for riders who don’t feel like pedaling. The bike ships configured as Class 2 with a 20 mph (32 km/h) speed limit, though pedal assist can be unlocked for speeds of up to 28 mph (45 km/h).

Be sure to check your local regulations to see how that impacts where you can ride. In most states though, the bike will be a 100% street-legal Class 2 e-bike.

Interestingly, Priority is using both torque and cadence sensing – a move we’re starting to see more of.

The torque sensor provides more natural-feeling pedal assist up to 20 mph, while the bike can switch over to cadence-based assistance above 20 mph, meaning they don’t have to pedal like crazy to still get good motor output at higher speeds.

That gives riders the more bicycle-like response of a torque sensor at typical riding speeds without making it difficult to keep the motor working as the bike approaches its 28 mph maximum.

Remember, of course, that the bike only ships in 20 mph mode, and so this will only affect riders who do choose to open it up for more speed to reach the Class 3 limit of 28 mph.

The rest of the bike leans into the Sauce’s do-anything personality. There are wide 27.5 x 2.4-inch WTB Intersector tires and 100mm of front suspension travel, giving it considerably more capability on rough roads and light trails than your typical commuter.

Priority also seems intent on making the E-Sauce surprisingly useful as a utility bike. Standard rear rack mounts work with conventional racks, including Priority’s MIK-compatible rack for carrying panniers, cargo, or even a child seat. Up front, the company’s new frame-mounted Headway Mount will support front racks and other accessories. The bike includes built-in LED lighting, but offers clever innovations like seat-tube integration for the tail light so you don’t have to worry about a light getting whacked off at a crowded bike rack.

Priced at $2,199, the E-Sauce obviously isn’t going to win a watt-per-dollar battle against the endless sea of $1,000-ish 750W e-bikes. But that’s also missing the point. The E-Sauce is a different type of product. You’re getting a belt-driven bike designed around nearly zero maintenance, built by an actual bicycle company run by real bike people, yet still sold with the convenience and pricing advantages of a direct-to-consumer model.

Priority says that simplicity is central to the bike: a smooth and quiet Gates belt drive, punchy hydraulic disc brakes, quality components, minimal upkeep, and enough versatility to serve as anything from a trail toy to a commuter or light-duty cargo bike.

The original Sauce apparently taught Priority that a fun, simple bicycle can have a much broader audience than expected. With a 750W motor and throttle now thrown into the mix, I have a feeling the E-Sauce might cast an even wider net.
