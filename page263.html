#5364. Scientists discover how deep sleep builds muscle, burns fat, and boosts the brain

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A good night's sleep does much more than leave you feeling refreshed. During deep sleep, the body releases growth hormone, a powerful chemical messenger that helps build muscle, strengthen bones, and regulate fat metabolism. For teenagers, getting enough sleep is especially important because growth hormone plays a major role in reaching their full height potential.

Scientists have long known that insufficient sleep can interfere with growth hormone production. What remained unclear was exactly how the brain controls this process, particularly during non-REM sleep, the deep, restorative stage that typically dominates the earlier part of the night.

Now, researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, have uncovered the brain circuitry behind this relationship. In a study published in the journal Cell, the team identified the neurons that regulate growth hormone release during sleep and discovered a previously unknown feedback system that helps maintain a delicate balance between hormone levels and wakefulness.

The discovery reveals that the relationship works in both directions. Sleep stimulates growth hormone production, but growth hormone also sends signals back to the brain that influence when we wake up.

The findings could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to treating sleep disorders associated with metabolic diseases such as diabetes, as well as neurological conditions including Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.

"People know that growth hormone release is tightly related to sleep, but only through drawing blood and checking growth hormone levels during sleep," said study first author Xinlu Ding, a postdoctoral fellow in UC Berkeley's Department of Neuroscience and the Helen Wills Neuroscience Institute. "We're actually directly recording neural activity in mice to see what's going on. We are providing a basic circuit to work on in the future to develop different treatments."

Why Deep Sleep Matters for Growth and Metabolism

Growth hormone is best known for its role in childhood development, but its importance extends well into adulthood. It supports the maintenance of muscle and bone, helps the body regulate how it uses stored fat, and influences blood sugar levels.

When sleep is consistently disrupted, growth hormone regulation can suffer. Because this hormone helps control glucose and fat metabolism, inadequate sleep may contribute to an increased risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

To understand how the brain coordinates these processes, the Berkeley researchers focused on a region called the hypothalamus. Located deep within the brain, this small but essential control center helps regulate hunger, body temperature, hormone production, and the daily cycle of sleeping and waking. Its basic functions have been preserved throughout mammalian evolution.

Within the hypothalamus, the researchers examined neurons that produce growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) and two distinct types of neurons that produce somatostatin.

These chemical messengers have opposing functions. GHRH encourages growth hormone secretion, while somatostatin suppresses it. Together, they help control how much growth hormone enters the bloodstream, largely by regulating its release from the pituitary gland, a small hormone-producing structure at the base of the brain.

The scientists also investigated how growth hormone influences the locus coeruleus, a region in the brainstem involved in alertness, attention, thinking, and the drive to explore new or unfamiliar experiences.

Disruptions in the activity of this region have been linked to several psychiatric and neurological disorders.

"Understanding the neural circuit for growth hormone release could eventually point toward new hormonal therapies to improve sleep quality or restore normal growth hormone balance," said Daniel Silverman, a UC Berkeley postdoctoral fellow and study co-author. "There are some experimental gene therapies where you target a specific cell type. This circuit could be a novel handle to try to dial back the excitability of the locus coeruleus, which hasn't been talked about before."

Scientists Map the Brain Circuit That Controls Growth Hormone

The research was conducted in the laboratory of Yang Dan, a professor of neuroscience and of molecular and cell biology at UC Berkeley.

To investigate how the circuitry works, the team studied mice using electrodes implanted in their brains. The scientists recorded neural activity while using light to stimulate specific neurons in the hypothalamus, allowing them to observe how different parts of the system responded.

Mice are particularly useful for studying sleep regulation because their sleep patterns differ significantly from those of humans. Rather than sleeping through one long stretch each night, mice typically sleep in brief episodes lasting only several minutes, scattered throughout the day and night.

This gave the researchers repeated opportunities to examine how neural activity and growth hormone regulation changed as the animals moved between sleeping and waking.

Using advanced techniques to trace connections between brain cells, the team discovered that GHRH and somatostatin behave differently depending on the stage of sleep.

During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the stage commonly associated with vivid dreaming, activity involving both GHRH and somatostatin increases. Although these hormones normally exert opposing effects, their coordinated activity during this stage contributes to increased growth hormone release.

During non-REM sleep, the pattern changes. Somatostatin activity decreases, reducing its inhibitory influence, while GHRH activity rises moderately. This combination also promotes growth hormone secretion.

The discovery helps explain how different sleep stages can support the release of the same essential hormone through distinct patterns of brain activity.

Growth Hormone Also Helps Control When We Wake Up

Perhaps the most surprising finding was that growth hormone does not simply respond to signals from the sleeping brain. It also influences the brain circuits that control wakefulness.

As growth hormone gradually builds up during sleep, it stimulates neurons in the locus coeruleus, increasing their activity and helping promote the transition toward waking.

This creates a feedback loop in which sleep encourages growth hormone release, and the resulting hormone activity helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle.

The researchers also identified an unexpected complication. Although greater activity in the locus coeruleus generally promotes alertness, excessive stimulation can have the opposite effect.

In an earlier study published the same year, Silverman demonstrated that when neurons in this region become overexcited, they can paradoxically promote sleepiness rather than wakefulness.

Together, these findings reveal a carefully regulated system in which the brain must balance competing signals to maintain healthy sleep and hormone activity.

Scientists describe this type of self-regulating process as homeostasis, the body's ability to maintain stable internal conditions even as its environment and physiological demands change.

"This suggests that sleep and growth hormone form a tightly balanced system: Too little sleep reduces growth hormone release, and too much growth hormone can in turn push the brain toward wakefulness," Silverman said. "Sleep drives growth hormone release, and growth hormone feeds back to regulate wakefulness, and this balance is essential for growth, repair and metabolic health."

A Surprising Connection Between Sleep and Brain Health

The newly identified feedback system may have implications that extend beyond physical growth and metabolism.

Because the locus coeruleus helps regulate attention, alertness, and cognitive function, growth hormone's influence on this region suggests it could also play a role in how effectively the brain functions after waking.

This raises the possibility that maintaining healthy sleep and growth hormone rhythms may support both physical recovery and mental performance.

"Growth hormone not only helps you build your muscle and bones and reduce your fat tissue, but may also have cognitive benefits, promoting your overall arousal level when you wake up," Ding said.

By identifying the specific brain cells and signaling pathways involved, the researchers have provided a foundation for exploring therapies that target disrupted sleep and hormone regulation.

Such approaches could eventually prove useful for people with sleep disturbances related to metabolic disorders or neurological diseases. However, the experiments were conducted in mice, and additional research will be needed to determine how these mechanisms operate in humans and whether they can be safely targeted with treatments.

The findings underscore a fundamental principle of sleep biology: Rest is not simply a period when the brain switches off. It is an active, carefully coordinated process in which hormones and neural circuits constantly communicate to support growth, repair, metabolism, and wakefulness.

The research was supported by the Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI), which supported Dan as an HHMI investigator until the year of the study's publication, and the Pivotal Life Sciences Chancellor's Chair fund.

Dan holds the Pivotal Life Sciences Chancellor's Chair in Neuroscience.

Additional co-authors include Peng Zhong, Bing Li, Chenyan Ma, Lihui Lu, Grace Jiang, Zhe Zhang, Xiaolin Huang, Xun Tu, and Zhiyu Melissa Tian of UC Berkeley, along with Fuu-Jiun Hwang and Jun Ding of Stanford University.

#885. James McClean: ‘Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen’

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The former Republic of Ireland international on growing up in a community still reeling from the Troubles and the controversy that scarred his career

“The perception of me is that, basically, I am a madman,” James McClean says calmly. “I’m outspoken. I’m a loudmouth. I’m very opinionated and I hate all things British. That’s from people who don’t know me. The reality is quite the opposite. I’m pretty boring. I go training, go home and switch off. A lot of my days are quite bland, quite basic.”

McClean sounds as sane as he looks gaunt on a bleak Monday in Derry. He spent 14 years in English football and the fact that he is now at war with his own club, Derry City, can’t obscure the fact that he is the fifth-most-capped Republic of Ireland international, having played 103 times. “People define success in different ways,” he says, “but I’m the only footballer from the north of Ireland to win over 100 caps for Ireland. I played 150 Premier League games over five seasons, 800 professional games and won a lot of individual accolades. I count that as success and don’t look back with regrets.”

But there’s sadness, too, in the way his life and career were scarred by his refusal to wear the poppy when playing games around Remembrance Sunday in England. “It’s completely changed me as a person,” McClean says. “It’s made me a bit of a recluse because I don’t trust a lot of people now. I’ve been stung bad.”

McClean’s autobiography, The Walls, is one of the best sports books of the year. It’s a powerful and sometimes disturbing read elevated by McClean’s willingness to show himself in an unflattering light.

I have written a lot about Derry and boxing, and Bloody Sunday, but it helps to be back here to understand how McClean can respect the millions of soldiers who died in two world wars while refusing to forget how the British army killed so many Irish people in this city.

He comes from Creggan, the estate affected most on Bloody Sunday in 1972 when 14 unarmed civilians were killed by the British army in Derry. Six of the fatalities were from Creggan. In 2010, after the Saville report finally concluded that all the victims posed no threat, the British prime minister David Cameron apologised for the “unjustified and unjustifiable” massacre.

McClean was born in Derry in 1989 and he writes that “learning how to make a petrol bomb was one of my first life lessons. I was 11 before I’d properly mastered it.”

He pauses when asked how he would feel if any of his three daughters or his son, who is 11, ended up making petrol bombs. “I’m very thankful they don’t have to. It’s a different era now. I just want them to be proud to be Irish.”

The 37-year-old spells out the contrast with his youth. “I grew up in a very Republican community still dealing with the effects of the Troubles. You would see gunmen on the street. People got shot, kneecapped. To get access to my street you had to go through a lane where you sometimes saw masked men doing punishment beatings. But you weren’t scared. You knew you’d done nothing wrong. That was the norm … but it’s not normal.”

His biological father, Joe Kelly, was an alcoholic in Creggan and McClean spoke to him only once – when he shouted at him in the street to stay away. But he remembers a happy childhood and, when his professional career began with Derry City, “I was very carefree”.

McClean was 22 when Steve Bruce signed him for Sunderland in August 2011. He made his Premier League debut that December after Bruce was replaced by Martin O’Neill. McClean soon became a regular starter and that first season in English football was full of joy and praise. He was fast, skilful and disciplined. Apart from wasting money his biggest vice was typing his name on Twitter where he would revel in glowing reports of his play.

“It was amazing,” McClean says. “I never thought about the politics when I first got to England so up until November 2012, when the poppy thing started, I never experienced any bother.”

Everything changed when he told Sunderland he wouldn’t wear the poppy because it also represented the British army which had occupied Derry for so long. “There was never a decision for me to make,” he says. “Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen. It was a very difficult year and I felt a lot of that could have been handled better by the club. But Sunderland came out with a statement before the game which drew all eyes on me. It was there to protect the club. They basically said this is James’s personal decision but we, as a club, wholeheartedly support the poppy appeal.

“I wanted to make my own statement but I was constantly told by the club that: ‘No, no, it’ll die over. Just leave it.’ I’d just turned 23 and I thought the club had a duty of care to look after me and that’s what they were doing. I’d won the player of the year the previous season but I felt they hung me out to dry.”

McClean recalls that: “The next game was away to Fulham on a Sunday, on Sky. A threat was made that I would be shot if I came on to the pitch. I remember [his wife] Erin panicking. It sounds extreme and people might say: ‘Oh, wise up, you’re not going to be shot on TV.’ But the night before the game I was assigned two police officers to stand outside my door. So the Met police took it seriously and that made it more real.”

O’Neill brought him on in the second half and his every touch was booed – by the Fulham and Sunderland supporters. The vitriol poisoned his world but McClean kept searching for his name on Twitter. He became furious whenever, after a mutually respectful encounter with a member of the public, the person would go online to abuse him.

Sometimes McClean would track the person down and demand an apology. “Maybe that madness was always there, but it got stronger,” he notes.

McClean moved to Wigan before joining West Brom where, in “the best kind of madness” he earned £42,000 a week while he had his best spell in the Premier League. But as one poppy season blurred into another, the death threats kept coming, as did the warnings that Erin would be raped. McClean says: “My reaction was always to fight fire with fire. I went into some games in the wrong frame of mind. It wasn’t: ‘Can I help the team?’ It was: ‘I hope I score today so I can shush them up.’”

McClean admits: “There were days where I went looking for the conflict.” He posted a photograph of himself listening to his favourite Wolfe Tones song, The Broad Black Brimmer. “I knew exactly what I was doing,” McClean writes. “It was an IRA song – and I was sure it would rile a certain type of person up.”

By the time he reached Stoke, for whom he played 111 games between 2018 and 2021, his problems in English football reached a new low. “Brexit made the idiots louder” and “the poppy season seemed to be getting longer every year”. He had also made a stupid mistake during lockdown when he posted a photograph of himself, wearing a balaclava, with his two eldest children below a caption which read: ‘Today’s school lesson – History.’”

McClean thought he was being funny but his choice not to wear the poppy, in contrast, was heartfelt. “I thought others might have followed suit but it never turned out to be the case.”

Did he feel that a more rounded understating of his stance had emerged by the time he enjoyed his happiest seasons in English football, while playing for a Hollywood-tinged Wrexham from 2023 to earlier this year? “A wee bit, aye. When you explain it to footballers you’ve played with, and to people on a personal level, it opens their eyes and they say: ‘Oh, I understand that.’ But the overall British population? I don’t think so.”

Reflecting on his Premier League years, McClean marvels at the brilliance of Kevin De Bruyne and Eden Hazard. “You had to accept there were lads capable of doing things that were beyond you,” McClean writes. “But there were also games where I thought I was coming up against a physical strength that I found suspicious. Coming back into the Premier League after a break convinced me that some players had to be benefiting from some kind of performance-enhancing support.

“I remember coming up against one player that I’d encountered at Sunderland who I always felt that I’d been able to match in terms of his speed and power. While I’d been away from the Premier League, he’d gone to a bigger club, and now he was a different animal completely.”

These are startling allegations and, while McClean has no concrete evidence to support his doubts, he trusts his gut reaction. “I’ve always been very fanatical about my fitness,” he says. “But when you come against the top Premier League clubs, no matter what height, what size, they’re just faster, they’re stronger, and you’re thinking: ‘How is that possible?’”

After their daughter Willow was diagnosed with global development delay and autism, Erin persuaded him to be tested for autism. He was found to be “at the high-functioning end of the spectrum” but he still struggles with the fact that “I’ve probably got body dysmorphia or some kind of bulimia. Maybe it’s the autism thing, because I’m so strict with my diet 95% of the time. But when I can’t work out I binge. So if I’m travelling and flying, I’ll just eat that much shite to the point where I have to make myself sick. I still feel really guilty, because the next day I’ll punish myself in the gym – which is not healthy.”

Life has become even more complicated since he returned to Derry City in January. “I was meant to be coming home for the fairytale ending. But you quickly realise the promises are just bullshit.”

McClean has not played for the club since July and, asked about the situation, he says: “It’s not good. It’s a player against owner and there’s only one winner. I think that’s it in terms of Derry City. You never think you’ll be betrayed by your own so it’s painful.”

When contacted by the Guardian, a spokesman for Derry City said: “The club cannot speak to, or comment on, how James feels.” He also rejected the suggestion that Derry City had failed to fulfil any promises made to McClean.

Earlier this month, McClean said he couldn’t “understand or accept” the way he was being treated by the club and that he was fit to play despite being left out of the squad by interim manager Mark Connolly. Derry City then released a statement in which they said: “The club has sought to resolve this internal matter privately. Out of respect for confidentiality and the fact that this dispute involves sensitive medical details regarding James’ hip condition, the club have previously refrained from making any public statement on the matter.”

McClean insists he could play football elsewhere but how does he feel if this is the end of his career? “It doesn’t unsettle me because I’ve been boxing off and on for 10 years and in the past 18 months I’ve really thrown myself into it. I’m not going to start a pro boxing career because it’s just not feasible at my age. But I’ve been in contact with Misfits and they want me to fight.”

Misfits Boxing was set up by KSI and the Sauerland brothers, who made their name in the traditional fight game, and it features YouTubers and former athletes who want to become boxers. It is dismissed by boxing purists but McClean sounds enthusiastic about making his debut in the ring once his contract with Derry expires: “Boxing is giving me that purpose to wake up and train hard.”

What kind of boxer would he like to become? “I’m a pressure fighter. I like to be constantly in someone’s face because I’ve got the fitness to maintain that. I’m not saying I’m by any stretch of the imagination a [Gennady] Golovkin or a Canelo [Álvarez], but I love watching them. I’m not looking to have a career in boxing but three or four fights a year is perfect. The dream was always to be a footballer but this is a new excitement, a new challenge.”

As we prepare to step out into a rain-sodden Derry, there seems little chance of any reconciliation for him on the football field. But he looks up and his taut fighter’s face suddenly relaxes into a smile. “It could be a blessing,” McClean says softly. “It could end up a blessing in disguise.”

#4627. Walking, Yoga, or Zumba? Which Exercises Help Relieve Menopause Mood Symptoms

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Physical activity may help support mood and improve quality of life during the menopause transition, according to a study published on October 13 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society.

This review of 12 clinical trials involving over 1,000 people ages 40 to 65 found that both aerobic and mind-body exercises may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved well-being.

It is common for individuals to experience psychological symptoms during menopause, including depression, anxiety, low mood, stress, and difficulty with thinking and memory. These symptoms can have a significant effect on quality of life, with an estimated 1 in 10 women leaving work due to menopause symptoms.

This study found that regular physical activity may help improve certain psychological symptoms and overall quality of life during menopause.

The types of physical activity in the review were broken into two categories: aerobic-based exercise (ABIs) and mind-body activities (MBAs).

The researchers found that ABIs showed the strongest effects on reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall quality of life. This may be particularly relevant during perimenopause when women may be more vulnerable to mood instability due to estrogen fluctuations.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, pointed out that there are various ways that ABIs can have positive effects.

Ross explained that aerobic exercise may help improve menopausal symptoms through several mechanisms, including the release of endorphins, reduced inflammation, improved cholesterol levels, and better regulation of body temperature and sleep cycles.

MBAs were shown to have a greater effect on improving psychological quality of life. These exercises involve mindfulness, body awareness, and breathwork. They may help improve emotional regulation.

ABIs tended to require fewer sessions. This may mean these types of exercises are easier to fit in, especially if you are facing time or energy constraints. For example, in one trial, participants were instructed to walk for 30 minutes, three times per week. In another trial, participants attended three hour-long Zumba classes per week.

In contrast, one trial using MBAs instructed participants to attend two 75-minute yoga classes each week, and another had participants attend daily one-hour yoga classes.

Stephanie Faubion, MD, Director for the Mayo Clinic Center for Women’s Health and Medical Director for The Menopause Society, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that this was a relatively small body of evidence. “While physical activity is an important component of a healthy lifestyle during midlife, I would be cautious about interpreting these findings to mean that exercise is a treatment for depression related specifically to menopause,” she said.

Research has shown that physical activity can positively affect mental health. Studies have also found that regular exercise can help improve symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Faubion told Healthline that, broadly speaking, regular physical activity may support mood through multiple pathways, including better sleep, improved physical function, and overall well-being.

However, she also noted that this study cannot tell us precisely why the improvements occurred or whether aerobic exercise is superior to other forms of activity.

Faubion said that recommending physical activity during and after menopause extends well beyond mood.

“Physical activity is important for cardiovascular and metabolic health, maintaining muscle mass and physical function, and supporting bone health,” she said.

“These become particularly important at midlife as women experience age- and menopause-related changes in body composition, bone, and cardiometabolic health,” Faubion added.

Ross agreed that physical activity is important for more than just mood and mental health improvement. She also noted that there are other types of exercise beyond ABIs and MBAs that can benefit people during menopause:

“These types of activities help with weight loss or allow women to maintain a healthy weight. If you are not ready for aerobic exercises, stretching and simple exercises help with balance and stability,” Ross said.

Faubion told Healthline that physical activity is an important part of healthy aging and should be encouraged during the menopause transition, but it should not be suggested that women can simply exercise their way out of significant depression or other bothersome menopause symptoms.

“Women experiencing persistent depression, anxiety, or symptoms that interfere with their quality of life should seek evaluation and discuss the full range of evidence-based treatment options with their healthcare clinician,” Faubion said.

#4289. The Video Games And More You Should Play This Weekend – October 16, 2026

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Welcome to spooky month where lawns suddenly grow giant skeletons, it's cold enough for a hoodie, the remakes of Resident Evil 2, 3, and 4 released on Switch 2, and we're all prepping to hunker down for Winter. And Grand Theft Auto VI.

We're still in the thick of new release season which, fun fact, used to just be the month of November. But now it starts in August and ends in March, give or take a few weeks.

Before we get to our regularly scheduled weekend recommendations, though, here are some of the biggest Game Informer stories of the week.

It has been a long wait for a brand new Castlevania game, and frankly, I think Belmont's Curse has been worth the time spent in the queue. Charles Harte agrees, as he wrote in his review, "Belmont's Curse is a strong reminder of why the Castlevania series is so influential, with a rich world, tense combat, and a map chock-full of secrets. While this latest entry certainly pays homage to the Castlevanias of yore, it also breathes fresh life into the franchise, making me excited for its future. Castlevania is back, and between Rose Belmont and Evil Empire, it's in great hands."

The game comes from the team behind Dead Cells, a game heavily influenced by Castlevania to begin with, and the result shows how great a fit the team and franchise is. Controlling Rose Belmont feels great, the world is big and fun to explore without being overbearing, and beating each boss is a rewarding challenge. I often feel one mark of a good game for me is if it keeps me up past my bedtime, and Belmont's Curse is, unfortunately, doing a great job of making me feel tired all day. But that's a good thing.

#4075. Whatever Happened to Daylight Saving Time Going Away?

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It will soon be time, once again, for our clocks to “fall back.” Nov. 1 is the date most places in the U.S. quit daylight saving time for 2026 and go back to standard time. We're still on this clock-changing treadmill, despite legislation that was supposed to eliminate it a few years ago, and despite medical professionals warning that these clock changes are bad for our health.

For example, a study out of the Stanford School of Medicine found that switching the time back and forth every year probably contributes to increased rates of stroke and obesity. Permanent daylight saving time would be an improvement on that, and permanent standard time would be even better, health-wise. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine published a position paper a few years ago stating that scientific evidence supports the idea "that the United States should eliminate seasonal time changes in favor of permanent standard time, which aligns best with human circadian biology."

But despite all this, we’ll still have to turn the clocks ahead again in spring. So whatever happened to those bills in Congress aiming to abolish the time changes?

The Sunshine Protection Act was a federal bill that would have made Daylight Saving Time permanent. If enacted, it would have taken effect on Nov. 5, 2023, the date that we would otherwise have changed the clocks. The result would be that our winter mornings would stay dark an hour longer than they currently do, but we’d get an hour more of daylight in the winter evenings.

The bill passed the Senate in March of 2022, but it wasn't taken up by the House of Representatives. In July of 2026, a similar law passed the House of Representatives, and President Trump wrote on Truth Social this month that "Republicans in the Senate have to get moving on what I call the 'No More Changing of Clocks Act.'" The Senate Majority Leader said it doesn't have the support to pass, since it would mean dark mornings for commuters and for children waiting for morning buses.

We operate on two different times throughout the year: "standard time" and "daylight saving time." These even get their own time zones! For example, if a time is marked as "EST," that means eastern standard time, which is different from eastern daylight time.

Standard time is the one we use in winter, and daylight saving time is the one we use in summer. The bill mentioned above would have done away with standard time and kept us on daylight saving time year-round. Meanwhile, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine has taken the position that we should stay on standard time (that is, winter time) year-round, since screwing with our circadian rhythms twice a year has detrimental effects on our health and safety. If they got their way, we would fall back some year and then never spring forward again.

States have their own ideas about what time it should be. Currently, states are not allowed to switch to permanent daylight saving time, but they are allowed to stay on standard time year-round if they prefer. Two states do so: Hawaii and Arizona (minus the parts of Arizona in the Navajo nation).

Nineteen states have passed laws or resolutions that would allow them to switch to permanent daylight saving time if the federal government ever allows them to do so. Those states, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures, are Alabama, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Louisiana, Maine, Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming. Three other states have made attempts to join the list: Similar bills made it partway through the Kentucky and Ohio legislatures but were not signed into law. California voters have authorized a law that isn’t yet officially on the books.

Until a federal law passes, those states are stuck with their current time zones. Current federal law, as passed in 1966, allows states to opt out of daylight saving time (as Hawaii and Arizona have) but does not offer a way for states to make daylight saving time permanent or to choose their own dates for changing the clocks. If you’re sick of time changes, you can always move to one of the territories that don’t observe daylight saving, which include the U.S. Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico.

#4692. We have reached a new threshold in genAI gloom, with a game dev cancelling a demo for fear of copycats ripping them off before release

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Drunken Goddess Reflux to get limited-access showing instead

In what I suspect is the start of a trend, assuming it isn't one already, the creators of Drunken Goddess Reflux have yanked a planned public demo partly because they're concerned about people using generative AI to clone their game and release a copy before they even launch.

New to Drunken Goddess Reflux? It's a drinking game in which you try to make a bouncy and buxom demon throw up before she imbibes your soul. I would not be very good at this game, but that's all fine, because I'm a games journalist and therefore do not have a soul. In a post on the eighth circle of Hell (Twitter) earlier today, developers Alliance Arts apologise for cancelling a public demo that was due to run on 19th October. They're going to hold limited-access online play sessions instead.

They give two reasons for the decision. Firstly: "We had planned to release the demo publicly with some restrictions on posting and streaming gameplay videos. However, we concluded that we can't properly manage those restrictions with our current team."

And secondly: "We're concerned that the demo's content could be used to quickly imitate or replicate the game's presentation and gameplay with AI or other tools, and that such works could be released before our launch."

The one caveat I have is that advertising yourself as anti-AI, or alluding to generative AI's negative effects, has become something of a marketing practice. It's possible that the AI line is just a plug for attention. That said, I'm likely being too cynical (see also: don't have a soul).

Ripping off other people's work is an age-old practice – speak not to me of Flappy Bird - but the past couple years have seen a surge in quickfire AI-generated clones. There was a whole hullabaloo recently over a viral needle-in-haystack game that attracted a bunch of imitators, making finding the original on Steam as difficult as, WAIT FOR IT... oh, you can fill in the rest.

Some copycats are, indeed, beating the original to shelves. In March, somebody released a clone of Japanese game Typing Magician created by feeding progress videos to a generative AI. The upshot, of course, is that developers may feel less willing to share about their emerging projects, which seems a bit sad! If that was you who photocopied Typing Magician, I'm siccing the drunken demons on you.
