#5576. CISA promises proactive critical infrastructure defense in new strategic plan

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Supply-chain security receives special attention in the plan, with CISA promising to “develop data-driven guidance and best practices.” AI is mentioned only briefly.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency on Monday laid out an ambitious vision for protecting U.S. government networks and critical infrastructure systems over the next four years.

The “CISA 2030: Defend, Harden, Prevail” strategy charts an optimistic path forward for an agency that the Trump administration has repeatedly downsized and marginalized for political reasons. The document — which includes a forward from Secretary of Homeland Security Markwayne Mullin praising President Trump’s leadership — aligns with the White House’s public messaging about how it is putting CISA “back on mission” after the agency took on a wider range of responsibilities.

Administration officials used the strategy to promise a more proactive CISA that stays ahead of evolving cybersecurity threats.

“CISA will transition from passive defense to an aggressive, anticipatory stance that actively eliminates adversarial footholds in defense-critical infrastructure prior to weaponization against our systems,” Nick Andersen, the agency’s acting director, said in an introductory statement.

“Resilience must be the foundation of our approach” because of how quickly the threat environment is changing, Andersen added. “We must ensure that when adversaries gain a foothold, we are prepared to detect them quickly, evict them decisively, and keep critical operations running without interruption.”

The plan outlines CISA’s commitments in three areas: basic defense, long-term hardening and systemic resilience to disruptions.

Within 60 days, CISA said, it will begin a review of its “core services and capabilities.” Within 180 days, the agency will complete a plan for implementing the strategy that includes metrics for measuring success. CISA will provide an update on implementation after one year.

The first part of the plan addresses the core of CISA’s responsibilities: working with other federal agencies, state and local governments, critical infrastructure organizations, private companies and international allies to prevent hackers from sowing chaos across vital U.S. systems.

“Our adversaries will continue to develop and deploy advanced capabilities,” CISA said in the document, “and we must work with partners to detect, disrupt, and mitigate cyber threats while helping rapidly remove adversaries from compromised systems.”

CISA said it would “integrate” various partners’ capabilities to develop a better picture of the threat landscape and generate better defensive measures. It also said it would help infrastructure operators implement better security measures for their operational technology, including stronger asset visibility and intrusion detection.

Supply-chain security receives special attention in the plan, with CISA promising to “develop data-driven guidance and best practices” to help organizations understand and secure their third-party dependencies.

Emerging technologies such as AI receive scant attention in the document, although CISA promises to oversee efforts to both deploy and secure those technologies.

Prioritization is another core tenet of the new CISA strategy. The agency vowed to “build capacity” throughout critical infrastructure sectors by creating processes to help its partners “focus resources where they will have the greatest impact.”

The U.S. cannot defend every computer system, cybersecurity experts say, so it must focus on the most important assets. For CISA, part of protecting those assets is understanding how they connect to each other and how they could fail.

The strategy commits CISA to improving intelligence-sharing ties with spy agencies and private firms, assessing infrastructure to support prioritization decisions (including through a U.K.-style National Risk Register) and leading the deployment of “meaningful and measurable security improvements” to infrastructure operators.

CISA’s risk-forecasting and asset-prioritization efforts have undergone several changes over the past decade. During the first Trump administration, the agency launched a program to identify the “National Critical Functions” essential to daily life and organized society. Under the Biden administration, that effort morphed into a focus on “systemically important entities.”

CISA has spent 2026 warning companies that disruptions are inevitable and their top priority should be preparing to operate in a degraded state, rather than trying to prevent intrusions altogether. In May, the agency and international partners released fortification guidance intended to help companies improve resilience. And in June, Andersen hammered home the point by acknowledging the inevitability of a major nation-state hack of U.S. critical infrastructure.

The new strategy continues that emphasis on persistent service delivery in the aftermath of an attack.

“National critical infrastructure resilience can no longer be defined simply by how we respond to disruption, but by the model we build to withstand it,” the document says.

To that end, CISA said it would continue its work to “ensure continuity of communications” during an emergency, along “investing in scalable, encrypted, and resilient infrastructure” that keeps vital services running after disruptions. The agency promised to use “advanced analytics, emerging technologies, intelligence, and cross-sector collaboration” to maintain a real-time understanding of infrastructure operators’ susceptibility to major compromises.

All of those readiness activities will require strong connections between CISA and a nationally dispersed network of infrastructure organizations. CISA’s field force of local advisers has dwindled as the Trump administration has downsized the agency, but CISA did not acknowledge those challenges in the new strategy, instead vowing to “build capacity through our ten regional offices to enable rapid mobilization against a persistent threat landscape.”

“CISA will equip its advisors to be the cyber and physical security workforce of today and tomorrow,” the agency said.

It remains unclear how CISA will be able to strengthen its partnerships with infrastructure operators that it has alienated with a flurry of changes and degradations to longstanding partnerships. The Trump administration eliminated a decades-old partnership framework, effectively freezing many important collaborative efforts between industry and government, and it has been slow to replace that system. The administration also stopped funding the information sharing group for state and local governments, forcing many of them to leave the group and forgo essential services.

Other cuts have focused on CISA itself. The Trump administration tried to eliminate the division that coordinates partnerships and scrapped a series of free assessments for infrastructure operators. In February, Andersen told staffers that CISA planned to “turn off” certain programs as it refocused its priorities.

The hollowing out of CISA has been most evident in election security, where some state and local officials say the agency has become virtually useless. but other infrastructure sectors have also complained about a lack of federal support in their fight against hackers.

Andersen, CISA’s acting director, has expressed a desire to turn around the agency’s fortunes. CISA is in the process of onboarding several hundred new employees to fill critical vacancies left by the Trump administration’s earlier workforce purges.

#3780. Jon Ossoff Harnesses Short-Form Vertical Video on TikTok and Instagram to Reach Voters in the Georgia Senate Race

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Behind a podium onstage at the second Georgia Senate debate, Senator Jon Ossoff made an appeal to a very specific audience. “I want everybody who’s clipping this debate for television news across Georgia and online tonight to roll that tape,” he said as he ridiculed his opponent, Mike Collins, for appearing to reverse his previously stated stance on abortion.

Ossoff knows his audience. Over the course of his campaign for reelection to the Senate in the battleground state of Georgia, the 39-year-old Democrat has vaulted to national stardom thanks in no small part to the power of short-form video. His pervasive presence on social feeds everywhere from Berkeley to Boston is a consequence of the clip-centered nature of his campaign, which churns out atomized video from his rallies and debates, and it has established Ossoff in the common liberal imagination as a great Democratic hope of 2028.

If 2024 was the podcast election, wherein Donald Trump’s return to power was fueled by his appearances with and support from garrulous commentators who built up vast audiences by talking about energy drinks and who constructed the pyramids, then these midterms have undoubtedly been the short-form video elections. Political stars are born by virtue of their campaign’s ability to produce clips designed to go viral, earn media attention, and rake in donations from voters who’ve learned to love the candidate through their feeds. Ossoff’s campaign has proved particularly adept in this regard: Its media strategy has cemented his reputation as one of the party’s most effective messengers on the stump, helped to raise an astonishing $110 million during this election campaign, and thrust his name to the forefront of the 2028 conversation.

For many Democrats, their love-at-first-sight encounter with Ossoff likely involved looking down at a video shot through a lens looking up at him. His rallies are cinematic productions. There are multiple camera shots, and one in particular stands out: the camera tilted upward at the candidate, the soft glow of the lighting accentuating his jawline, his eyes looking out beyond the crowd. This is called the hero shot, and it’s become a trademark of his media strategy, a visual grammar now synonymous with the young senator from Georgia and reminiscent of that of a young senator from Illinois. It helps that, like Barack Obama, Ossoff is tall and handsome, his shirtsleeves perma-rolled to the elbows as a kind of sartorial cue that this is a serious man getting down to business.

The rallies are for a Georgia audience, the voters Ossoff needs in order to do the immediate job of winning reelection in November. His campaign is determined to maintain the perception that this race is a local one, with the senator hyperfixated on the concerns of his constituents and uninterested in either national ambitions or the chatter among prospectors in Washington.

Yet Ossoff’s media strategy of filling social feeds with digestible hits from his rallies is inevitably a national one. And it’s been swell for fundraising. He raised $33 million in the third quarter, adding to his gargantuan total election-cycle haul of well over $100 million. His campaign says nearly 97% of the third-quarter contributions were small-dollar commitments of less than $100. (For comparison: Collins, his opponent, has raised $9.2 million in total this cycle.)

Sound bites have been a part of American politics for centuries, since before Abraham Lincoln told Republicans at the Illinois State Capitol that “a house divided against itself cannot stand.” What’s new is the format and how it has seeped into every crevice of American life, conquering our collective attention. “We’re in a clip-based economy,” says Keith Edwards, a progressive political commentator who oversaw content strategy on Ossoff’s 2021 runoff campaign. “Clavicular is a clear example of that. He really came out of nowhere just from the clips that surfaced online.”

Only a small fraction of America regularly watches the streams hosted by Twitch personalities like Clavicular and Hasan Piker, but our feeds are inundated with content from these figures, churned out by so-called clippers who are often paid to blast the videos out into the world for engagement revenue.

“[Ossoff] understands this in a way that [Donald] Trump used to. I think Trump just doesn’t have it in him anymore,” says Edwards. “Sure, it’s a speech. But really, the rally is a clip-making factory. When I go live on Tuesdays,” he adds, referring to his own stream on YouTube and Twitch, “maybe 4,000 people are watching concurrently, but the cut-downs and the clips that we make from it generate millions of views. So it’s just about being really focused with your time and understanding that what happens in that room is a production process to generate as many clips as possible.”

Ossoff is well suited to these shifts in media consumption. When he first ran for Senate in 2020, recalls Hussien Salama, the lead video producer for the campaign during the runoff election in early 2021, his campaign embraced TikTok in an effort to appeal to younger voters at a time when many politicians were keen to avoid the platform over its ties to China. Ossoff, then in his early 30s, was more natural in front of the camera than other candidates Salama would go on to work with, and he had a close relationship with his video team. But the candidate started paying real attention to social strategy after a video compilation of his stump speeches assailing his then opponent, David Perdue, went viral on TikTok. “That success and hearing from students, young voters on the ground being like, ‘We love your TikTok,’ made him engage much further,” Salama says.

Before getting into politics, Ossoff was a documentary producer, and his work often investigated public corruption. This helps to explain his preternatural ability to weave broader indictments of Trump’s administration into a persuasive narrative. There are characters, like “Prince Don and Prince Eric,” who are making foreign business deals while their father makes American foreign policy. Or Natalie Harp, Trump’s peculiar executive assistant whom Ossoff mentioned at a rally in August: “He doesn’t want to do the job. He wants to build his ballroom and travel with Natalie on their apparently defenseless flying palace gifted by the emir of Qatar.”

Ossoff's political persona has evolved—and exploded—since his first congressional run in 2017, though questions remain about his retail politicking.
