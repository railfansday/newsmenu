#5486. Chinese EVs found back door into Western car markets – at 40% off MSRP

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The dumb oil war in Iran is driving up the cost of just about everything, including new cars. That’s giving Chinese EVs a huge advantage over Western “legacy” car brands already lagging in tech and struggling with affordability – and those legacy brands about to have a whole new to them problem on their hands: USED Chinese EVs.

Western automakers have leaned on protectionist trade policies to keep cheaper Chinese rivals at bay, placing strict limits on the number of new Chinese cars allowed in and hefty tariffs on new battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) are as high as 45.3% in the EU. That supply limitation is, of course, ratcheting up demand for the high-tech, low-cost Chinese machines, and buyers in Europe and Canada are finding that used Chinese EVs – which offer comparable tech to the newest Western EVs, a price tag up to 40% lower than their already low MSRPs, and which are not subject to the same protectionist import duties – are a workable alternative.

According to data shared by importers/exporters China EV Marketplace (CEVM), pre-owned vehicle sales to EU customers grew 280% year over year, with sales climbing to 7,900 units through September.

“Tariffs have already weakened demand for new Chinese BEVs among our European customers. We expect the hybrid import cap to put pressure on new PHEV sales too,” CEVM COO Jakub Geršl told CarNewsChina. “Pre-owned cars are exempt from the additional BEV tariffs and from the new hybrid (PHEV) quota. That is why we are making pre-owned vehicles the main focus of our European business.”

CEVM has more than 2,600 used Chinese EVs on its website as I type this, and their inventory is refreshed almost daily with fresh pre-owned examples of cars from brands like BYD, Geely, Zeekr, and others – and the company claims its prices are about 40% below the original price (MSRP) on average.

“Lower new-car prices put pressure on pre-owned prices as well,” Geršl adds. “Our Shanghai-based team searches for those opportunities. We regularly find cars with around 6,000 km on the clock priced 40% below their original Chinese list price.”

That price reduction is driven by a number of factors, including the relentless march of technological progress, the flood of new and updated EVs coming out of Chinese car brands, and a burgeoning domestic Chinese car culture that values newness above all else. If you’re reading between the lines there, I think you’ll find that that means more low-mileage, low-price, high-tech, and high-value Chinese EVs are coming to the West – and probably a lot sooner than brands like VW, who are struggling to compete as it is, would want.

And that’s the real problem – whether you agree with him or not, Elon Musk will tell you that technology and markets move faster than legislation, and once consumers go electric, precious few of them ever go back to gas or diesel, regardless of what that dude filming himself wearing PitVipers in the drivers’ seat of his pickup and your racist uncle on Facebook are telling you.

My concern would be lack of service facilities. At some point every car, even EVs, needs to go to the service center for something, and the more technology-laden the vehicle, the more important it is to have service centers that know what they're doing. I wouldn't count on the local Chevy dealer being able to deal with a mandarin software stack.

Overland Expo lent us its Nissan Frontier Ultimate Build for a weekend trip in Pure Michigan. It’s awesome, but does camping equipment correlate with camping enjoyment?

There’s something out there. In the pitch-black, beyond the glow of our fire, which is burning strong into its 11th hour, I keep hearing leaves crunching. I think back to the nice lady who discovered our encampment not 20 minutes after we pulled our e-brakes. She lives in the area and was looking for firewood. She left us with a friendly warning, “You know it’s bear-hunting season, right?”

We were deep in the Huron National Forest at the end of a two-track service road, far enough off the beaten path that Apple Maps only shows it as green. It’s overgrown after weeks of rainfall, and it’s beautiful, at least during the day. Representing humanity in these woods are senior news editor Caleb Miller, associate news editor Jack Fitzgerald, and myself. The air is still, without even a breeze, yet I hear rustling, and then another branch cracks. This one sounds louder than the others. "Jack, you’re sure it’s just dead sticks falling?"

“It’s just dead sticks,” he reassures me, with a soft cadence as if he’s comforting a child. There’s another crunch from his side of the fire, and he whips his head toward the void. We are so effed.

Tonight, I will be sleeping in the five-foot bed of my 2020 Toyota Tacoma TRD Sport. Draped overtop, and serving as my only protection from whatever lingers in the forest, is a Rightline Gear mid-size truck bed tent from Amazon. This tent comes with a rain fly, and it straps to the box of my truck with the tailgate down. I also have an Anker Solix C300 portable power station for charging my devices, and between me and the composite plastic ridges of the bed is a Nemo Tensor inflatable sleeping pad and an Ohuhu lightweight sleeping bag. It’ll approach 40 degrees tonight, so I will be sleeping in my jeans.

It could be worse. I could be on the ground with the rodents scavenging for scraps of the sausages we just cooked over the fire. That is Jack’s fate. He’s in a tent, with only a waterproof mat between it and the ground, because we forgot to bring a tarp. He has a thin roll-up sleeping pad, a sleeping bag, and nothing else. It’s a night spent with the bare minimum.

On the flip side, his excellency, Sir Caleb Miller, is resting in a box high above nature’s will. This weekend, Caleb is driving and living out of the 2026 Nissan Frontier Pro-4X Ultimate Build, a custom truck built by the Overland Expo Foundation as a demonstration of how to turn a stock truck into a mobile campsite.

Caleb’s digs are in the Tune Outdoor M1Lite camper, which rises above the Nissan’s roof by about three feet and features dimmable LED lighting and a 360-degree view of the world below. He’s sprawled out across a Pittman Outdoors air mattress, custom-fitted to the camper, and his body temperature is regulated by a diesel-powered Eberspäecher Airtronic heating system. He’s got a shower, too, from Rixen’s Enterprises, but we’re not close enough to a significant water source, so His Highness will just have to smell as bad as his two lowly subjects.

Miller is a benevolent despot, though; the Ultimate Build is equal parts communal and manorial, loaded with gear that elevates the campsite for all of us. Our persistent fire, for example, is encapsulated by a Breeo Y Series fire pit. It features a grill, on which we cooked the aforementioned sausages with a side of onions and peppers to near perfection, and a carrying case for stowage. In the morning, I’ll be in charge of the eggs and bacon using the JetBoil Genesis Basecamp, and Jack will wield the axe from Forrest Tool Company to refuel the fire. There’s also the Travoca Rigel Dual Zone eCooler, which slides out from the bed and has been keeping our meats, cheeses, and fruits at the perfect temperature in near silence.

We haven’t switched the Frontier’s engine on since we parked, yet the eCooler has run worry-free. That’s thanks to the Battle Born 100Ah 12V LiFePO4 Deep Cycle battery, which also powers the rest of the truck’s kit, including the LED strip lights embedded in each arm of the Taruca Rouge 270 XC awning that spans around the side of the Frontier. The lights have three modes: red, amber, and white. For now, we’re catching a vibe with the reds in full effect. There are even two solar panels mounted on top of the camper to keep the auxiliary battery topped up. The REDWorks Scout25 Power Panels from REDARC Electronics route into a BCDC Core25 charger and integrated Smart Battery Monitor.

The Ultimate Build’s gear adds up to roughly $15,900. That doesn’t include any of the Frontier’s off-roading equipment, like the ComeUp winch, upgraded Fox suspension with remote reservoirs, or the all-terrain Nokian tires. Just the comfort gear. The sleeping situation in my Tacoma was just $613, and Jack’s setup costs even less. The question we aim to answer, aside from “Where are the bears?” is whether it’s worth dropping 16 bands to have a Manhattan studio apartment on wheels. Will Caleb really have the best time on this camping trip?

To signal the end of social hour, he dumps a cup of water across the glowing red embers of our fire. It exhales in relief after its 15-hour shift. I switch each of the Taruca awning’s lights to white and begin washing the sausage juice off our plates. Jack is collecting trash in a bag that we’ve somehow already filled to the brim. “I’m gonna put this in the back seat.” Damn, that’s right. Because of the bears. That thought is sidelined when I put away the last utensil and unzip my tent to find it full of bugs.

Moths, mosquitoes, and some insects that I’ve never seen before are flying around and crawling across my nylon ceiling. Yours truly is truly a dumbass, because I had left my light on in a tent that wasn’t fully sealed—there’s a gap down below where it stretches over the open tailgate. I hate things that crawl. My perpetual anxiety is making me think that at any moment I’ll be having a 4-D experience of The Revenant, and, to be honest, the onions weren’t perfectly cooked. They were raw. Why did I come out here? Why did I buy all this camping gear? Outside the bug-tent, I hear the sound of a single-cylinder kicking to life. It’s Caleb turning on his heater before he shuts the tailgate.

The next morning I can hear the daybreak before the light enters the tent. The breeze rustles its way through the trees; the birds are singing their greatest hits. It’s calm. I’ve successfully traveled forward in time through my entomological dreams with the help of a little dissociation and about four IPAs. Speaking of, I need water. I open my eyes to a diffuse daylight through my nylon ceiling. There are still a couple of critters up there; I wonder how many I swallowed overnight. I sit up, open the flap of my tent, and there she is, Pure Michigan. Perfectly green, layered in a blanket of dew. The serenity is palpable, and suddenly, I remember exactly why I bought all this camping gear.

It’s not long after when the boys emerge from their shelters. Neither seems worse for wear, which, to me, says a lot about Jack in his barebones setup. Tough lad. That’s not to discredit Caleb, though. I’m sure he suffered a lot of guilt about being so warm and cozy.

It’s Sunday, and we’re in a time crunch to get back to Ann Arbor to catch the NFL’s late-window games, so he’s already started collapsing our setup while I make breakfast. We’re all alive, back at it again for another day. Turns out Jack was right; the crunches in the night weren’t bears after all.

We can’t all have the Frontier Ultimate Build to explore our country’s wilderness; that much is obvious, but we can find a balance. Focus on what’s important to you, and the rest will follow. I, for one, am looking to fix my bug problem. So along with a bottle of bug spray, I might buy a fiberglass bed cap on Facebook Marketplace to make my Taco a hard shell. Jack made it clear that he requires his coffee in the morning, and although that JetBoil certainly did the trick, I’m looking at a propane-powered, two-burner stovetop right now on Amazon for $70.

The camper on the Nissan looks mighty fine, but nine grand? I don’t know about that. You don’t need much to get out there and explore. Start small. Sleep in your back seat if you have to, but by all means go out and see these incredible, jaw-dropping, vibrant landscapes that our country holds while we still have them.

#3813. With War, Dominic West Solidifies Himself as Pop Culture’s Greatest Adulterer

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

War, the new HBO drama about a high-profile divorce between a movie star and a billionaire tech founder, opens with Dominic West cheating on Sienna Miller. Or, more accurately, Dominic West’s character of Morgan Henderson cheats on Sienna Miller’s character of Carla Duval. It’s a scene that feels scandalous but also, well, familiar.

It seems like West does his spouse dirty in every show he stars in. Behold the curriculum vitae: Jimmy McNulty in The Wire, a serial womanizer whose wife, Elena, leaves him due to his infidelity. In later seasons, he has an affair with the assistant state’s attorney. Noah Solloway in The Affair, which, as the title implies, is a show about a guy who cheats on his wife. Then there’s Prince Charles in The Crown. You know the story. (Then there’s the case of life possibly imitating art, when in 2020 West was spotted extracurricularly scootering around Rome with his then costar Lily James. Amid the ensuing tabloid storm, the actor walked outside his Cotswolds home with his wife Catherine FitzGerald. They left a note that read: “Our marriage is strong, and we’re very much still together. Thank you.” The moment was instantly memed.)

With all respect to West’s previous cheating-on-television canon, War might be his finest adulterous work. War is glossy, catty, and glamorous, swinging between multimillion-dollar townhomes in West London and high-end lawyer’s offices where they have retainers; the characters are so rich that they wear million-dollar watches and so bitchy they’ll stab each other in the back to get what they want. “Every man has his price,” says Morgan while drinking green juice at a five-star hotel. “And mine is prohibitively high.”

West plays his cheating roles deftly: He’s just smarmy enough that you believe his transgression, but not smarmy enough that you loathe him for it. In War, for example, Morgan Henderson does indeed cheat on his wife with a 20-something aspiring actress. But as it turns out, Morgan’s actually a pretty great stepdad, whereas Carla’s parenting skills have more…mixed reviews. (During a research and discovery session, Carla’s lawyers find out from a Hollywood director that “she wanted to get away from her family” and was “not exactly the maternal type.”) In The Crown, Prince Charles did humiliate Princess Diana. But Prince Philip pressured him into that marriage and Charles really, really loves Camilla Parker Bowles. (West himself admitted to The Sunday Times that his own tabloid drama shaped his role as Prince Charles. “I’d had a very acute understanding of what it’s like to feel the horror of your name or your photograph coming up in the newspapers,” he says.) As for Noah? The Affair is told with a dual his-and-hers narrative. Each episode shows the perspective of West’s Noah as well as his paramour, Ruth Wilson’s Allison. As a result, the viewer is unable to truly villainize either. As the saying goes, it takes two to tango.

Then there’s his looks: He’s rakish yet age-appropriate, straddling the delicate aesthetic line where you both understand why a woman might be attracted to him, but also that this is a man having a midlife crisis. “I have been made redundant—by my wife,” West’s Morgan grumbles to his business colleague.

Miller—who is no stranger to extramarital tabloid scandals herself—has also mastered her getting-cheated-on performances. In Anatomy of a Scandal, she played Sophie, the wife of a British cabinet member who is accused of having an affair with (and eventually sexually assaulting) his young aide. She tries to piece together the truth wearing a number of sumptuous Max Mara coats. You feel for Sophie—but you don’t pity her: The show delves into her psyche as she debates leaving her husband and the lifestyle he can provide. The final episode culminates in her starting a new life with her children in the country.

In the first episode, West’s Morgan serves divorce papers to Miller’s Carla at her own movie premiere.

Arguably part of the reason War is so delicious is that the two actors, at the top of their adulterous craft, are finally squaring off against each other. You root for both and therefore root for neither. You do root, however, for more gossipy drama to blow up in their faces. (Like when Morgan figures out Carla is filing for divorce because The Daily Mirror runs a paparazzi picture of her with lawyers in Paris—and rushes to beat her to filing.) But it’s also because, well, War deliberately plucks plot lines that pop in our pop-cultured riddled brains. On a macro level, that means the casting of West and Miller. On a micro level, it’s specific moments, like when Miller’s Carla gets served divorce papers at her own premiere—reminiscent of when Olivia Wilde got served custody papers while speaking onstage at CinemaCon.

Maybe that’s why War works so well. The tabloid business is based on our infinite fascination with imploding celebrity relationships: Who among us hasn’t clicked on something about Kim and Kanye, Jen and Brad, J.Lo and Ben, or Gwyneth and her “conscious uncoupling”? War allows that to scratch that lowbrow itch in our brains in a highbrow, HBO way—by casting two of the very celebrities we associate with such a scandal.

It also gives us more sordid details that we could have ever gossiped about. War brings us inside the lawyers boardrooms and peels back the pages of prenups of the rich and famous. A key plot point of War is that Carla wants their prenup thrown out, as Morgan got around $700 million richer during their marriage. Meanwhile, Morgan’s law firm, Cathcarts, is desperate to make Carla look like a frivolous, Marie Antoinette–level spender—cataloging that she spent 300 pounds on hand-cut Norwegian ice. Carla’s law firm, on the other hand, is desperate to cover up that the aforementioned 20-something Henderson cheated on their client with was a honey pot, planted by his wife who wants ammo for her divorce filing.

It’s the perfect low-stakes mess. Both characters are wealthy enough that even if their net worth’s got halved, they’d still live a lavish life; both characters are morally complicated enough that you don’t feel either of them are wronged. You just get to sit and watch it play out by the perfect couple. As West’s Morgan says about his own divorce: “This is horrible. And I’m very sorry about it!”

#4147. Mass Effect 3 ending discourse ignored the RPG's scale, ambition, and exuberance, including that of its oddly great co-op

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Ah, come on, it was fine. Better than fine. We can argue for another fourteen years about whether BioWare should have redone the ending or not, but Mass Effect 3 was really a compendium of endings, a ludicrously ambitious wrapping-up of dozens of plot threads, character arcs, and running gags. Even if you can point to other RPGs that, on an individual basis, are more branching and reactive, Mass Effect 3's largely successful closing of a galaxy's worth of choose-your-own adventure books - filled in and followed across three evolving instalments - showed an audacity and grandiosity that no other three-part series had matched before. Or, at least on that scale, has pulled off since.

Have You Played? is an endless stream of game retrospectives.

It didn't hurt that Mass Effect 3 also had the trilogy's fiercest, bangiest action by far, nor some of its highest-stakes, often most lip-chewing moments of decisionmaking. It is true that the accumulation of War Assets wasn't always as heavy on character drama as the game-long preparation for Mass Effect 2's Suicide Mission, but then you don't simply play Mass Effect 3 to make a number go higher. You play it to punch or hug reporters, to love or shoot your friends, to save or damn entire species. And it has loads of that, right up to the point to have to choose the colour of an explosion. Potentially beyond, if you treat the (assuredly, delightfully) fanservice-forward Citadel expansion as Shepard's true last ride.

Back in 2013, and again with 2021's Legendary Edition, I was sad that Mass Effect 3 ended and glad that it happened. That, at the risk of appearing less furious at EA's stewardship than I am, does not change with BioWare's closure. What does bother me is the uncertainty over what now happens to Mass Effect 3's surprisingly entertaining, weirdly long-lived co-op mode.

I don't doubt this was put in for boring, C-suite, "gamers want multiplayer" reasons. But that only gave the resultant palled-up superpower maelstroms a sense that they were better than they had any right to be, with explosive moments of player-to-player power synergies and spacious, blindspot-filled maps that could maintain a thrilling feeling of being permanently surrounded. Post-release additions to the species-spanning character roster also showed glimpses of that same, fuck-it swagger that BioWare exhibited in the campaign, as they increasingly moved away from familiar soldier archtypes towards electric whip wielders, regenerating lizards, teleporting ninjas, and three-foot-tall psychic asthmatics. While many of today's co-op shooters fret over miniscule balance changes, in pursuit of some mythically perfect meta, Mass Effect 3's gave you a crate of action figures and told you to go nuts.

While I haven't played it recently, there's a small but loyal contingent who do, and for their sake I hope the multiplayer's P2P setup helps it survive the EA cull. I was, and for now technically still is, too good to be doomed to oblivion. Or, worse, vibe-coded Legendary Edition mods.

#4426. Rivian Buys Synthetic Data Startup To Boost Its Autonomy Ambitions

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Rivian has acquired U.K.-based AI startup Third Dimension to help it accelerate the development of autonomous driving for its electric trucks and SUVs. The deal could give Rivian a way to turn real-world driving footage into realistic 3D simulations, potentially helping it train and validate self-driving systems faster, TechCrunch reported Thursday.

Third Dimension basically uses AI to turn images and videos into lifelike 3D environments. Its technology was initially developed to accelerate video game production, but Rivian plans to use it to create simulations based on actual driving scenarios, according to the outlet.

The company previously said in a blog post that its lifelike simulations could teach robotic systems to handle edge cases before they happen in the real world. In Rivian's case, the vehicle's autonomous driving system could train on things like swerving cyclists and erratic drivers before waiting for such things to happen actually.

Rivian currently offers Autonomy+, which allows hands-free highway driving and automatic lane changes on command. It wants to further develop this system to be even more capable, like Tesla’s Full Self Driving (Supervised), where it can drive point-to-point hands-free with supervision. That development work is also central to its planned robotaxi partnership with Uber, which is expected to put up to 50,000 R2 robotaxis on the road in 2028.

Third Dimension's 15-member team, most of whom are based in the U.K., will be led by Rivian’s Senior Vice President of Autonomy and AI James Philbin.

“For us, it’s about taking real scenarios, but making them simulatable,” Philbin told TechCrunch. “These are not synthetic kind of video game styles, they’re real things that happen in the world, but now we get that extra dial of being able to move the cameras around, shift other agents, have maybe different lighting or weather conditions. So it’s a really powerful tool for us,” he added.

What he’s basically referring to is that self-driving systems need to be tested against an enormous range of road conditions, including unusual situations that are difficult to encounter during everyday driving. Simulations let engineers recreate those scenarios without waiting for them to happen on public roads.

Rivian already gets swathes of driving data from its current vehicles on the road. According to Philbin, Rivian receives more than 400,000 driving sequences daily from customer vehicles whose owners have enabled external camera sharing. Third Dimension's technology could help turn that data into more realistic simulations, and we'll see if this really helps move the needle for Rivian to rival Waymo, Tesla, and others in this space.

They dress like gurus, all alike in black, with similar sneakers and hoodies. They read the same books, many of them science fiction. They speak in a similar language, with a messianic tone. In her latest book, The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State, published this past August, she argues that today Big Tech controls the United States—and, in turn, the entire planet. They wield their power authoritatively, favoring presidents who best serve their interests, and helping to erode the democratic fabric of nations, one fake news story at a time.

Vanity Fair: In your book, you define the Artificial State as “a future in which humans are ruled, and many replaced, by machines owned by corporations.” How close is that future?

Jill Lepore: The place where people can most easily see what I’m talking about is with what used to be called the public sphere. A liberal democracy depends on a free and open public discussion of the state of the world where people can get reliable information and exchange ideas and deliberate. The Artificial State doesn’t make citizens, it unmakes citizens; it makes trolls.

What this communications infrastructure asks of you is not to participate in civil society; it is to remove yourself from participating in civil society, to remove yourself from social relations, to alienate yourself from your fellow citizens, and instead to engage in the machinery of outrage and condemnation and grievance that is destructive to civil society.

Certainly in the US, there’s democratic pushback. Our municipal and state elections since last spring have been influenced by this debate in a way that I think surprised candidates, campaigns, and elected officials. The public has found that objecting to data centers is a way to say no to the entire apparatus.

What are your predictions for the midterm elections?

The polling seems to indicate Democrats will pick up a lot of seats in Congress, but the polls have been less and less reliable over the last quarter century. I don’t think anybody’s really counting on those predictions. Trump’s power, however, is very much on the decline.

Elon Musk and Donald Trump on May 30, 2025.

The switch that came about in the summer of 2024, when Elon Musk announced that he would be supporting Trump, was a consequence largely, I think, of the Biden administration’s determination to regulate the cryptocurrency industry. As these companies began to turn their attention and investments toward accelerating the development of AI, the Biden administration was saying, “Well, no, wait, hold on. What’s this going to look like?” That’s what turned Silicon Valley toward Trump.

The power of the Silicon Valley elite is not national, it’s global. Yes, Trump’s power as the leader of a superpower is significantly global as well, but I think ultimately Sam Altman is a more powerful figure than Donald Trump at this moment.

#4658. 6 ways bats are surprisingly human

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 1:30 PM EDT

Bat Week officially begins today. It’s the one week where Earth’s only winged mammals get top billing. Often associated with disease, vampires, and other generally unpleasant things, bats are actually important pollinators and play critical roles in our ecosystem.

“They’re just so cool and so interesting. During first forays into bat studies, it was just one question after another,” Rachel Page, an evolutionary biologist at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama, tells Popular Science. “The more I sort of scratched the surface, the more interesting it became—which is the fun part.”

One reason bats are so interesting to scientists like Page is that they are surprisingly similar to humans. Here’s how.

Sharing a meal with good company brings immense joy for us humans. For many vampire bat species (subfamily Desmodontinae), sharing food is a matter of survival. They survive exclusively on blood and can only go about two nights without a blood meal before they die. To get around this, they rely on their social networks.

“They have the equivalent of friends. Basically, if a bat comes back from a night of foraging and has not been successful, other members of that social group will share food,” Page explains. “They’ll regurgitate blood for the bat that came back hungry.”

If that donor bat has difficulty getting food later, the one recipient will often become the donor. “So there’s this very reciprocal relationship,” says Page.

According to Page, bats will have their distinctive personalities just like people. It’s present across species, but also individual bats as well.

“You have some species that are very feisty, and they’ll go out of their way to pick a fight, and then other ones that are much more passive, calm, and very gentle,” she says.

At the edge of Panama’s Soberanía National Park, tiny, fruit-eating bats will take over the roost of larger, carnivorous bats. The Seba’s short-tailed bat (Carollia perspicillata) weighs roughly the same as a strawberry. By comparison, the frog-eating, fringed-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) weighs roughly as much as an apricot and twice as much as the short-tailed bat.

Page and her colleagues in Panama have witnessed fights between the two species from roosts set up throughout the forest.

“Basically, it was the male Seba’s short-tailed bat who was instigating the fight. He was coming over and was kind of boxing and threatening much larger bats with wide open mouths and lots of teeth,” Page recalls. “And he just kept at it, and then finally, this group of fringed-lipped bats left.”

Even in the thick jungles of Panama, bats can find motionless insects on leaves. Initially, scientists believed that the thick tree cover would give small insects an “acoustic camouflage” that would confuse a bat’s echolocation system. But they were wrong.

The bats can see through this acoustic camouflage by changing the angle at which they approach the still insect.

“If you approach a flat leaf at just the right angle, you can actually get an echolocation signature,” Page explains. “You can get an echolocation signal that jumps back at you, but only if you direct your echolocation call at just the right angle.”

That’s exactly what the bats were doing in order to find insects who tried to hide in leaves.

When it’s time for the babies to wean or leave the roost, mother tent-making bats (Uroderma bilobatum) will physically prod their young with their forearms.

“There is this interesting tug that you see across so many different species. For birds, when they’re ready to fledge, there’s this sort of uncertainty.”

When sick, humans and even non-human primates change their behavior. We isolate so that the disease does not spread, and will look to plants and other sources of medicine for relief. Bats appear to change their diet to less nutritious leaves. While this may sound backwards, it’s in the hope that they have medicinal properties.

In an ongoing study with a team from the Ukrainian Bat Rehabilitation Center, the Smithsonian team is looking at the bats from Barro Colorado Island in Panama.

“We have fecal samples from these bats, so we were able to see exactly what they are eating,” says Page.

In addition to changing their diets, vampire bats will self-isolate when they are sick and others will act in a way familiar to any human parent—continuing to care for our young, even when sick.

“You don’t do as many of the normal behaviors unless it’s taking care of your babies,” says Page. “And again, it’s like a human relationship. If your little kid has a fever, you’re not going to shut them in a room and leave them. You’re not going to desert them.”

To find dinner, the fringe-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) will listen in on their prey’s mating calls. These frog-eating bats eavesdrop on the mating calls that the male frogs use to attract females. They then use the calls to assess if the frog is edible or poisonous before going in for the kill.

“They’ve really cracked this communication of another entire group,” says Page.

According to Page, it’s similar to being in a quiet room and hearing someone open up a bag of potato chips and is loudly eating. The noise makes everyone pay attention. “Bats attend to those acoustic signals of other bats, and that attention then helps them learn,” Page says. “They can learn those distinctions of palatable versus poisonous, and they can learn that socially as well from observing the other behaviors with bats.”

#559. Could lowering high blood pressure be as simple as stimulating your ear?

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A small device that delivers gentle electrical pulses through the ear could offer a new approach to treating stubborn high blood pressure. In a clinical trial led by researchers at Queen Mary University of London, the technology proved safe, well tolerated, and practical for patients to use at home.

Although the treatment did not significantly lower blood pressure compared with a placebo device across all participants, researchers observed encouraging results in patients with more severe hypertension. The findings suggest that stimulating nerves in the ear could eventually help certain people whose blood pressure remains elevated despite taking medication.

The results come from the SCRATCH-HTN trial, published in Hypertension, a journal of the American Heart Association. Researchers emphasize that larger studies are needed to determine whether the approach can provide meaningful benefits and which patients might respond best.

How Ear Nerve Stimulation Could Lower Blood Pressure

The experimental treatment uses a technique called transcutaneous autonomic neuromodulation (tAN), which aims to influence the autonomic nervous system. This network of nerves automatically controls essential bodily functions, including heart rate, digestion, and blood pressure.

By delivering mild electrical signals to nerves in the ear, researchers hope to influence the body's natural mechanisms for regulating blood pressure without requiring an invasive procedure.

The handheld device, developed by Afferent Medical Solutions, uses two small clips positioned on the tragus, the small flap of cartilage located just in front of the ear canal. These clips deliver gentle electrical stimulation, allowing patients to administer the treatment themselves without visiting a clinic.

Clinical Trial Tests the Device in 63 Patients

The clinical trial, funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), enrolled 63 people with uncontrolled hypertension. All participants were already taking at least one medication to lower their blood pressure, but their readings remained above recommended levels.

Researchers randomly divided the participants into two groups. One received active nerve stimulation, while the other used a sham device designed to serve as a comparison. This approach helped investigators assess whether the electrical stimulation produced benefits beyond those associated with using the device itself.

Participants used their assigned devices for 30 minutes daily during the first two weeks. For the following 10 weeks, they reduced their sessions to once per week.

The results showed that the treatment was safe and generally comfortable for participants. Most also followed the prescribed schedule, suggesting that the technology could be practical for regular use at home.

However, when researchers compared blood pressure changes across the entire study population, they found no statistically significant difference between patients receiving active stimulation and those using the sham device.

Promising Results in Patients With Severe Hypertension

Despite the lack of a significant overall benefit, the researchers identified a potentially important finding among participants who entered the trial with more severe hypertension.

A prespecified subgroup analysis, meaning the researchers had planned this comparison before examining the results, revealed a greater reduction in blood pressure among these patients when they received active nerve stimulation rather than the sham treatment.

This finding raises the possibility that the technique may work better for people with particularly difficult-to-control blood pressure. However, the trial was relatively small, and the subgroup results require confirmation in larger studies before any conclusions can be drawn about its effectiveness.

Dr. Ajay Gupta, Chief Investigator, Clinical Reader at Queen Mary University of London and Consultant Physician at Barts Health NHS Trust, said:

"This is an exciting, highly patient-acceptable technology that could ultimately provide a cheap, scalable solution for millions. Although the trial did not achieve significance across the broad unselected group, those with more severe hypertension exhibited a clear blood pressure reduction in the active group compared to sham.

"As the first and most thorough study of its kind, this points towards important new research directions that could help us understand whether non-invasive neuromodulation can benefit particular groups of patients with uncontrolled hypertension."

A Potential New Option for Hard-to-Control Blood Pressure

High blood pressure, also known as hypertension, is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease and increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes. It places additional strain on blood vessels and the heart, potentially causing serious damage over time.

Although medications help many patients manage their condition, a substantial number continue to have blood pressure readings above recommended targets even while receiving treatment.

This ongoing challenge has encouraged scientists to explore additional approaches that could complement existing therapies.

Ear nerve stimulation is one such possibility. Because the device is small, does not require surgery, and can be used at home, it could offer a convenient option if future research establishes that it reliably lowers blood pressure in certain patients.

For now, the findings represent an early step rather than proof of an effective new treatment. The researchers say larger clinical trials will be essential to confirm the encouraging results in patients with severe hypertension and identify which groups, if any, could benefit most from this approach.
