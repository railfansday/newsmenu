#4412. The Video Games And More You Should Play This Weekend – October 9, 2026

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re in the thick of the busy holiday review season, and there’s no shortage of new games trying to capture your attention. As you head into the weekend looking for your next gaming love affair, we’ve got an impressive variety of new titles to keep on your radar.

And if you’re simply looking to catch up on what’s happening in gaming this week, we’ve got you covered on that as well.

Star Wars: Galactic Racer launched this week and it is worth your time, regardless of your thoughts on Star Wars these days. It is absolutely a Star Wars game, with familiar locations, music motifs, and characters throughout, but the IP is honestly the least interesting part. It is a truly fantastic racing game, with a sense of speed and tension that will bring you back to the days of Burnout (and it makes sense, because much of the team over at developer Fuse Games worked on the Burnout series). Don’t let the roguelite campaign scare you; it works really well and moves at a brisk pace that keeps the experience from feeling repetitive. And within that campaign is a great story, better than most in the racing genre. Once you finish the campaign, there’s still plenty of game left for you. With a standard Arcade mode, miniature campaigns in the Scenario mode, and an expansive suite of multiplayer races and tournaments, you’ll likely have Star Wars: Galactic Racer on your hard drive long after you pick it up.

If you’re like me, you’re always on the look for new gear- and ability-gated exploration games that recall the joy of early Metroid and Castlevania games. Echo Weaver offers a new twist on that idea, by keeping the exploration element, but making progression be tied to knowledge acquired over the course of play, rather than new equipment. It’s a clever twist that has me intrigued from the early hours I’ve played.

Our reviewer, Kyle Hilliard, has already had a chance to experience the full game, and had this to say: “Echo Weaver’s unique approach to the Metroid structure is admirable, unique, and well executed. The sense of discovery is fantastic, and though I realistically probably won’t immediately play the game again, the idea that I could start with knowledge of basically all of the abilities to rip through the game at high speed is an enticing one.”

Gears of War: E-Day rewinds the clock to depict the events of Emergence Day, in which protagonists Marcus Fenix and Dominic Santiago first encounter the monstrous Locusts. The series has referenced E-Day since its first entry in 2006, so fans (including myself) are eager to witness its portrayal in The Coalition's new release. In his Gears of War: E-Day review, Game Informer executive editor Brian Shea praises the release for delivering "an astonishing collection of shootouts, supported by improved traversal and tactical abilities, masterfully arranged battlefields, stunning visuals, and a strong, diverse arsenal of weapons and equipment."

Thekla’s new puzzle game is a massive project with hundreds of distinct puzzles that fit together into a larger narrative and puzzle framework. It’s an overwhelming, impressive collection of brainteasers created by integrating several distinct puzzle games into a larger, coherent whole. In my mind, I’ve come to think of it almost like the video game equivalent of a frame story. No matter how you look at it, Order of the Sinking Star is a puzzle game built for experienced, patient puzzle gamers, featuring complex logic challenges that will push any player to think outside the box.

We've once again got a great reason to dip back into Valve's unreleased-but-popular MOBA-shooter Deadlock. The Cursed Apple got a major makeover with its latest update, and a new slate of heroes is looking to join the roster, with playtime counting toward votes on which character joins each week. We've already seen the Rat King and the sweet old Baba win the vote, but four more are still on the way. The graphics overhaul is huge, though; not only do the streets and boulevards of this cursed New York look phenomenal, but small UI and indicator tweaks have made Deadlock even easier to read at a glance. Deadlock remains the most impressive game not officially released, and if you can get an invite, it's worth diving in this weekend to see what the team has been cooking up.

One of the most intriguing and tactically rich games of the last several years, The Old King’s Crown is a true triumph for integrating numerous mechanics into a thematically rich narrative framework. Each player takes on the role of a potential inheritor of the monarchy after the old king dies. Using bluffing, card management, and control of the game board, each player controls a faction that is subtly distinct from the others, leading to a fascinating emergent narrative, like a rebellious uprising seizing control from the old nobility. Unfortunately, the biggest challenge for your weekend enjoyment of the title might be tracking down a copy; Eerie Idol’s excellent game is so celebrated it can be hard to find a copy. But if you’re lucky enough to find a way to play, I think you’ll be happy to add it to your shelf. If you can’t, it was just one of the titles that won accolades in our list of the very best board games of 2025, and you could try to snag one of those instead.

#3349. Junk bonds are 'flashing yellow.' Watch these warning signs

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Cracks are forming in the junk bond market as investors demand higher payouts for owning the market's riskiest debt. It isn't time to ditch high-yield bonds, but investors should pay attention to the warning signs.

High-yield bonds now yield 8.1%, up from 7.22% a month ago. The increase reflects a jump in yields across the curve as investors bake in more inflation from high energy prices and other pressures, including concern about the deficit — hitting nearly $2 trillion in the fiscal year that ended Sept. 30.

The high-yield market is also showing stress on the credit side with spreads recently widening to levels not seen since April, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Credit spreads are the difference in yield between the bonds and Treasurys of similar maturities. Wider spreads mean investors are demanding higher yields for holding corporate debt, viewing it as riskier.

Spreads are at 315 basis points in the overall high-yield market, higher than a year ago but still below levels in March when they reached 346 bps. One basis point equals one one-hundredth of a percent, or 0.01%.

The high-yield market consists of bonds rated BB+ by S&P and Fitch and Ba1 and under by Moody's. The lowest-rated cohort, CCC and below, has seen the most movement with spreads climbing dramatically over the past year to roughly 1,250 bps.

Right now, the high-yield market is "flashing yellow" but is "far from red," said Michael Arone, chief investment strategist at State Street Investment Management.

It makes sense that investors are demanding more compensation for taking on additional credit risk as borrowing costs rise, he said.

Yields are elevated across the board with the 10-year Treasury reaching its highest level since 2002 earlier in the week.

"The bigger question is whether this is simply a repricing of interest rate risk, or the beginning of a more fundamental reassessment of credit quality," Arone said.

He's in a wait-and-see mode since earnings are still growing, interest-coverage ratios remain good, and while default rates have ticked up some, he believes it is not concerning.

That said, the starting point in spreads is likely weighing on investors' psyche, since they are still low by historical standards.

"There's a small margin of error here, which I also think raises the anxiety level," Arone explained. "The compensation that investors are receiving for taking on this credit risk isn't overwhelming relative to history, and therefore subtle changes in credit spreads can be concerning."

While there may be concerns about part of the lower-rated market, the overall high-yield market is in good fundamental shape.

In fact, credit quality is at a record high, with BB bonds making up over 60% of the market compared with 38% prior to the global financial crisis, said Kelley Gerrity, a fixed income strategist at Morgan Stanley Investment Management.

"We've had higher-quality companies coming in, and with higher rates now, you also have more discipline from companies that are more indebted ... just because of the higher cost of capital, so that actually is creating a bit of a healthier picture as well," she said.

The lowest tier of high yield has always been more speculative, since the bonds have a higher default risk. Therefore, it's no surprise that they are the ones seeing the most widening in spreads, said Collin Martin, head of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research.

The overall move in high-yield spreads have been "orderly," he said. "Are there cracks forming? I think the cracks are logical cracks with the lowest-rated bonds and it's too early to say that it's spreading to the broad credit market."

In addition, fluctuations within the CCC-rated and lower spreads have been idiosyncratic, Gerrity added. Morgan Stanley recently broke down the spreads in the lowest cohort into two buckets: performing assets and non-performing assets, which are defined as spreads above 1,000 bps.

The non-performing spread to worst is 2,818 bps, while the performing bucket — the biggest piece of the CCC market — is 461 bps, she said.

"It is isolated," she said. "I'm not necessarily sure that that will go away anytime soon, but it doesn't tell the story of overall concern and flashing caution for credit markets right now."

For now, investors should remain selective within higher yield, Gerrity advised.

"We're looking for the best relative value opportunities, and we think that exists within that single-B cohort, and so we're leaning into that," she said.

Investors should be concerned if there is a steep widening in the broader high-yield market.

But there's scant evidence of stress in the BB cohort. Spreads in that group are at 194 bps, up from 179 bps a year ago — although the move hasn't been in a straight line.

"We're going to be focusing our attention on what are considered some stronger businesses," Martin said. "If we start to see the markets demand higher spreads there also, that's what we'll be looking at to see if risks are really rising."

It's also important to understand that the Federal Reserve is raising rates in a strong economic environment, said R.J. Gallo, chief investment officer of global fixed income at Federated Hermes. The central bank is concerned about an inflation problem amid high fossil fuel prices due to the Iran war, while growth has been stronger than expected, he noted.

"With growth being good as the reason that the Fed is hiking, then you wouldn't expect high yield to blow out because growth means revenues stay up, means cash flow and profitability stays up," he explained.

High yield becomes a disaster when the economy is heading into a sharp economic downturn, Gallo said.

"That's when spreads really widen out," he said. "But a recession is not the odds-on bet. Now, talk to me in six months if the Fed keeps hiking over and over, if oil prices stay high for longer, well, then maybe we will start to wonder."

#4421. RuneScape: Dragonwilds Review - An Adventure In Busywork

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

RuneScape: Dragonwilds strikes a respectable balance between a fantasy RPG and survival crafting game. The variety of environments and hidden lore for those who want it give depth to an otherwise nondescript setting and offer many hours of content for those who can tolerate its crafting system. But, without having an attachment to the RuneScape series, or a love for chopping wood or mining ore, it’s difficult to enjoy past the opening chapter. Though there is fun to be had, I wish certain parts of it didn’t feel as tedious or locked behind a grind for materials.

Dragonwilds expands the RuneScape world and brings back familiar faces to ground players in the dragon-infested continent of Ashenfall. It revolves around the history of Ashenfall’s early settlers and the takedown of Dragon Queen Kuldra, told indirectly through glowing blue books and side quests. Those stories of grief and greed are interesting, but mostly a backdrop for common tasks like plundering temples and stabbing rats.

Most activities tie back to the crafting system, whether that’s foraging for food or smithing swords. You can build tools, weapons, armor, jewelry, furniture, structures, and more. Dragonwilds lets you spend as much time as you want farming materials, though some, like ores, are more necessary than others. Magic spells can speed up some of the monotony, but that also requires leveling up your skills enough to learn those spells.

Even with crafting at its core, Dragonwilds is bloated with workstations and items that are more laborious than fun to collect. I blame the increasingly high number of materials needed to craft higher-level weapons and armor, and the multiple station types that could have been combined into one. As you get stronger, the number of materials you need for your gear increases, too. For example, my gear now needs Fine Cloth woven from threads and gold bars. By the time I reached the halfway point of the game, I had separate stations for tools and weapons, armor, jewelry, bows and arrows, and magical weapons like wands.

Classes don’t really exist in Dragonwilds. Instead of explicitly choosing a class like in a traditional RPG, you swap between roles by switching your gear – like swapping from armor to robes to switch from melee to magic. Changing classes felt pointless because I could force my way through most challenges with a sword, and bows and wands are less effective given the circumstances. Many confrontations involve flanking from multiple sides, so long-range combat isn’t ideal. Magic isn’t bad, but not many creatures have magical weaknesses and melee can easily do more damage with attack speed and buffs.

Dragonwilds is massive. Just the first arc took 20 to 30 hours to finish before I slew my first dragon. Fellhollow, the second arc, took about the same time to complete its story quests, and it didn’t make me any more excited to reach Dowdun Reach because I adapted so quickly to expectations. At the start of each region, I expect to grind for materials for three to five hours before I could expect to survive an “expedition” from my base, let alone bungling around uncharted territory to complete a quest with vague instructions. Thankfully, this steep on-ramp can be smoothed over through a setting that eases the experience grind, but the base way to play encourages the tedium to set in. To its credit, Dragonwilds spices up exploration in each region with gimmicks like “soulrifting” in Fellholllow, which unlocks alternate planes and invisible bridges.

Ideally, you avoid dying in any game, but Dragonwilds twists the knife as much as a Soulslike; when your health depletes, you drop your entire inventory and teleport back to the last place you camped. To avoid the inconvenience of refilling your bag, it’s easier to run back to the gravestone to retrieve your inventory. I’m grateful that I don’t just lose all the items, and that Dragonwilds spawns gravestones outside of dungeons instead of making me go back inside to claim them. Still, it’s annoying to be unexpectedly overpowered and then need to dive back into dangerous territory just to avoid another crafting spree. You can turn this annoying convention off in the settings, but playing the game as intended leads to cumbersome situations.

For better or worse, Dragonwilds brings back the “old school” feeling of discovering what to do without overt handholding. An NPC may suggest a general direction, but won’t tell you that you need an Iron Pickaxe to mine a dragon's teeth to complete your quest. Players who have trouble understanding these hints could spend hours trying to decipher exactly how to find the items they need to progress.

Dragonwilds prioritizes the journey of completing quests over how easy they are to finish. After all, that means more time in the crafting system and discovering more about your environment, like rocks you didn’t know you could mine or hidden lore from a side quest you might have missed. Alternatively, it could lead to hours of repetitive grinding for materials, trying to unlock new clues or power-ups without getting any closer to the next objective.

RuneScape: Dragonwilds freshened my perspective on survival-crafting games with base-building and farming under dragon-dominated skies. I floundered for answers on how to progress to the next story quest without clear direction, and while I could explore in the meantime, other games in similar genres can offer the same thing with more entertainment along the way. In a genre full of compelling modern classics like Valheim, RuneScape: Dragonwilds is a difficult game to recommend.

#4724. Wellness Apps, Medically Tailored Meals Help Slow Weight Gain After GLP-1s

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Weight regain is common after stopping GLP-1 medications such as semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound).

In fact, previous research has shown that most people regain at least half of their lost weight within 1 year of stopping treatment, with weight regain often accelerating during the first 3–4 months.

However, a new study suggests that structured support, including a weight management app or medically tailored meals, may help slow weight regain after discontinuing GLP-1 medications.

The small study, published October 7 in Obesity Science & Practice, found that participants who received either intervention regained significantly less weight over four months than those receiving usual care.

The study included 39 adults with an average age of 50, an average weight of 189 pounds, and an average body mass index (BMI) of 31.4.

All participants had lost more than 10% of their body weight while taking GLP-1 medications and had stopped treatment within the previous 30 days.

All participants also completed questionnaires at the beginning and end of the study and used Bluetooth scales to track their weight at home throughout the four-month period.

Researchers randomly assigned participants to one of the following three groups:

Participants continued to receive care from their healthcare providers, which may include nutrition, exercise, and lifestyle counseling from a clinician or registered dietitian. The type and amount of support varied depending on the provider’s recommendations and available resources. The researchers did not provide any additional intervention.

Participants received a subscription to Noom, an app that uses behavioral strategies to support weight loss and maintenance. These include tracking food intake, exercise, and weight, as well as educational lessons on the psychological, physiological, social, and environmental factors that influence eating behaviors.

The app also provides tools to help users recognize and manage triggers that may lead to overeating. Participants were encouraged to use the app for at least 15 minutes daily, including completing lessons, logging food, and tracking their weight.

These are typically nutrient-dense meals developed by registered dietitians to meet the nutritional needs of people with specific health conditions, such as chronic kidney disease or uncontrolled type 2 diabetes.

For this study, participants in this group selected 10 portion-controlled, “weight wellness” meals each week from more than 70 options, which were delivered to their homes.

After four months, participants who received either medically tailored meals or access to the digital weight management app experienced significantly less weight regain than those receiving usual care.

The estimated weight regain was 10.75% of body weight in the usual-care group, compared with 3.6% in the Noom group and 3.58% in the medically tailored meals group.

Although participants in all three groups regained weight, the findings suggest that structured nutritional or behavioral support may help limit weight regain after stopping GLP-1 medications.

“These findings highlight the benefits of ongoing support for patients on a weight loss journey,” said Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA. Ali wasn’t involved in the study.

Denise Pate, MD, a board certified internist with Medical Offices of Manhattan who was also not involved in the study, told Healthline that the findings are significant “because they show that obesity management is multifactorial, not only medication, but the combination of types of meals and behavioral change all play a role.”

However, because this was a small, four-month study, larger, longer-term trials are needed to determine whether these approaches can help people maintain weight loss over time after stopping GLP-1 medications.

Ali noted that research suggests GLP-1 medications are most effective for weight management when taken long-term.

“Obesity needs to be recognized and treated as a chronic condition, similar to diabetes or hypertension,” he said.

Pate agreed, emphasizing that obesity is not always recognized as a chronic condition that may require ongoing treatment.

“I feel that the medical community, insurance, and the population as a whole have yet to fully accept obesity as a chronic medical condition, which it most definitely is for a large part of patients. For this reason, I think patients should stay on these medications long term if needed,” she said.

“GLP-1 medications are highly effective tools for transitioning to a healthier diet and lifestyle, which for most people may require long-term use,” Ali added.

Pate explained that one of the goals of GLP-1 medications is to help people eat smaller portions by recognizing when they feel full and stopping when they do.

“I tell my patients from the start, this is the goal,” she said, adding that she hopes patients will retain those habits after stopping treatment.

Ali recommended maintaining a balanced diet rich in protein and vegetables while limiting added sugars and refined carbohydrates.

He added that tracking apps and regular follow-up appointments with a healthcare professional or registered dietitian may also help people maintain their progress.

“Patients need to understand that the GLP medication is not a miracle drug, although with the dramatic weight loss, it can be perceived as such. It is only a tool to be used in conjunction with an exercise routine as well as a healthy diet,” Pate said.

#3356. Living with Mom and trading on margin. The regular investor gets in on Wall Street's record stock borrowing binge

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

HOUSTON — During a late night at home in May 2021, Hy Luu, a 29-year-old engineering consultant, was researching how to obtain more capital to invest in shares of Tesla.

That's when he discovered margin investing: the act of borrowing money from a broker to buy securities such as stocks, while using existing investments as collateral. Borrowing would give him more buying power, and he could pay it back as the stock appreciated. The main risk would be a margin call, or having to deposit more money into the account, if its value fell.

It was all too good to be true, Luu thought.

Luu — who lives with his mother, Kim Nguyen, in a three-bedroom, two-bathroom house in Houston — decided to get her opinion on the topic, given that she used to work as an accountant and just so happened to still be awake that evening. The 66-year-old retiree found it interesting.

That was the reassurance he needed, and it wasn't long before he started using margin to invest in Tesla for the first time. A year or so later, he had amassed more than $100,000 of margin debt.

"I went really crazy," he told CNBC in an interview. "The stock was going up, and so I was like, 'There's no way I could lose.'"

After five years of margin investing — including a period in 2022 where he worried that further declines in Tesla could trigger a margin call — Luu is working to pay down his largest margin balance ever. In June, he exercised $165,000 on five Tesla call options and bought 500 shares at $330 per share. After accounting for the cash in his account, this drove his borrowing to more than $156,000.

Tesla swung from below $370 a share to more than $430 during that month.

Between his Tesla investments, his company stock and 401(k) — while subtracting his margin debt as well as other debt he's accumulated, such as his car loan and student loans — Luu's net worth has skyrocketed to more than $800,000 from negative territory seven years ago.

"I'm very close to becoming a millionaire," the investor said. He did stress, "What I'm doing is risky. I don't recommend anyone do it."

Yet, Luu has found himself part of a growing trend taking hold in the market.

Retail investors like Luu are borrowing more and more money to invest. Robinhood's margin book grew to a record $21.6 billion in the second quarter, up 127% year over year and a 332% increase from the $5 billion seen in Q2 2024. To margin invest on the platform, eligible users must have a portfolio value of at least $2,000.

"Robinhood has made a really concerted effort to go after customers that are actively using margin because they're the most active traders," said Devin Ryan, head of financial technology research at Citizens.

Brokerage firms can benefit from margin investing by collecting interest on margin loans, while highly active customers can also generate additional trading-related revenue.

"If you look at the offers that Robinhood has had in the market over the past couple of years, it's been targeting those customers with obviously transfer bonuses, but also very low margin rates, very high yields on their cash," Ryan said.

To be sure, other parts of Robinhood's business are also growing, including retirement assets — which have nearly quadrupled in the last two years.

Another factor fueling the rise in margin investing is easier access through smartphones, according to Ryan.

"Having very little friction in investing and having everything at your fingertips, and then having a really seamless mobile experience where it's not clunky, I think, plays a big part of that," he said.

A Robinhood spokesperson told CNBC its products are designed to appeal to a number of customers, saying that "we don't believe it's accurate to characterize" its transfer promotions as specifically targeting "active traders who are trading on margin or may trade on margin." The company said it tries to "meet users where they are," and they "reject any implication" that the company is "encouraging risky trading."

Robinhood's margin debt isn't the only one reaching records. Total margin debt hit an all-time high of around $1.5 trillion in June, per the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, which incorporates data on both institutional investors such as hedge funds and retail investors.

Not all of that debt is driven by fresh borrowing due to new borrowing decisions. For example, as equities rise in value, an investor may need to cover a short position that's been marked to market, or valued at its current price.

Nonetheless, Sam Huszczo, chief investment officer at SGH Wealth Management, thinks the market's bull run — which has seen multiple record highs this year alone — is feeding a shift in investor psychology, and margin debt is one sign.

"That's greed," the founder said, adding that it's "not everywhere" in the market, but it's "certainly creeping into pockets."

"Margin, that's only the tip of the iceberg of what we can see," he continued. "Fear of missing out is just the emotion of jealousy with a brokerage account."

Huszczo considers the market to actually be "way healthier" than people think. Though he recognizes that increasing margin debt and the rise of risky assets such as leveraged ETFs inject more fragility in the system.

"People need to differentiate between what's a legitimate momentum growth investment and what's reckless chasing," Huszczo also said.

Huszczo shares the commonly held belief among financial advisors that investors' portfolios shouldn't be overly concentrated in one stock or even one sector.

Yet Luu has been zeroed in on Tesla ever since he bought his first share in late 2019. Earlier that year, he had started his current job — a time when he knew nothing about investing, he said. It wasn't until a co-worker told him to download Robinhood months later that he began looking into stocks and other assets.

"I started making money, and I quickly learned, 'Now I'm making money, what do I do with it?'" he said. "I started investing in index funds and even in crypto, and then I did the math. I looked at the returns, and I was like, 'This is very slow returns. I'm never gonna reach retirement, financial independence until like 65.'"

Little did he know that a decision he had made that summer would become his guiding light.

"I was choosing between a Honda Civic and a Tesla Model 3, and my friend, he scheduled a test drive for me to drive the Model 3," Luu said. "My plan was only to go into it just to test drive it. I wasn't going to buy anything. I wasn't going to put in any reservation. Long story short, after the test drive, I put in my reservation the same day, and I got the car like two weeks later."

Seeing the value the car provides in his life, Luu said he can envision a future where electric and autonomous vehicles are commonplace. Because of how much he believes in that future, he considers Tesla the "easiest" investment he's made, and over the course of seven years of investing in the company, he has generally held his shares.

However, things got a bit more difficult with his strategy when Tesla shares began falling in 2022, putting him in the vulnerable position of potentially receiving a margin call. 2022 was Tesla's worst year on record, with the stock finishing off the year at $123.18 after a roughly 65% decline.

In search of more capital, Luu made a serious financial decision with his mother's approval.

"I refinanced the house to get access to an extra $30,000 of capital, and I used that to pay down my margin debt," he said.

Luu understands the level of risk that accompanies trading with margin. Because he's been using margin for most of the nearly seven years he's been investing in Tesla, he has deemed his investing strategy "gambling with an asterisk."

For now, it has paid off. In the last five years, Tesla shares have risen more than 43% to $375. However, the volatile stock is about 25% off of its all-time high near $499.

"It's very easy to see it as gambling, because obviously all your money is in one stock, and that's inherently risky," he said. "But the reason why I say there's nuance to it is because it's a matter of investing temperament, financial discipline and time horizon."

With a time horizon of "many decades," a temperament he labels as "very calm and collected" and a "very high" conviction in Tesla, Luu believes he's "at no risk of panic selling," even when the market tanks.

Still, the situation points out another danger: Missing an opportunity to make an investment when an equity is undervalued. When Luu was scrambling to raise funds to meet his margin obligation, it reduced his flexibility to purchase more Tesla shares at the lower price.

Margin levels this high have been followed by risk-off periods in equities.

Whenever monthly margin debt has grown above 45% year over year since 1960, there were declines in the stock market five out of six times, data from Fundstrat Global Advisors shows. June's margin debt record came in at 49%, but the S&P 500 has still added more than 13% in 2026. A swing into the red by year-end in accordance with this historic pattern would be a dramatic move — one that would be considered highly unusual for the stock market.

The firm also found that the median performance for S&P 500 forward returns following a breakout of 40% year-over-year margin debt growth has been worse off 12 months later as opposed to one month after it. To be exact, the S&P 500's median 12-month forward return is a 4.2% decline, while the median one-month return is a drop of 2.2%.

In essence, the more rapidly margin debt rises, the worse it is for S&P 500 returns over 12 months.

While margin debt doesn't necessarily cause S&P 500 declines, it can exacerbate existing moves to the downside if there's a significant amount of leverage in the market, said Hardika Singh, economic strategist at Fundstrat.

"The premise of gains beget gains, it turns into losses beget losses, and that's what's troublesome," Singh said. "You have these losses, they keep piling up, and then the stock gets sold, and everyone's starting to close out their position, and it becomes this huge train wreck that's very hard to stop, and it adds a lot of volatility to the market."

Then again, Singh sees why investors like Luu are leaning into such a risky strategy. "No one ever got rich by diversifying," she said.

Luu, who said he's been a "hobbyist" from an early age, has folded in investing as one of his many hobbies, which include activities like climbing and playing guitar. It's become such a hobby that he's created his own social media account detailing his investing journey.

Even if Luu reaches his financial goals, he "wouldn't ever stop investing," he said. Instead, he said he "would probably start investing in SpaceX."

It's not just Luu who views trading as a hobby.

Fundstrat's Singh explained that American traders, particularly younger ones, feel they have to take bigger risks to get ahead. She said it's part of a broader trend dubbed financial nihilism, in which young investors grappling with a tough job market and high inflation turn to less-stable investing tools.

"It makes a lot of sense why individuals, specifically retail investors, keep gravitating towards this margin debt," the economic strategist added. "This is like a drug, and I think people just can't get enough of it in this economy."

Kevin Davis, a 59-year-old retail investor who made a living as a rapper in the 1980s and '90s before becoming a stock broker, believes the risks with leveraged trading outweigh the rewards.

"It's gambling. You're borrowing money you don't have, and so if it goes the other way, now you have a clock on your money, and it's ticking," said Davis, who owns an investing technology platform. "The market's not going to save you. It never does."

"Don't play the margin game," the Florida resident warned.

Luu became an engineering consultant because his father, who died of lung cancer in 2012, exposed him to computing. His mother said that between her two sons, Luu most resembles his father not only in terms of his features but also his character.

Reflecting on what her late husband might have thought about Luu's investing pursuits, Nguyen paused for a moment, holding a slight smile.

She then said, "He would've said he's proud of him."

#4463. Echo Weaver Review - Discovery Joy And Challenging Platforming

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Like most games born of Metroid, Echo Weaver’s inspiration is obvious. You explore and fill out a large, continuous map in a mysterious location and your abilities expand as you progress. Its other presumed inspiration, however, Outer Wilds, only makes itself apparent as you dig deeper into the unique structure of the game. You are stuck in a time loop in Echo Weaver, and it dictates what you can do and what you know about your suite of abilities. You don’t unlock new traversal moves and expanded weapons as much as you remember you literally had them all along. It makes the reward of discovery powerful in Echo Weaver, but it also creates frustrating barriers to progress.

As the titular Weaver, the character basically knows as much as the player holding the controller. You have a boomerang-like glaive you throw primarily to solve puzzles, and though it can be used to attack enemies, you won’t encounter very many. You also quickly learn that every few minutes, a vague calamity strikes, and you are forced back in time to start over. You keep your map progress and obviously everything that you’ve learned, and that’s more than enough to make death or resets not feel too unfair.

What really sets Echo Weaver apart is that many of your most useful abilities are not ones that you unlock, but rather ones you learn exist at some point along the way. For example, you can set your glaive on fire from the moment you start the game, but you won’t learn how until you’ve made significant progress. Mechanically, this is basically how every Metroid-inspired game works: Make enough progress and you will be able to do a new thing. But learning the often complicated procedures necessary for these later abilities that you could always do makes the reveal all the more rewarding. You weren’t given the ability to set your glaive on fire – you figured it out yourself. And that’s a good feeling.

This all plays into the time loop structure, too. The shortcuts needed to get to certain areas so you have more time to explore them were there all along – you just didn’t know how to see them. It’s why Echo Weaver feels more like a complicated puzzle box than a search action game where you’re killing the same creatures over and over, and I appreciate how that sets it apart from other games in the genre.

When you’re figuring things out and making good progress, it feels like you’re working in tandem with the time loop. But on the occasions when you’re not solving the puzzles you need, which is frequent, it can be frustrating. Running out of time when you’re trying to explore or just figure one last thing out becomes a burden, and the best course of action is often to just reset the time loop (essentially killing yourself), which feels like giving up in a moment that you don’t want to.

The worst moments of the time loop coming to a close are when you’re engaged in a complicated platforming gauntlet. Echo Weaver’s greatest shortcoming is that its jumping doesn’t feel great. Leaping is solid for general exploration, but when you’re trying to make it through thickets of thorns and endless pits that steal your health (and time), the action struggles to keep up with the demand. And when you’ve finally made it through and time loops just before you hit that checkpoint, it can be incredibly disheartening. Though resetting and trying again is built into the game’s design so it doesn’t become too much of a burden, I did hit a handful of distracting bugs primarily related to areas not loading properly when I fast-traveled to them.

Echo Weaver’s unique approach to the Metroid structure is admirable, unique, and well executed. The sense of discovery is fantastic, and though I realistically probably won’t immediately play the game again, the idea that I could start with knowledge of basically all of the abilities to rip through the game at high speed is an enticing one. Developer Moonlight Kids puts a lot of trust in the player to figure out how it all works, and the reward is palpable. That trust leads to some frustrated, aimless wandering, and the platforming challenges occasionally venture into feeling unfair, but by the end you will feel like a genius. That’s a good feeling to leave a game with.

#4489. Clive Barker’s Hellraiser: Revival Review – An Angel To Few, A Buggy Demon To Many

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Clive Barker’s Hellraiser: Revival lives up to the horror franchise’s name by delivering indescribable suffering. Unfortunately, it’s inflicted on the players, and there’s no ecstasy to be found in what’s often a miserable experience. Despite boasting an alluringly dark, faithfully gore-filled presentation and borrowing from a successful first-person survival horror blueprint, the game stumbles both intentionally and functionally thanks to a litany of bugs.

Aiden Lynch is the uninteresting lead at the center of this tale. He must rescue his girlfriend, Sunny, after she unlocks the series’ dreaded puzzle box and is whisked away into the hellish Labyrinth by the Cenobites, led by the iconic Pinhead (with Doug Bradley reprising the role and unsurprisingly killing it). To restore Sunny's mortal body, Lynch must navigate the Labyrinth's dangers while battling a Cenobite-obsessed cult in the human world. This decent story is one in which the bland protagonist is the least memorable part, and has interesting things happen to and around him; he reminds me of an edgier version of Resident Evil's Ethan Winters.

The story is propped up by a few solid scares and plenty of unsettling moments, plus neat callbacks for fans while remaining an accessible entry point for newcomers. Hellraiser: Revival commendably doesn't skimp on the franchise’s disturbing gore and sexual content while never crossing a truly problematic line for the latter. Unfortunately, any entertainment the story elicits is hampered by cinematic and in-game cutscenes that frequently suffer from poor lip syncing, subpar audio mixing that muffles quieter voice lines, and subtitles that sometimes don't match the dialogue or fail to appear at all.

The first-person blend of gunplay and melee lacks weight, with shooting feeling particularly bad thanks to unwieldy aiming. While basic combat is unremarkable, weaponizing the puzzle box adds some cooler wrinkles, like using telekinesis to grab and toss environmental objects (like dismembering foes with saw blades) or absorbing torchfire to lob at targets like I was X-Men's Pyro. Quick-turning by pressing the left stick downward is awful because it's so easy to trigger accidentally while sprinting. Many times I'd run to or from something only to whip around in the opposite direction because my thumb slipped, and there’s no option to remap the input.

Combat and stealth can be an uninteresting breeze since the sadomasochistic cultists have more sex drive than brain cells. They shoot at walls, occasionally blow themselves up with their own grenades, take cover infrequently, barely pursue when I flee, and lack any real tactics. I know they get off on experiencing pain, but they should have some sense of urgency or self-preservation. Other encounters are often nightmarish due to the game’s habit of flooding players with mobs of aggressive, mutated humans called Unworthy while frequently providing insufficient resources to survive. The resource economy is completely off. I found abundant ingredients to craft certain items, like basic bandages, that suddenly became nigh impossible to find/craft in the later hours despite searching just as thoroughly. Surviving by the skin of your teeth is a pillar of the genre, but I'd go long stretches unable to craft anything.

Money to purchase goods is just as limited, so I was ill-prepared for several battles. The store also frustrates, as certain items are on sale one moment and randomly unavailable the next. I have no idea why that is or what it adds. Saving up for an AK-47 before a big fight, only for the store to not sell it when I need it, is flat-out dumb. Things have a habit of being absent when you need them most, whether battling armored enemies without the blunt melee weapons ideal for fighting them or entering boss fights with a sliver of health. These instances didn’t make me think, “What a fun challenge!” Instead, I would groan, “Seriously, what do you want from me here?”

A lack of polish further spoils the experience. I once beat one of the primary antagonists by routing him around a table and filling him with bullets as he slowly chased me around without any deviation from his doomed strategy. Enemies trap themselves in geometry; the biggest example was witnessing a major boss get completely stuck and glitch out on a car it climbed onto in what I can only assume was supposed to be a scripted moment.

The only relative reprieve from combat is exploration-focused segments in the Labyrinth, where you dodge traps or awkwardly guide blocks to solve navigation problems using the sometimes finicky telekinesis. The Labyrinth areas have the strongest art direction and mood of Hellraiser: Revival; the macabre visuals look great. Since they lack combat, trips to the Labyrinth are consistently decent, but even these segments aren't bug-free. A chase sequence with the Chatterer lost all of its tension when he became stuck behind what I guess is the Cenobites’ greatest weakness: a stone block. To “reset” him, I had to turn around, walk under the suspended block he was stuck on, and backtrack a good distance before the sequence fixed itself and we resumed our chase. It felt like going through a shoddy haunted house where the animatronics keep breaking down, and the lights come on to reveal the rusted machinery running everything.

There’s no way around it; Hellraiser: Revival is a technical mess, and it falls apart more and more as the roughly 10-hour adventure progresses. The most recurring issues are bad environmental pop-in and characters and objects constantly flickering in and out of existence, as if the game itself is prepared to collapse at any moment. I often fell through floors when knocked down, giving me glimpses of the hidden skybox below it before I stood up. I once hit an enemy through a solid wall, robbing me of any potential loot. Audio sometimes drops out completely, such as when I executed a large threat in silence when it should have sounded like a mess of gore. A subtitle once lingered on screen long after a conversation finished. The healing animation froze midway during a fight, leaving me vulnerable to enemies and leading to a cheap death. A combat encounter while waiting for an elevator to arrive failed to recognize that I killed everyone, forcing me to reload a save and do it again.

After watching Hellraiser: Revival’s finale stumble through the same visual and audio potholes plaguing the rest of the journey, I was angry. But upon taking time to cool down and reflect, my ire shifted to disappointment and relief that I didn't have to play more. During the rare moments Hellraiser: Revival isn't on fire in some way, it's a halfway respectable take on the popular horror franchise. I just wish playing it didn't so often feel like enduring the twisted machinations of Pinhead himself.

People who adhere to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet do not experience significant changes in potassium levels while taking blood pressure medications or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) drugs, according to a new study.

Researchers also reported that people taking these medications do not experience significant changes in their kidney function.

Both types of medications are known to increase potassium levels. High potassium levels can lead to hyperkalemia, a condition that can cause issues with the heart rhythm. People with impaired kidney function due to chronic kidney disease are at increased risk of hyperkalemia.

“The DASH diet is a well-known, proven nutrition pattern that is known to improve blood pressure,” said Marian Budu, MD, a clinical research coordinator on the GoFresh Trials at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and the presenting author of the study, in a news release.

“Knowing that the DASH diet did not increase blood potassium levels in this population with normal baseline potassium levels provides assurance that the DASH diet can be prescribed to a wide range of people,” she continued.

The preliminary findings are being presented at the American Heart Association’s Hypertension Scientific Sessions 2026 meeting this week in Virginia. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the research provides medical professionals with important information.

“While we have traditionally exercised caution before recommending the DASH diet in patients who are prescribed ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or potassium-sparing diuretics, these results show that those patients could likely adopt the DASH diet without significant concern for harm,” said Cheng-Han Chen, MD, an interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA.

“DASH diets do not appear to increase potassium levels to any significant degree in patients taking medications that are known to potentially raise serum potassium,” added Kevin Volpp, MD, the founding director of the Penn Center for Health Incentives and Behavioral Economics in Pennsylvania and the scientific lead of the American Heart Association’s Health Care by Food initiative.

“This is important because it is a theoretical concern that diets rich in potassium could cause high levels in potassium in some patients.”

The new study was a secondary analysis of the GoFresh and GoFresh Rx randomized trials that enrolled Black adults in the greater Boston area with high blood pressure.

Some of the participants were taking medications to lower their blood pressure, and others were not taking any hypertension drugs.

The study focused on four classes of blood pressure medications: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and potassium-sparing diuretics and beta-blockers, as well as NSAIDs.

The analysis took place in 2026 and included 356 Black adults with an average age of 53. About 70% were women.

At the start of the study, the participants had systolic blood pressure readings between 120 mm Hg and less than 150 mm Hg. They also had a diastolic blood pressure reading of less than 100 mm Hg.

The participants also had varying degrees of kidney function. In addition, they had limited access to grocery stores.

Half of the participants received three months of weekly DASH-patterned groceries and weekly nutrition counseling sessions. The other half received a cash stipend every four weeks to buy groceries, with no nutrition counseling.

The participants’ blood potassium levels were measured at the beginning of the study and again after 12 weeks.

The researchers reported that eating more potassium-rich foods in the DASH diet did not increase potassium levels to dangerous levels among participants taking blood pressure medications.

They noted no significant increase in potassium levels, regardless of whether participants were assigned to the dietary intervention or the monetary stipend group.

In a news release, the researchers acknowledged that their study did not include people with stage 4 or stage 5 kidney disease or those with higher-than-normal potassium levels.

They added that the study population comprised only Black individuals living in Boston, so the results may not generalize to the broader population.

The study authors said that future research should focus on participants with suboptimal kidney function and those with higher potassium levels.

Volpp agreed that more research is needed.

“This study was conducted in patients with non-elevated potassium levels at baseline and does not answer the question of whether they are safe in patients with elevated potassium levels or significant impairments in renal function,” he told Healthline.

Chen noted that potassium levels should be taken seriously.

“Certain classes of blood pressure medications can potentially cause high potassium levels, especially in people with kidney disease,” he told Healthline. “If potassium levels become too high, this can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and muscle pain, or even serious heart problems such as arrhythmias.”

The DASH diet is a dietary plan that emphasizes fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins such as chicken, fish, and beans.

The plan also includes whole grains, low fat dairy products, and cooking with vegetable oils. The diet discourages the intake of red meat, salt, fat, and added sugars.

Previous research has indicated that the DASH diet can provide numerous health benefits.

A preliminary 2022 study concluded that the dietary plan can help lower blood pressure and improve heart health, but those results haven’t been published in a scientific journal. Still, the findings were consistent with a 2019 study that found the DASH diet can help lower the risk of heart disease.

In addition, a 2020 study reported that adults aged 65 and older with obesity lost body fat while following the DASH Diet. A 2023 study suggested the diet can help reduce belly fat.

A 2019 meta-analysis suggested that the DASH diet may also help lower the risk of certain cancers.

Many of these protective effects are attributed to the diet’s high fruit and vegetable content.

Chen said the DASH diet has many benefits, but the eating plan might not be for everybody.

“The DASH diet has been shown to be beneficial in lowering blood pressure, losing weight, and controlling blood sugar and cholesterol levels,” he said. “However, the amount of planning and portion tracking required to follow the diet may be too much for some people.”

Chen recommended that people ease into the DASH diet.

“The best way to start on the DASH diet is to slowly introduce the changes to your meals,” he said. “This can be as simple as including a few extra servings of fruits and vegetables each day as well as swapping some whole grain products for refined grains. Over time, more of the diet can be incorporated.”

Volpp agreed that introducing the DASH diet in increments is a good strategy.

“For many people, it is easiest to make small incremental changes sequentially,” he said. “One way to get started is to increase fruit by a couple of servings a day. Another is to pay attention to the amount of sodium in packaged foods and consciously choose products lower in sodium. A third is to make a conscious effort to eat more vegetables.”

“Make changes first that are easiest for you to make; it will be easier to make those changes stick,” Volpp added.

Muramasa: The Demon Blade is a Nintendo Wii exclusive and a beloved title among fans of developer Vanillaware, later enhanced with a PlayStation Vita remaster in Muramasa Rebirth in 2013. Rebirth also brought Genroku Legends, a standalone DLC with new stories and playable characters to the game’s gorgeous, painterly side-scrolling action. In a touch of painful irony, Rebirth has remained locked to the Vita, just as The Demon Blade was to the Wii before. Finally, though, Vanillaware is teaming up with publisher Marvelous to bring all of Muramasa’s history to modern consoles.

Dubbed Muramasa: Revenant Blades, this 4K remaster combines Rebirth and its Genroku Legends DLC into one cohesive package – you can lay that Wii and dusty Vita to rest now. I played roughly an hour of it in a quiet Marvelous office space in Tokyo’s Shinagawa district, and have been staring at my Switch 2 ever since, doing my best to manifest its immediate existence on the console. I haven’t touched the Muramasa series in well over a decade, but I didn’t miss a beat, slicing my way through the game’s first two acts on the Moral (see: hard) difficulty. It was tough, but that challenge only pushed me to pay even closer attention to what I was doing in this classic Vanillaware action-platformer beat ‘em up. I already can’t wait for more.

Muramasa has always been a looker of a series, but in this renewed 4K presentation, completely redrawn by Vanillaware’s artists, it is especially beautiful. Set during Japan’s Edo period, under the reign of shogun Tokugawa Tsunayoshi (who was in office from 1680 to 1709), it features the feudal Japanese visuals you’re imagining. As you move from left to right, or right to left, or up and down, or some combination of these directions, parsing through different screens within a given level, massive mountains hover in the background; trees bend and rotate as you walk through the forest, giving way to a labyrinthine vibe that protagonist Kisuke is likely also feeling; Machiya-style houses stack atop and beside Nagaya homes in cities, creating an endless puzzle of buildings you can enter to search or fight outside.

Combat remains as fun as always. Though it plays like a beat ‘em up, you use swords, so the result is a frenetic hack-and-slash experience that’s easy to understand, with plenty of depth to master. This Revenant Blades package promises over 100 swords to forge and dozens of skills to upgrade and master for your kit. Upgraded controls make all of it feel better to play, too, and the resulting combat reminds me of 2D fighters as I juggle ninjas and samurai in the air, countering their counters, and clashing blades to ensure I’m not hit – it’s intense, requiring all my focus to not die.

My learnings are put to the test against the two bosses I face in my hour-long preview: Kurozaru, a hulking menace that’s fast and uses shurikens and AOE attacks to stop me from getting too close for too long, and some giant centipedes, which crawl around the entire level and require me to slash away at different bits of their bodies to finally kill them.

There’s plenty more in the game I don’t get to see, like the other five stories to play through, each with new localization and English voiceover, additional skills and katanas to unlock, and the ways unseen parts look in this 4K remaster. But what I did get to see and play is enough to ensure I’ll be working my way through the rest next year. Until then, I’ll keep staring at my Switch 2.
