#3798. Queen Camilla Sparkles in an Heirloom Tiara and Show-Stopping Amethysts

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

It was a night of tiara triumphs as King Charles III and Queen Camilla hosted a state banquet in honor of the Sultan of Oman.

Kate Middleton and Prince William, Princess Anne and Sir Timothy Laurence, and the Duke and Duchess of Gloucester all donned their white tie finery for the Windsor Castle event.

The dress code called for tiaras, and the royal women did not disappoint. The Princess of Wales sparkled in her favourite Lover's Knot Tiara, Princess Anne returned once again to the Princess Andrew Meander, and the Duchess of Gloucester dazzled in the Gloucester Honeysuckle Tiara.

The Queen, meanwhile, paid tribute to Queen Elizabeth II in the Girls of Britain and Ireland Tiara, arguably the most famous piece in the late monarch's collection.

King Charles and Queen Camilla hosted the Sultan of Oman for a state banquet at Windsor Castle

But the adornments did not stop there. Queen Camilla added amethyst earrings that belonged to Queen Victoria's mother, the Duchess of Kent. Around her neck was a show-stopping amethyst necklace that has not previously been seen, although it is possible it was crafted using gems from the royal collection.

On Camilla's wrists were two more impressive heirloom jewels, namely two art deco Cartier bracelets that previously belonged to the Queen Mother. The bracelets are from a set of five different bracelets; two are entirely encrusted in diamonds and the other three feature diamonds alongside a different precious stone: sapphires, emeralds and rubies.

The bracelets were acquired in the 1920s by the Queen Mother when she was Duchess of York. She enjoyed wearing all five bracelets together, notes royal jewelry authority The Court Jeweller, particularly in the early years following her wedding to the Duke of York (later King George VI) in 1923. The bracelets could also be fashioned as a tiara: worn as three strands stacked one on top of the other.

Queen Camilla wore two of the Queen Mother's Art Deco Cartier bracelets to join King Charles III at the White House state banquet in March

The Art Deco bracelets later passed from the Queen Mother to her daughter, Queen Elizabeth II, who wore the bracelets as individual pieces on a number of occasions. One of her final outings in the jewels was to the Diplomatic Reception at Buckingham Palace in 2019.

In the years since the queen's death, they have become a regular fixture of Queen Camilla's wardrobe. The Queen often chooses the gems that best match the color of her outfit for a cohesive look.

The emerald bracelet, for example, proved the perfect choice when she was draped in a rich green dress for a reception celebrating Shakespeare at Buckingham Palace in 2024. Wearing red for the Qatar state banquet later that year, the ruby bracelet was the finishing touch. And for a Highgrove House event in 2025, the Queen accessorized her sapphire blue dress with actual sapphires.

Queen Elizabeth II wore the bracelets on several occasions, including the diamond and emerald piece for the Diplomatic Reception at Buckingham Palace in December 2019

This thematic dressing followed through to the state banquet in Washington, DC, in March 2026, where the diamond and ruby piece was the final glittering addition to her pink ensemble.

The Sultan of Oman was met in London by Prince William on Tuesday morning before the pair travelled together to Windsor, where the majority of the royal engagements took place. The Sultanate of Oman owns a number of London residences, including Nuffield Lodge, 35 Upper Brook Street, and the embassy at 167 Queen’s Gate.

The Sultan made his way to the Royal Dais on Dachet Road in Windsor, where he was officially welcomed by King Charles and Queen Camilla as the sound of a Royal Salute was fired from the Home Park and the Tower of London. The trio then joined the Prince and Princess of Wales for a carriage procession to Windsor Castle, as has become tradition in recent state visits.

At a reception celebrating Shakespeare in 2024, the Queen wore the emerald and diamond bracelet (just seen) as the finishing touch for her emerald dress

State visits are traditionally hosted at Buckingham Palace, but it remains under renovation.

Upon arrival in the Quadrangle of the Castle, the royals were greeted with a Royal Salute by a Guard of Honor while the regimental band played the Omani and British National Anthems. The Sultan and King Charles inspected the Guard before all five royals viewed a military parade. This was be followed by a lunch in the State Dining Room, with other members of the royal family, and a special exhibition of Oman-related items in the Green Drawing Room.

The high point of the state visit, however, was the state banquet. King Charles III and Queen Camilla were supported by the Prince and Princess of Wales, Princess Anne and Sir Timothy Laurence and the Duke and Duchess of Gloucester, as they welcomed the Sultan and some 150 esteemed guests to St George's Hall, Windsor Castle.

In late 2024, for the state banquet held in honour of the Emir of Qatar, Queen Camilla chose the ruby and diamond piece to offset her scarlet gown

Ever since the Trump administration increased tariffs on foreign automakers, the automotive landscape hasn’t been the same. Most brands competing in the major U.S. market have been trying to find ways to survive and stay competitive. One reason why the current government increased the tariffs was to convince more of these car makers to build their cars in the U.S. instead.

One major car brand has just called the administration’s bluff, with a major announcement concerning local American production.

Audi just announced that it’s set to build a $4 billion assembly and manufacturing plant in Georgia. This new facility is set to produce 200,000 SUVs annually and will also generate more vital American jobs. Moreover, the brand says that this new investment is part of a long-term strategy for profitable growth as well as showing its focus on customers’ needs.

This will mark a major milestone for the Audi brand, as it will be its first assembly facility in the U.S. According to Audi Chairman of the Board Gernot Doellner, “We have much greater ambitions for Audi in America. The United States is one of the world’s most important premium markets, and we have not yet realized our full potential. We are determined to change that — with premium products, a stronger brand and a competitive local production base. This is about building a stronger Audi business in America — not only about building a factory.”

Audi says the plant will create 4,500 new jobs, with construction set to begin next year and production expected to start in the early 2030s. A U.S. official said that the new Georgia plant will produce the Q7, Q8, and Q9 SUVs. Furthermore, Audi also has plans to build 25,000 cars per year in South Carolina. The South Carolina facility is for the Scout brand, another Volkswagen subsidiary, and will cost $2 billion.

Just last year, Trump’s tariffs cost Audi $1.4 billion because it brings most of its vehicles to the U.S. market from Mexico or Europe. Sales also haven’t been so good in the past two years; in the first half of this year, sales slipped 7%, and 2026 sales are already down 16%. US Senator Bernie Moreno, an Ohio Republican, said that Audi’s major investment in Georgia would never have happened without tariffs or without eliminating California’s ability to regulate fuel economy standards.

#5455. New Li Auto i6 Goes 811 km (504 Miles)

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Li Auto’s new i6 electric vehicle has a very interesting look. It makes me think of something out of some futuristic movie or The Jetsons. It certainly has an MPV (multipurpose vehicle) design that is current in China but not really anywhere else. Anyway, aside from the futuristic, cool styling, the new i6 has great specs and features.

The i6 has a CLTC-rated range of 780 km (485 miles), but the company recently livestreamed a drive in which the vehicle drove 811 km (504 miles) before reaching 0%. This was using 11.8 kWh/100 km. The vehicle has a 96 kWh battery.

Interestingly, Li Auto has shifted away from using CATL batteries. It is using in-house batteries that CALB and Sunwoda helped to manufacture. “Dian Dong Che Zhu Ren, a Chinese automotive outlet, reports that this design change allows the i6 to carry an extra 3 kWh of cells. It also claims that Li Auto’s latest SiC power modules feature revised current paths that reduce operational losses and add 30 km of winter driving range,” CarNewsChina writes.

“The 5C charging-capable Lian Shan battery is likely to roll out to Li Auto’s existing lineup, and the automaker is also betting big on its safety capabilities. It pledges that Lian Shan packs will never self-ignite or combust in collisions, and like Xiaomi’s Dragonscale batteries, Li Auto is offering up a free new car if they do.”

As far as the endurance test goes, it was not one of those hardcore hypermiler attempts. It included realistic use of the car. “Li Auto says it conducted the test around Lake Tai (Taihu) in the Yangtze Delta, with temperatures between 13 and 20°C. The i6 carried four occupants, their luggage, and 50 kg of additional gear. The automaker stresses that the air conditioning was on during the 18-hour drive,” CarNewsChina adds. The drive occurred in city driving conditions with speed limits between 40 and 80 km/h. So, yes, the car went much further than it would have on the highway driving much faster. Also note that the car didn’t die when it got to 0%. They just stopped the test then, without pushing to see how far the battery buffer from 0% down to dead would take them.

#5956. Amex Cruise & Yacht Collection: Worth It and Who Competes

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On October 20th, American Express Travel announced that its longstanding cruise program is now the American Express Cruise & Yacht Collection, with the refreshed program going live on October 22nd, 2026. The old name was Cruise Privileges, and AMEX says the partners and cardmember benefits remain consistent with the previous program. This appears to be a new coat of paint on an existing perk, not a new perk, but from an acquaintance at American Express Travel, I am certain there’s a desire to make this into more than it is right now.

The collection spans 20 cruise lines and yacht brands, and the names AMEX leads with are hotel-at-sea brands: the Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection, Orient Express Sailing Yachts and Aman at Sea, with Virgin Voyages and Explora Journeys called out as newer partners. (I covered Ritz-Carlton’s challenges last year, so I will leave that story where it is, they have recovered some since then.) AMEX is clearly positioning this as a luxury product, not a mass market cruise discount.

Eligibility is limited to the Centurion Card, the Platinum Card and the Business Platinum Card. Cardholders book an eligible sailing through AMEX Travel and receive an onboard credit of $100 to $300 per stateroom – a special amenity that varies by line, trip planning with no booking fee, and 2x Membership Rewards points on the booking. AMEX Travel exclusive offers can also apply, and the collection benefits combine with the cruise line’s own public offers and any loyalty recognition a traveler holds with that line.

The rules narrow eligibility. Only some select sailings qualify, cruises must be five nights or longer (this is a larger limitation for some lines than others), and the primary cardholder must be a named passenger on the reservation. Cardholders can pay with Membership Rewards points, but AwardWallet notes that Membership Rewards redemptions for cruises have historically been poor value, and I agree. Pay cash, earn the 2x and save your points for premium cabin flights as is often the advice from Live And Let’s Fly and its Boarding Area peers.

For a Platinum cardholder booking a “contemporary” (mainline) or premium sailing who does not already work with a travel advisor, yes. A $200 or $300 credit for booking the same fare you would find elsewhere is free money, and having one point of contact at AMEX Travel beats calling a cruise line call center when something goes sideways. That said, many agencies have similar (sometimes better benefits) without adding to the cost of the sailing and most agencies don’t charge for their services.

It is a weaker play at the top of the market. On a luxury yacht suite that runs well into five figures per person, a $300 stateroom credit is a rounding error, and the 2x earn is modest. Chase Sapphire Reserve, by comparison, earns 8x on Chase Travel purchases. The difference is structural. AMEX Travel is the agency of record on the booking, so you cannot pair the AMEX amenity with the perks an independent advisor would have negotiated for the same cabin. You pick one or the other, and on expensive sailings the advisor package is often the bigger number. An example is below in the agency section.

Chase competes in the space. Voyages by Chase Travel attaches perks to designated sailings booked through Chase Travel, including up to $100 in onboard credit plus a specialty dining experience on Royal Caribbean, up to $200 in onboard credit plus free laundry on Virgin Voyages, and a $250 per person Destination Experience Credit on Explora Journeys. Credits are typically added to the reservation automatically. Chase does not market it with anywhere near the polish of the AMEX rebrand, but on Explora a per person credit can beat a per stateroom one, that said, Destination Experience credits must be applied toward an excursion offered by the brand vs a Journey Experience credit which can be used on anything (and is offered by an approved travel agency.)

Beyond those two, I could not find a comparable cruise program from the other premium issuers. That makes two obvious targets. Capital One already runs the Premier Collection for luxury hotels on the $395 Venture X, with a $100 experience credit on those stays, as CNBC notes. Bolting cruise lines onto that framework would be a natural next step. Citi is the second. The $595 Strata Elite built The Reserve by Citi Travel for 4.5 to 5 star hotels, per the same CNBC comparison, and Citi has the American Airlines relationship to pair air with sailings. Either bank could launch a credible rival with partners AMEX has already shown will sign.

The truth of card travel programs is that they are travel agencies with a bank logo. A luxury agency in a consortium like Virtuoso can often deliver more. Virtuoso advisors regularly secure onboard credits ranging from about $100 to more than $1,000, complimentary shore excursions, upgrades when available, and on Virtuoso Voyages sailings a private reception and an onboard host.

As you know, I own Scott & Thomas, a Virtuoso agency that sells many of the same lines in the AMEX collection, so weigh my view accordingly. My advice is simple. Before booking a five-figure sailing through any card portal, get a quote from a luxury advisor for the same cabin and compare the full package, not just the fare.

One thing that AMEX and Chase can’t do that only specific agencies can is offer unpublished fares unavailable to the public. We are one of those agencies and have access to unpublished rates from 16 different premium, luxury, and expedition cruise lines (over 1,200 at last check.) They could offer this in spots but don’t have the right market shape to sell targeted sailings in the way an agency can. The card programs are more of a generalist approach whereas a qualified agency offers something more.

The Cruise & Yacht Collection is a smart rebrand of a perk that was already decent, and for a Platinum cardholder booking without an advisor it is worth using every time. It is not the best deal available at the luxury end, and Chase has a credible alternative on some lines. The fact that Capital One and Citi have not answered yet feels temporary. Expect at least one of them to follow AMEX onto the water within the next year.

#5460. Trump’s DOE Budget Request Cuts Weak Bets And Useful Energy Research Together

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There are Department of Energy programs in the Trump administration’s FY2027 budget request that I would cut too. Hydrogen for buses, trucks, building heat and routine electricity storage has consumed years of public funding and attention without overcoming the basic efficiency and cost advantages of using electricity directly. Ending more attempts to force those applications into existence would be progress. The problem is that the administration has wrapped those defensible cuts into a much broader retreat from batteries, electric vehicles, buildings, solar, wind and public energy research while treating several much weaker technological bets far more gently.

Across thirteen selected DOE technology program lines, FY2026 enacted funding totals about $4.288 billion. Trump’s FY2027 request reduces that to $2.618 billion, a decline of roughly 39%. The distribution matters much more than the aggregate. Solar, wind, vehicles and buildings together fall from $1.005 billion to only $40 million, about a 96% reduction. These are selected annual DOE programs, not the entire department or every federal dollar that supports the technologies, and the FY2027 figures are a presidential request rather than enacted appropriations. But budget requests tell Congress what an administration thinks deserves public support, and the pattern is difficult to mistake.

The useful way to judge these programs is not to divide technologies into politically favored and politically disfavored camps. Research, demonstrations and deployment support should face different tests. Early-stage research should answer a credible question that is worth answering. A demonstration should resolve specific technical, operating or commercial uncertainties. Deployment funding should accelerate something with a plausible path to delivering useful services competitively. A technology can justify a bounded experiment while being a terrible candidate for a billion-dollar rollout, and commercial success in a sector does not mean there are no remaining public-interest research questions.

Solar and wind illustrate the latter point. Their DOE program lines go from $220 million and $100 million respectively to zero in the FY2027 request. Neither technology requires the federal government to prove that it can generate electricity at commercial scale. That argument was settled years ago. The remaining public questions concern durability, manufacturing, recycling, materials, grid integration and operating electricity systems with very large shares of variable generation. Firms should fund ordinary product competition, but shared knowledge and system-level research often produce benefits that no single company can capture.

Vehicles fall from $397 million to $20 million, while buildings fall from $288 million to $20 million. That is especially difficult to square with the actual transition. Battery-electric vehicles have already won substantial passenger-vehicle markets and are spreading rapidly into commercial-duty niches where economics align. Heat pumps are a mature technology whose remaining problems increasingly concern installation quality, controls, building integration, flexible demand and how to electrify existing buildings without making unnecessary renovation a prerequisite. Those are exactly the kinds of practical problems that public research can help solve across an industry.

Hydrogen is a different case. The annual hydrogen and fuel-cell line falls from $160 million to zero, and much of its energy mission deserves to disappear. Producing hydrogen, conditioning it, moving it and converting it back into useful work generally adds energy losses, equipment and cost compared with direct electrification. Necessary industrial hydrogen is different because ammonia, methanol and other chemical processes require the molecule itself. A sensible federal portfolio would focus its hydrogen expertise on reducing emissions from hydrogen that industry actually needs while ending serial attempts to create a giant new hydrogen market for applications where electricity already has the advantage.

That does not mean the much larger regional hydrogen hubs have simply been cancelled. The hubs are multiyear awards funded separately from the annual technology-program comparison, and five remained nominally retained after the administration’s review. Retention, however, is not the same thing as a functioning project. Reporting from Pennsylvania continues to describe uncertainty, cautious partners and little evidence of meaningful momentum, while the FY2027 request proposes clawing back unobligated clean-hydrogen funding. The result is less a coherent technology decision than a prolonged “will they or won’t they?” process that makes planning harder for good projects and bad projects alike.

The mixed treatment of other technologies reinforces the point. Critical-minerals funding rises sharply, from $83 million to $364 million, and some of that can be useful if it builds processing, refining and qualified industrial output rather than merely subsidizing extraction. Geothermal remains at $150 million, including substantial support for enhanced-geothermal work whose eventual electricity economics remain uncertain. Civilian nuclear falls only about 9%, from $1.685 billion to $1.534 billion. Existing-fleet work and bounded research can have value without accepting the proposition that new nuclear or SMRs are likely to become the fast, inexpensive backbone of the transition.

The cement awards show why program management matters as much as headline appropriations. Brimstone’s large demonstration award was reinstated while Sublime’s remained unresolved in the October award snapshot. Neither administrative status tells us whether the underlying technology will succeed. Brimstone still has to prove the economics of a rock refinery whose business case depends on several saleable outputs, while Sublime has its own feedstock, energy, qualification and cost questions to answer. A demonstration should purchase evidence about those uncertainties. Reinstatement is not commercial validation, and cancellation is not scientific falsification.

ARPA-E makes the institutional problem clearest. Its total allocation falls from $350 million to $200.3 million, while project funding drops from $310 million to $160.3 million. The purpose of an organization like ARPA-E is not to select a portfolio in which every experiment becomes a company. Some experiments should fail. Some should reveal that an attractive concept does not scale. Some should create knowledge that private firms subsequently exploit. The public value comes from asking useful questions at stages where private capital cannot capture enough of the upside to justify carrying all of the uncertainty.

That requires competent people inside government who can distinguish research from demonstration, negotiate milestones, inspect projects, interpret evidence and stop work that is no longer justified. Hollowing out that capability while cancelling and reinstating awards through broad political sweeps weakens both sides of the process. It becomes harder to kill bad projects intelligently and harder to protect good experiments from indiscriminate cuts.

I would keep the cuts to weak hydrogen-for-energy applications, narrow several other portfolios whose commercial claims have outrun their evidence, and impose much harder tests on new nuclear and speculative geothermal electricity ambitions. I would restore serious capacity around electrification, buildings, industrial conversion, batteries, renewable generation and useful shared research, while supporting mineral processing where it removes real industrial bottlenecks. That is not a defense of every clean-energy grant. It is an argument for judging technologies by the useful work they can perform and judging public programs by the evidence they buy.

A stopped clock can cancel something that deserved to go. It still cannot decide what the United States should build next.

#5461. Who Killed The Elon Musk Movie? Nope, Not That Billionaire, Must Be Another One

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tesla CEO and well known billionaire Elon Musk has made quite a journey since 2004, when he joined Martin Eberhardt and Marc Tarpenning as the lead investor in their EV startup Tesla Motors. All of it, and more, is captured in the new documentary film, “Musk.” It was set to open on 472 screens across the country on Friday, October 16, but if you blinked, you missed it. The film’s distributor, Bleecker Street, has already pulled it out of action. Well, timing is everything…

Leading up to last week’s blackout, the “Musk” documentary was riding high on a cushion of supportive publicity, with acclaimed documentary filmmaker Alex Gibney at the helm and Elon Musk providing a fascinating, high profile subject in the great tradition of the US auto industry.

Among other works, Gibney was a consulting producer on the hard-hitting 2006 documentary directed by Chris Paine, “Who Killed the Electric Car?” The film excoriated General Motors for killing off its 1990’s-era EV1 electric sedan after just a few years at low-volume production. The car was leased, not sold, and only a few escaped the junkyard crusher when GM halted production and recalled the vehicles.

With that in his background, Gibney might be expected to create a somewhat flattering portrait of the little known young investor who made the Tesla Motors dream come true. In fact, Paine eneded “Who Killed the Electric Car” with a shot of a Tesla prototype. Paine also went on to direct the 2011 sequel “Revenge of the Electric Car” featuring Tesla along with GM and other EV-curious automakers.

Musk himself gave the sequel a glowing review on social media back in 2019. “Great electric car documentary about early days of Tesla, GM Volt, Nissan Leaf & Gadget. So much has happened since then,” Musk inscribed on Twitter, the social media site he acquired in 2022.

Yes, so much has happened since then. Gibney is well known for his deep dives into American entrepreneurs run amok and other topics dealing with dark side of success and power in the hands of ethically bankrupt people, almost all of them men (Gibney’s 2019 HBO documentary on the now-imprisoned Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes is one notable exception).

In “Musk,” Gibney takes another crack at the power nexus so deftly wielded by Elon Musk, all the way on up to the entrepreneur’s post as chief chainsaw-wielder at US President Donald Trump’s “DOGE” budget-cutting office.

“Musk” also fills the gaps in Musk’s home life, including interviews with the mothers of his 14 or so children, which presumably makes for juicy viewing. That helps explain why Bleecker Street was aiming for a wide release, a rather unusual plan for a long-format documentary totaling almost four hours. So unusual, in fact, that Bleecker Street pulled the plug after just one week.

As of this writing, Bleecker Street continues to list hundreds of screens on its website. However, according to film industry press, that page is out of date. The “Musk” trailer  is also available on the Bleecker Street website, but if you want to see the whole film — all 3 hours and 52 minutes — take a trip to New York City and catch it at the Angelika Film Center & Cafe on Houston Street.

That’s it. Only one location reportedly remains from the 472 planned screens. If you know of any others, drop a note in the comment thread.

Coincidentally or not, on October 15 the Hollywood Reporter was among those reporting that Bleecker Street suddenly has a new owner. “Indie Distributor Bleecker Street Sold to Firm Backed By Billionaire Pierre Omidyar,” reads the headline, referring to eBay founder Pierre Omidyar.

“The capital from the acquisition will allow Bleecker to continue to operate its film business and build out its event label, Crosswalk,” HR continued, taking note of Bleecker Street’s already prominent position in the indie/specialty film markets.

“Currently, the company is in theaters with Alex Gibney‘s Elon Musk doc, Musk, which will be expanding its theatrical release this weekend,” HR added somewhat optimistically, considering the state of affairs just a few days later.

However, HR reporter Mia Galuppo did take note of the writing on the wall. “Ironically, Bleecker’s new owner Outerlight backs streamer Passionflix, which is run by Elon Musk’s sister Tosca,” Galuppo reported.

Make of that what you will. For the record, Passionflix is a niche streamer specializing in romance, as the name suggests. “Passionflix set out to create a dedicated streaming service for romance audiences, combining a curated content library with original productions,” explains the streaming platform Leyra.

Evidently the film bug runs deep in the Musk family. In yet another coincidence-or-not, last week Semafor scooped word that Musk has invested in $1 billion in Skydance, the newly formed conglomerate birthed from the acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery by Paramount. Among a long list of cable TV and sports/entertainment ventures, Skydance is now the proud owner of the news organizations CNN and CBS News along with Warner Bros. and Paramount Pictures, HBO, and Paramount+.

The other billionaire behind Skydance is Oracle founder Larry Ellison, known for his close ties to Elon Musk and right wing politics. Semafor cites the billionaire-level cable executive and investor John Malone, who surmises that Ellison leveraged his considerable stake in Tesla stocks to amass the big bucks for the Skydance venture. In the same article, Malone also indicates that Ellison financed the deal that put Twitter into Musk’s hands.

But wait, there’s more. Semafor additionally notes that industry observers are already anticipating a “more expansive partnership between Musk’s lower-earth-orbit satellite network, Starlink, and Skydance.”

To be fair, the web of Elon Musk’s family and friend circle extends beyond the Bleecker Street sale and the Skydance merger, and those connections are just one factor with a possible connection to the abrupt cancellation of an uflattering documentary about one of the most politically powerful private citizens to haunt the halls of the federal government. Timing-wise, the “Musk” film was set for a tough challenge from another new, long-format documentary dealing with hucksterism and deception, the new Elizabeth Holmes documentary by Nathan Fielder. Fielder’s film has the added attraction of full frontal nudity, earning a coveted “R” rating to attract adult audiences.

The timing for Gibney’s project could be more fortuitous after Election Day, whereupon the Republican Party is expected to lose its exclusive control over Congress. If the Democrats do gain a majority in the House of Representatives (possibly the Senate, too), they will take over the oversight proceedings that Republican members of Congress have let languish. Musk has already emerged as a principal target due to his role at DOGE as well as in his private sector ventures, giving the “Musk” movie publicity mill a chance to gin up again.

More recently, the call for investigations also includes the widely excoriated manipulation of SpaceX’s Starlink and Starshield satellite services, which crippled Ukraine’s ability to defend itself from Russian attacks last week. That’s fresh material not included in Gibney’s documentary. Perhaps a sequel is already in order…
