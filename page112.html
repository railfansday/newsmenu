The iPhone Duo is now in Apple stores, even if most buyers won’t get theirs until January. Here’s how to make the most of a hands-on demo.

The iPhone Duo went on sale Oct. 23, and anyone who didn’t preorder the device on Oct. 16 is now looking at a delivery date in late January. Apple stores have demo units on display, though, so you can spend time with Apple’s first foldable long before one could arrive at your door. A few minutes is enough to check what a spec sheet can’t tell you: how the hinge feels, how visible the crease is, how the phone sits in your hand folded and unfolded and how apps behave when you open it up. Here are six things worth testing on your next visit to an Apple store.

Here’s what should you be looking to do when you actually pick up the iPhone Duo. Because while the foldable phone might have a fun, unique design, you may run into things you’re unfamiliar with — or even dislike — when using it day to day. You’ll want to be confident that the device is a good fit for your needs before plunking down $2,000 or more for the folding phone.

We’re going to break down a series of things you should check out when you finally get to play with it at the Apple Store.

When the Duo was first revealed, Apple showed off a very intriguing animation when you unfold the it. Leave it to Apple to make a function so simple look so aesthetically pleasing.

It’ll likely be the first thing you notice when you first open the Duo up to see its inner display, and there’s a good chance you’ll continue to open and close the device to see the effect. At the end of the day, it’s a parlor trick — but a very good one, adding delight to ordinary use.

Foldables, well, fold, and they typically come with creases. The Duo has a minimal crease and a matte display, both of which are wins. That said, even the most noticeable creases tend to “disappear” when you’re viewing content — you just get used to them while watching TV shows or YouTube videos. The Duo’s minimal crease should be even easier to ignore.

And let’s not forget about the matte screen. Several foldables ship with glossy displays, but the iPhone Duo will have a less shiny screen, making it easier to view in bright environments without annoying reflections.

The outer passport-size display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for watching videos because of how it crops content to fit the new aspect ratio. Still, it’s handy to prop the phone up in tent mode and watch shows that way. You also have a large inner display to work with, but because of its passport-style design, even that screen has a different aspect ratio than the widescreen format people are used to on regular phones, so there will likely be some letterboxing in videos.

Try watching something with the phone set up in both ways to see if it bothers you enough to dissuade you from buying the phone.

Again, it’s not an iPhone Duo issue but a foldable phone issue — the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8 is the same size and format. The aspect ratio of the display might crop the top or sides of videos, so it’s worth trying out if you expect to watch a lot of full-screen videos.

Like videos, apps on foldables can sometimes appear a little weird, and that could be the case with the iPhone Duo‘s aspect ratio. When you’re at the Apple Store, check out how your most-used apps look and feel on both the inner and outer displays, and make sure you rotate the inner screen to see how apps behave in this orientation, too — that wide screen could be great for watching vertical videos or scrolling through social media.

If some apps, such as Instagram, look a little different or are wonky, it’ll hopefully be just a matter of time until the developers make an update that optimizes the experience for this new design. Keep in mind that you can also have two apps open onscreen at once, making it easier to multitask.

If you want the best camera experience on an iPhone right now, you should be looking at the iPhone 18 Pro or Pro Max, but that doesn’t make the Duo a slouch for shooting photos, either.

Yes, the Duo is missing a third camera (telephoto), and if that’s important to you, then you probably shouldn’t even be considering Apple’s foldable. But still, if you’re going to shell out this much money for a new phone, test out those cameras — even if it’s in a controlled environment like an Apple Store.

Another thing to check out before you pull the trigger is your selfie shots with this new phone. There are two main ways to do this: with the phone closed, holding it up in the regular way you’d take a selfie using the outer screen’s 12-megapixel front-facing camera, or with the inner display open and shooting with the twin 48-megapixel rear cameras (the outer screen will show a preview of the shot).

There’s also a front-facing camera underneath the inner display, which is mostly for video calls — and which we suspect would produce the least-impressive shots from the device. Try them all and see what you think of the pics. And since you’re at an Apple Store, make sure you delete them afterward.

The odd but interesting aspect ratio of the front display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for typing, so it’s best to do a typing test when you have one in front of you. While the inner display will provide a roomier and more comfortable typing experience, you probably won’t want to have to open up the Duo every time you want to shoot off a quick text to someone. If you have bigger hands, typing on the front screen could be a bit of a nightmare.

Typing one-handed on the small cover screen should be manageable, but try it out to see if it works for you. Some may find it convenient; others may find it cumbersome.

And when you’re typing on the inner display, try the Duo in both portrait and landscape orientations to see which feels better. You can also choose between a split keyboard and larger keys. It’s thoughtful of Apple to offer options that let you tailor the typing experience to your preferences, but you may find that none of them suit your style.

#3220. Anthropic's IPO is risky business

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic's much-anticipated initial public offering is giving potential investors some perspective on the risky business of artificial intelligence.

More than 90 pages of Anthropic's IPO prospectus, revealed Monday, center on the AI lab's multitude of corporate risk factors, many of which go beyond the kinds of boilerplate scenarios typically found in corporate filings.

From the end of humanity to the company's current clash with the Trump administration, Anthropic's IPO document underscores how one of the most well-capitalized startups in history foresees the future of its operations.

The Claude maker's risks include a laundry list of ways its models can cause harm, including potentially "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity."

It's imperative for Anthropic to emphasize AI safety because failure to do so could adversely affect its "brand, business, financial condition, and results of operations," the filing said. But fierce competition, market pressure and stakeholder expectations make the task of safely developing and implementing bleeding-edge foundation models particularly challenging.

Anthropic can't guarantee that its AI safety efforts will be adequate, as the technology's inherent complexity makes it difficult for the company to plan for and predict every potential scenario.

The public's souring position on data centers poses a major risk for Anthropic, which relies on an ever-expanding portfolio of state-of-the-art computing facilities to train and maintain its prized foundation models.

And with the midterm elections approaching, the tech industry's massive data center build-out has turned political, with lawmakers in 16 states considering moratoriums, according to the National Conference of State Legislatures.

"Opposition to data center development  has also become more organized and coordinated across jurisdictions and our public profile may draw particular attention to projects associated with us," the filing said.

Anthropic's future success depends not only on building giant data centers, but also on the electrical and related power infrastructure needed to juice those computing facilities.

The lack of "sufficient electrical power" across the nation and world poses big risks to Anthropic and its rivals, the filing notes. Even if energy becomes more readily available through "on-site natural gas-fired generation," that power could become more expensive with so much demand, the filing said.

Rivals like OpenAI and Meta have come under fire from lawmakers and plaintiffs' attorneys over allegations that the design and implementation of their AI models and apps have fostered certain child safety-related harms. Meta recently agreed to pay roughly $17 billion in a settlement with multiple state attorneys general in litigation involving child mental health and technology addiction.

Thus far, Anthropic has largely avoided scrutiny, but the filing noted that the company could potentially face its own legal headaches. Based on its terms of service, minors aren't allowed to access Claude and related products.

Anthropic knows it has work to do protecting children "as adoption of our models grows, including by minors and other vulnerable users, notwithstanding our efforts to restrict such access."

The company calling to pace the frontier also says it's a risk to move too slowly.

Anthropic needs to keep racing and needs the rest of the business world to keep up. If companies curb their AI spending, Anthropic could feel the burn.

"If the market for our models, platform, and applications develops more slowly than we expect or declines, our growth may slow or stall, and our business, financial condition, and results of operations could be adversely affected," the filing says.

The prospectus also cites reduced demand for frontier models as a risk. Customers might find that lower-cost models work fine for them and that "incremental performance gains at the frontier do not justify the additional cost," instead choosing cheaper, or open-source alternatives.

Ortberg had told investors last month that the production stabilize was taking a "little bit longer" than expected becuase of the wings production pace.

#228. A 'canary in the coal mine': Measles resurgence exposes weaknesses in U.S. public health, experts say

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For nearly two years, the U.S. has had to swallow an uncomfortable reality: A highly infectious disease it once eliminated is spreading again.

More than 4,400 measles cases and at least three deaths have been reported nationwide this year, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. The spike followed nearly 2,300 cases in 2025. But many public health experts argue that the return of measles isn't the only concerning development — it's also what the resurgence signals about America that's causing alarm.

Measles is often described as one of the most contagious viruses, and it is uniquely unforgiving of weaknesses in public health defenses. A safe and highly effective vaccine has existed for decades.

But the virus's spread exposes declining vaccination rates, eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities, experts said.

Together, those vulnerabilities have made measles a revealing test of the country's public health defenses. For some experts, the resurgence of a disease the U.S. eliminated more than two decades ago is a warning sign of its ability to contain future threats, from other vaccine-preventable diseases to emerging pathogens.

"Measles is the canary in the coal mine for so many different reasons," said Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiology professor and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health. "It's the canary in the coal mine for other vaccine-preventable illnesses, where we could see a return of things that used to harm kids, but also for very acute, no-notice dangerous infectious disease emergencies."

The U.S. is on track to lose its official measles elimination status during an upcoming review by the Pan American Health Organization, which will meet in Washington, D.C., in mid-November.

"It says a lot about a country if it is unable to control measles," said Nuzzo, who is one of 10 experts CNBC spoke with about the ongoing outbreak and its implications.

The U.S. has the tools to prevent measles from spreading. But pockets of low vaccination coverage are leaving the country vulnerable.

The measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

That translates to 280,000 kindergarteners who did not complete their MMR vaccination series, according to the CDC. Childhood vaccination exemptions in the U.S. also reached a record high 4.2% for incoming kindergartners during the 2025-2026 school year, up from 3.6% the previous year, the agency says.

"The bottom line is that not enough people are vaccinating their kids," said Brown's Nuzzo.

While some cases stem from international travel, much of the recent spread has been driven by local transmission in "too many" communities where vaccination coverage falls below 95%, she added.

Among states across the U.S. collecting MMR vaccine data, 67% of counties and jurisdictions now have immunization rates below 95%, according to a September investigation by NBC News.

Recent U.S. outbreaks have often occurred in "closely knit communities" where people have frequent contact and low vaccination coverage, said Dr. William Moss, an epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

But declining vaccination rates do not mean most Americans have turned against immunization. Polling suggests broad support for childhood vaccines. A July KFF survey found that many Americans sit in a more uncertain middle ground about the shots, leaving room for conflicting information to influence their decisions.

"This is not an edge-case issue," Nuzzo said, adding that the vast majority of parents still vaccinate their children.

Even if outbreaks are concentrated in a minority of communities, it can create a "dangerous situation" far from those areas, she said. Infants too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot receive immunizations for medical reasons can be particularly vulnerable when the virus spreads into communities with high vaccination rates.

Those risks come as the Trump administration reshapes federal vaccine policy.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in August suggesting that the combined MMR vaccine be split into separate shots, while repeating false claims about its safety. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has also overhauled a key federal advisory committee responsible for recommending childhood immunizations, though the changes are frozen amid ongoing litigation.

Several experts told CNBC that such moves risk further eroding confidence in vaccines and weakening efforts to protect children.

Public health infrastructure was also "really decimated by Covid," and the CDC in particular lost many experienced scientists and physicians "who had spent their lives working on vaccine-preventable diseases," said Scott Weaver, a virologist who is the research director of Galveston National Laboratory.

He added that the CDC, as of September, is also no longer immediately incorporating state-reported "measles-associated" deaths into its national published tally, making it harder to track outbreaks in real time. The agency lists three measles-related deaths in 2026, but Pennsylvania has reported at least five deaths.

"We have folks in federal health leadership right now that are throwing gasoline on the fire, and that is not helping the situation," said Jess Steier, a public health scientist and founder and CEO of Unbiased Science, an organization that aims to make complex scientific concepts understandable.

If the U.S. fails to materially improve vaccination rates, the measles resurgence could extend beyond ongoing outbreaks.

At "current" state-level vaccination rates, measles could become endemic again in the U.S., resulting in an estimated 851,300 cases over 25 years, according to a large-scale epidemiological model published in JAMA in April 2025 by researchers at Stanford and other institutions. Under that scenario, the model predicts the measles cases could lead to 170,200 hospitalizations and 2,550 deaths over that period.

The model, which simulated infectious disease spread in the U.S. at various childhood vaccination levels, relies on immunization data from 2004 to 2023 for that baseline scenario. The model assumes that diseases are introduced by someone who travels abroad and brings them back, most commonly a U.S. citizen who isn't vaccinated.

While many people are currently immune through vaccination, outbreaks can increase in size and frequency if immunization rates decline over a longer period.

Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist and the director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota, said he views the measles situation in Canada as a direct warning for the U.S.

Canada lost its official measles elimination status in November after a multi-province outbreak that generated more than 5,400 cases across 10 jurisdictions in 2025. Canada has a population of more than 40 million people.

"We have a population of 350 million. If you extrapolate, if we had the very same experience that Canada had over the next year or two, that would be up to 50,000 cases of measles in the U.S.," Osterholm said. "I think that's a potential future we have to be mindful of."

An even larger measles outbreak isn't the only potential problem.

Vaccination coverage for other childhood diseases has similarly declined. The share of U.S. kindergartners vaccinated against polio fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, while coverage for diphtheria, tetanus and acellular pertussis, or DTaP, dropped to 92%, according to CDC data.

The U.S. has already seen a sharp rise in pertussis, or whooping cough. U.S. reported cases reached roughly 43,300 in 2024 and nearly 28,000 in 2025, up substantially from pandemic-era levels, according to the CDC. Brown's Nuzzo said the resurgence reflects, in part, fewer people getting vaccinated and the fact that protection from the pertussis vaccine wanes over time.

The simulation model published in JAMA also examined how falling childhood immunization rates could affect the future spread of several diseases previously eliminated from the U.S. through vaccines. Under current vaccination levels, the researchers found that diseases including rubella and polio were unlikely to become endemic in the U.S.

But in a hypothetical scenario in which childhood vaccination rates fell by 50%, the model projected 9.9 million cases of rubella, 4.3 million cases of polio and 197 cases of diphtheria over 25 years.

Rubella can cause devastating complications when an infection during pregnancy passes to a developing fetus, while polio can cause paralysis and death, said Dr. Nathan Lo, assistant professor of infectious diseases at Stanford Medicine.

The projections are not predictions that these outbreaks will occur, but they illustrate how much risks could grow if vaccination coverage deteriorates.

"What vaccines often buy you is not just the protection, but also less uncertainty," said Mathew Kiang, an assistant professor of epidemiology and population health at Stanford Medicine who also worked on the simulation model. "I think even more frightening is just the really wide uncertainty that we just wouldn't be sure of what could happen."

The measles resurgence is also raising questions about how effective the U.S. can be at detecting and responding to emerging public health threats before they spread.

"Public health is all about prevention," said Dr. Manisha Juthani, president of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials and commissioner of the Connecticut Department of Public Health. She said the country must maintain its ability to detect threats, invest in surveillance, and recognize that infectious diseases do not respect national borders.

Those capabilities are crucial for curbing emerging pathogens, especially when scientists initially know little about how a new disease spreads, how severe it is or how to treat it. A pathogen could spread before vaccines or treatments are available, making early detection, clear public communication and coordination especially important.

Other experts argue that weaknesses in those capabilities could also have implications for the country's defenses against deliberate biological attacks.

In an April 2025 op-ed, Steph Batalis, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, argued that the measles outbreak exposed a broader vulnerability in the U.S.' biodefense strategy and, specifically, a weakened ability to deter potential biological attacks.

She argued that the country's struggle to contain a known, vaccine-preventable disease "might only embolden those who see our public health vulnerabilities as an opportunity."

But Osterholm, who works extensively on bioterrorism, said low MMR immunization rates and rising measles cases do not necessarily indicate that Americans would refuse a vaccine or antibiotic during a biological emergency. The shots and drugs used to counter biological threats can differ from those routinely administered to the public, he said, and the prospect of a severe outbreak could prompt urgency among the public to seek protection.

That point highlights an important limitation: The measles resurgence by itself does not establish whether the U.S. could withstand entirely different or novel public health threats.

But as a disease the U.S. knows how to prevent spreads, experts say the stakes of addressing those vulnerabilities extend to whatever might come next.

#175. AI Agents Are Media’s New Favorite Audience

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every morning, Michelle Seo receives a briefing from Chattiana, her Muse artificial intelligence agent.

Chattiana’s briefing lists Seo’s agenda and to-do list, and plucks water-cooler topics from the headlines in her various newsletters along with links to the sources.

“I’m kind of challenging myself as an introvert to start more conversations,” Seo, a San Diego-based public relations consultant, tells WIRED. The newsletter, she says, gives her a sense of the daily news and options for filling an awkward silence on a Zoom call.

Seo is far from the first person to consume her news with an AI assist. In fact, the trend has become so common that some news outlets are starting to produce content that treats AI agents—not human readers—as the intended audience. This can look like anything from feeds optimized for AI to pieces structured to allow agents to create personalized articles.

“We believe that the media ecosystem is changing,” says Axios chief revenue officer Jacquelyn Cameron. “We see this with open web deteriorating. We see that search is shrinking. We see that hyperpersonalization is surging, that LLMs are becoming more important in the way that people are consuming” their news.

Major publishers have responded to that change in a variety of ways. Many, including WIRED’s parent company Condé Nast, have signed deals to allow AI platforms to surface their content. Outlets are grappling with “generative engine optimization,” or GEO, strategies to ensure articles appear in responses to AI prompts. Last year, AIOSEO, a SEO plug-in for WordPress sites, launched a feature that provides websites with LLM.txt files, which help AI discover and interpret sites’ content.

Others have taken a more defensive posture: The New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft in 2023 for copyright infringement, alleging that the companies illegally trained models on reporting. That case is still ongoing.

What Axios and a handful of others are trying, though, represents a more foundational shift in how the media operates—and who its stories are for.

“We have always said,” Cameron says, “that we want to be where the audience is.”

At the Digiday Publishing Summit in September, Axios announced the upcoming launch of Axios Direct: feeds designed for AI agents.

“We do believe that a significant portion of news consumption will move into AI systems, LLMs, and devices,” Cameron says. She describes the feeds as a more efficient way for agents to access Axios content, cutting back on narrative to prioritize structured data—more similar to a Bloomberg terminal than a series of articles.

Every’s founder Dan Shipper says the most common way Every’s content differs from a typical article is that its writers include prompts in pieces whenever possible, allowing readers to test concepts themselves. An article about a new model, for example, includes a series of prompts the reader can copy-paste into AI, demonstrating how the model answers questions and evaluates input.

Certain types of articles—such as guides—also include buttons that readers can press to “Read With Claude” or “Read With ChatGPT.” Clicking pulls up a prompt, instructing AI to “Start with a tight summary: one paragraph, bulleted. Then offer to go deeper.” Writers pack stories with details without worrying about losing readers’ attention, knowing agents will pick out the most relevant bits.

Two years ago, the outlet launched its first “extendable” article: a long-form feature accompanied by a database of reporting, from interview recordings to news articles to YouTube videos. Readers could query a chatbot, which would fetch additional details from the database.

“As a writer, you have to make choices about what goes into the story you’re telling,” Shipper says. “And there’s always a question about, are those the right choices? Or, are those the right choices for a particular reader?” With AI, journalists no longer choose.

“People are gonna do crazy, crazy stuff with this,” Shipper adds.

As outlets eye AI agents as a new audience, another question looms: Can publishers actually make money off of this?

Rasmus Kleis Nielsen, a professor of communication at the University of Copenhagen, says that while the market of publishing for agents is “emerging,” it remains immature and “extremely uneven.” Major questions remain, he says, about price points and how AI agents will interact with content in the future.

Cameron and Allison Murphy, Axios’ chief operating officer, are optimistic about AI as an audience proxy. According to the executives, Axios’s short-form, highly structured style has already made the publisher a favorite for AI to pull from, when responding to prompts. “Time is money for crawlers,” Murphy says. “They don’t want to have to go three quarters down the page to find the main point. So, that’s something that’s already benefiting us.”

But does AI crawling its content actually benefit Axios’ bottom line? Even if readers don’t actually visit the Axios homepage, the brand visibility in and of itself can be positive, Nielsen says. It’s not unlike publishers making content for social platforms like TikTok and Instagram that rarely bring viewers back to their websites.

Publications in general are grappling with how difficult it has become to monetize certain types of content when a chatbot can offer up answers on a silver platter. People only want to pay for information that other publications—and LLMs—don’t have. At Every, that means in-the-weeds guidance on AI and how to use it; at Axios Pro Deals, it’s speedy access to information that could move the market. An all-access Axios Pro Deals subscription costs $2,499 a year, while an annual Every subscription costs $288.

Shipper sees that $288 as a subscription for a human reader, along with their agents. “I may not want to read all of your stuff every month,” Shipper says. But if his subscription can include his agent, “I may want my agent to know it so that it can tell me, ‘Hey, this thing is important.’”

Murphy says that Axios’ starting point is one of acceptance: People are already using AI to absorb information, and that more people will be doing so in the near future.

“I also try to make it a little less existentially terrifying by thinking, this isn’t the first time this has happened in media,” Murphy says. People get their news via social media, via short-form video, via podcasts. “It isn’t really AI as the audience,” Murphy adds. “It’s AI as a tool that helps us reach our audience.”

TL;DR: Microsoft Research says its Magnetar system makes proofs of ordinary program execution faster while relying on standard hash-based cryptography.

Zero-knowledge systems promise that a computation can be checked without trusting the machine that performed it. One reason they remain difficult to deploy is the cost of generating those proofs. Microsoft Research’s October 15 Magnetar announcement tackles that bottleneck using a virtual machine designed to prove ordinary programmes.

The researchers say Magnetar supports code compiled from Rust, Go and C and performs between 10 and 39 times faster on average than certain comparable proving systems across their benchmarks. Verification takes roughly 12 milliseconds, according to Microsoft’s tests. The system is built around standard hash functions, a design intended to reduce reliance on cryptography vulnerable to future quantum attacks.

For blockchains, lower proving costs could make verifiable offchain computation and complex application logic easier to use. But benchmark figures are not a guarantee of equivalent improvements inside production rollups or every smart-contract workload. Integration, hardware conditions and proof sizes all matter.

There is also a broader potential use case: institutions and AI agents could produce verifiable evidence that software followed specified instructions without exposing underlying private data. The research suggests a future in which cryptographic proofs are attached to more ordinary computer operations, although that future will depend on implementation outside the laboratory.

Different workloads demand distinct trade-offs between speed, cost, and performance. Deploying and operating AI models to meet business requirements often also requires complex adjustments. Simplismart says its software automates much of that work, tailoring how models run on existing computing infrastructure instead of requiring customers to manage those configurations themselves.

“Nobody actually needs the fastest or the cheapest inference,” Amritanshu Jain (pictured above, left), co-founder and CEO of Simplismart, said in an interview. He added that companies instead need to run AI models at speeds and costs that make sense for their specific applications.

Simplismart’s software sits between AI models and the computing infrastructure they run on and adjusts how models are deployed and managed to meet customers’ requirements. It says it can optimize factors such as how quickly a model responds, how many requests it handles, and how much computing power it consumes, helping companies get more out of their existing GPU stack.

The Bengaluru-based startup offers companies a way to deploy and run AI models through its own platform or within their existing cloud and on-premises infrastructure. It also provides tools to fine-tune models, automatically scale computing resources as demand changes, and monitor performance. Additionally, Simplismart allows cloud providers to build and operate their own AI inference services on GPU infrastructure using its software.

The collaboration involves Simplismart’s engineers and Nvidia’s solutions architects, while Nvidia has also helped introduce the startup to potential customers and partners, Jain stated.

Don't let your microwave become a hotbed for microplastics.

Standing near your microwave almost certainly won’t give you cancer, but that doesn’t mean the popular kitchen appliance isn’t linked to health concerns. Study after study has shown that the resulting chemical exposure from microplastics could pose significant health risks, and they have been linked to increases in medical issues like infertility and even cancer. Cheap plastic containers can leach microplastics into food, and that risk increases when you heat them in the microwave.

Here’s everything you need to know about microwaving plastic.

Most black plastic containers are not microwave-safe.

“If you’re using takeout containers to reheat food, they need to be labeled as heat-safe,” advises Ricardo Jarquin, executive chef of Loews Coral Gables in Miami. “Most restaurants do not use them because they’re more expensive.”

Aluminum, cardboard and paper-based containers are typically plastic-free and safe for storing and reheating food. Ziploc and Rubbermaid both faced a 2025 class-action lawsuit for advertising their plastic containers as “microwave-safe” and “freezer-safe.” When heated, plastic containers can leach chemicals into food. Deli containers may be considered food-safe, but glass and ceramic are safer options for reheating food in the microwave.

#357. Common drugs may help dangerous viruses spread through the body

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Medications are designed to treat diseases and relieve symptoms, but some can also produce unintended effects that put patients at risk. While side effects are common with many treatments, researchers have identified a particularly concerning possibility involving drugs used to control inflammation.

Scientists at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) have discovered that a widely used class of anti-inflammatory medications may make it easier for certain viruses to infect cells and spread throughout the body.

"We have found a surprising and potentially serious side effect of a class of anti-inflammatory drugs. They can help viruses spread more easily in the body," says Denis Kainov, a professor at the Department of Clinical and Molecular Medicine at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU).

Common Anti-Inflammatory Drugs May Help Viruses Spread

The medications at the center of the findings belong to a class called Janus kinase inhibitors, or JAKs. These drugs are widely prescribed to control inflammation, but their effects on the immune system may also leave the body more vulnerable to viral infections.

"Among the viruses that more easily affect the body with the help of these drugs are Rift Valley fever virus, influenza A, adenovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19," Kainov says.

These viruses can cause significant illness, making any treatment that potentially increases their ability to spread a serious concern.

Why Arthritis Drugs Can Weaken Antiviral Defenses

In a new study published in NAR Molecular Medicine, Kainov and his colleagues investigated how JAK inhibitors influence the body's ability to defend itself against viruses.

"JAK inhibitors are often prescribed for autoimmune diseases and inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis," says Erlend Ravlo, a PhD research fellow at NTNU and first author of the article.

Although these medications can be highly effective at reducing inflammation, their benefits come with an important trade-off. By suppressing certain immune responses, they can also interfere with the mechanisms that normally protect the body from viral infections.

"In particular, the drugs suppress an important immune signalling pathway that helps protect healthy cells from viral attacks," Ravlo said.

This pathway plays a central role in coordinating the body's response when viruses attempt to infect healthy tissue.

Scientists Discover How JAK Inhibitors Disrupt Immune Protection

The research team found that JAK inhibitors, including the medication baricitinib, can reduce the activity of genes involved in fighting viral infections.

"These genes play a crucial role in the body's response to viral infections," said Aleksandr Ianevski, an NTNU researcher and coauthor of the new paper.

When JAK inhibitors interfere with this immune signaling system, they weaken an important layer of antiviral protection. Without the usual defensive response, viruses may find it easier to establish an infection and spread between cells.

To investigate these effects, the scientists combined advanced virology methods with gene expression analysis and organoid technology. Their experiments included cells from the lungs, eyes and brain, along with miniature organ models grown in the laboratory.

These approaches allowed the team to examine how the drugs affect antiviral defenses across different types of human tissue.

"Although JAK inhibitors are effective in the treatment of inflammation, this shows that they can pose a hidden risk for patients who have latent or active viral infections," says Ravlo.

What the Findings Mean for Patients and Future Treatments

The results highlight the importance of carefully considering the risks associated with JAK inhibitors, particularly when patients may already have a viral infection or when outbreaks are occurring.

Researchers suggest that healthcare professionals should exercise caution when prescribing these medications during periods of increased viral transmission.

However, the same immune-suppressing properties that create potential risks for patients could also offer advantages in certain research settings. Under controlled laboratory conditions, these effects may help scientists develop vaccines or evaluate potential antiviral medications.

The researchers hope their findings will encourage greater awareness of how JAK inhibitors influence the immune system and support further investigations into their safest and most effective uses.

"We recommend further studies to better understand how the drugs can be used optimally, especially during virus outbreaks or pandemics," says Ianevski.
