At the plate, particularly with men on base, his offensive prowess is every bit as imposing.

The Brewers have imposed their will on October baseball. Friday's 3-2 victory over Tampa Bay in Game 1 of the 2026 World Series was merely the latest example.

But behind the plate and in the batter's box, William Contreras has been nothing short of tyrannical.

"If people aren't noticing Contreras, like he was maybe the MVP of the Dodgers series," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said after the game. "It should have been co-MVPS.He was maybe MVP of the Padres series. I don't think they pick one for that, do they?"

But Contreras has spent this October making one question why not. Because on this stage, in these environments, his presence has shifted outcomes. His edge has become contagious, his intensity woven into the fabric of a team that has never lacked either.

And that's what makes his October so remarkable. The Brewers haven't needed someone to carry them. Their offense has produced, their pitching has delivered, and their defense has been relentless. Yet somehow, amid all that collective excellence, Contreras has found a way to distinguish himself.

Not by doing what his teammates cannot, but by making an already formidable team that much harder to beat.

In Game 1, Contreras collected two singles, including a first-inning RBI knock that knocked in the second run of the game and set the tone for the Brewers' victory.

But, man, it was his work behind the plate that made the difference.

Victor Mesa Jr. opened the fifth with a single off Jacob Misiorowski, giving Tampa Bay the makings of a threat. Misiorowski retired the next two batters, but Mesa advanced to second on a fielder's choice. Then, with Yandy Díaz at the plate, two outs and an 0-2 count, Mesa took off for third.

Contreras fired a strike to third, cutting him down with ease.

On October 24, 1946, a V-2 rocket captured the first-ever photo of Earth from space.

While these grainy, black-and-white images might not look like much today, they were a huge deal at the time, because no one had ever seen Earth from space before.

The V-2 rocket launched from the White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico. It carried a 35-millimeter motion picture camera that captured a new frame every second and a half. The rocket soared to an altitude of about 65 miles before falling back to Earth.

Both the rocket and the camera were destroyed after crashing into the Earth at a speed of about 340 miles per hour. But the film survived because it was protected inside a steel case. Scientists had to drive out into the New Mexico desert to retrieve the film. When they saw the images for the first time, the scientists were literally jumping up and down with joy.

The ability to photograph Earth from space drove both an expanded interest to continue pushing into space and led to unexpected societal changes with regards to how humanity considered its home planet.

For military and intelligence agencies, the potential now existed for reconnaissance at a far larger scale than what was possible with high-altitude aircraft. That capability expanded to agriculture and meteorological purposes, eventually making Earth observation one of the most common reasons satellites and space stations are deployed into orbit today.

Earth photography from space also gave birth to the environmental movement, as people began to see the planet differently. The fragility of this orb became more apparent, as now Earth appeared a "blue marble" set against an endless black void.
