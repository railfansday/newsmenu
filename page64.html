Stop me if you’ve heard this one before: Neil Young is filing suit after “Rockin’ in the Free World” was played at a Trump rally. The lawsuit—filed yesterday (Monday, October 26) in Virginia’s Eastern District Court—specifically cites an “unauthorized public performance” of Young’s 1991 protest anthem at a rally in Youngstown, Ohio on October 12. The named defendant is actually not President Donald J. Trump himself, but rather the Trump 47 Committee, his primary political action committee and the event’s organizer.

“‘Rockin’ in the Free World’ is a blistering anthem that contrasts the hollow promises of American political rhetoric with the harsh realities faced by the poor, homeless, and forgotten,” the lawsuit reads. “The lyrics paint vivid, street-level portraits—a mother discarding her baby, addicts lost in despair, garbage piling in the shadows of prosperity—while the repeated chorus delivers a savage irony, celebrating freedom that clearly isn’t shared by all. In short, the Song is the opposite of everything that Donald J. Trump stands for.” Pitchfork has reached out to Young’s legal representation for further comment.

Young is seeking $10 million in damages on the grounds of copyright infringement, asserting that the Trump 47 Committee did not have “license or Young’s permission” to play the song at any “purely political event.” He also demands that neither “Rockin’ in the Free World” nor any of his other songs be played at future events organized by or associated with the PAC.

During the 2020 presidential election, Young sued Trump’s campaign committee, Donald J. Trump for President Inc., over their use of “Rockin’ in the Free World” and another of his songs, “Devil’s Sidewalk,” at campaign rallies. The parties settled out of court under the stipulation, according to the new suit, that the corporation “would never use ‘Rockin’ in the Free World’ without a license and permission from Young.” However, as a separate organization, the Trump 47 Committee would be exempt from the terms of that specific settlement. A new group theory result that came out of this formalization project is reported in this paper. While formalizing the first-generation proof, we found counterexamples to a closure assertion used in a published route to the Unbalanced Group Theorem. The paper explains the obstruction and gives an alternative argument for the simple-group case, built on Solomon’s 1976 theory of maximal 2-components, whose key step is Theorem 2.3, the preservation of simple tops.

We are working with group theorists to compile the errata we found in the literature, and will publish them here.

The classification of finite simple groups is one of the great collective achievements of mathematics. Its first-generation proof spans thousands of journal pages, written by about a hundred mathematicians over several decades. It shows that every finite simple group is cyclic of prime order, alternating, of Lie type, or one of 26 sporadic groups.

Here we present a complete formalization in Lean following the first-generation proof, with every logical step checked by Lean’s kernel. Along the way, we found an issue in a published argument for the Unbalanced Group Theorem. The accompanying paper explains the issue and the alternative route used in our proof. #607. Khan backs new inquiry to improve welfare, health and safety in boxing

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Boxing lags behind other sports in welfare and safety standards; leading UK King’s Counsel launches inquiry to improve standards across boxing and combat sports; Global Fighters Association aims to establish boxing’s first trade union

Former world champion Amir Khan is among a growing number of boxers backing a King’s Counsel-led inquiry aimed at improving welfare, health and safety standards across boxing and other combat sports.

Khan is one of Britain's most decorated boxers who won multiple world championship belts across several weights.

He has backed the Global Fighters Association's (GFA) appointment of leading UK barrister Jonathan Caplan KC to lead a "root-and-branch" inquiry into the safety, governance and treatment of boxers at every level of the sport.

Jonathan Caplan KC has been tasked with leading an inquiry to gather evidence from within boxing and other combat sports in order to raise standards around welfare, health and safety and governance issues.

He intends to consult a broad range of stakeholders, including fighters, coaches, physiotherapists, doctors, surgeons, referees, judges, promoters, boards of control, governing bodies, media commentators, venue operators, managers, and the friends and families of fighters.

Caplan, speaking to Sky Sports said: "We will publish I hope far-reaching recommendations explaining our conclusions.

"We're doing this inquiry late, it should have been done a long time ago.

"There have been other significant inquiries into sport. An MP-led select committee held an inquiry into concussion in sport around two years ago, while the Whyte Review examined allegations of physical and emotional abuse in British Gymnastics. Combat sports are now crying out for the same level of detailed scrutiny. The welfare of participants across all combat sports must be fundamental to what we're trying to achieve. This inquiry is long overdue, and it's something that has never been done before."

The inquiry has been commissioned by the Global Fighters Association (GFA), which aims to establish itself as the first trade union representing boxers and participants across other combat sports.

The GFA's goal is to become a collective voice for everyone involved in combat sports, from those training and competing at their local gym to professional athletes who depend on the sport for their livelihood. Its ambition is to represent participants at every level, both inside and outside the ring.

While the sport's biggest stars generate a significant proportion of its revenue, for every global superstar such as Anthony Joshua or Tyson Fury, there are hundreds of fighters further down the ladder working hard to make a living from the sport. Their welfare, financial security and long-term health are among the issues the inquiry aims to address.

Paul Smith enjoyed a professional boxing career spanning 2003 to 2017, competing at middleweight and super-middleweight. During that time, he won both English and British titles and challenged for a world title on three occasions.

Now a trainer and co-founder of the GFA, Smith told Sky Sports: "The average fighter going to work every day is just trying to make a living from professional boxing. There is enough money in the sport for them to earn a living, but at the moment, it's simply not reaching those fighters.

"There isn't a system in place that adequately protects fighters when it comes to health and safety. We've seen so many people injured in the ring and, unfortunately, boxing isn't a sport where you simply lose a race. You can suffer serious injuries, be hit and even be killed. As sad as it is, it happens."

Smith added: "We want to establish a union where fighters feel safe and know they have someone on their side. If something goes wrong, they should be able to pick up the phone and speak to someone who can support them straight away.

"There are so many situations where rules may have been breached and a fighter is required to attend a hearing or answer questions. We want a GFA representative to be there alongside them, ensuring they have support and someone looking after their interests. The same applies if they have a disagreement with their manager or become involved in a dispute."

The GFA says that, in recent months, several fighters have suffered serious injuries in and around the ring, leaving their families to rely on public fundraising pages and the goodwill of fellow fighters and promoters to cover emergency medical treatment and rehabilitation costs. The association argues that these cases highlight the need for a more robust, sport-wide system of protection and support for fighters.

Former world champion Amir Khan also voiced his support for the initiative, saying: "There has to be an organisation that sits above everything, somewhere we can all turn to when something goes wrong, whether it's in training, at a weigh-in, with a contract or even in retirement. The GFA is that organisation."

Founded in 2022, the Global Fighters Association (GFA) is stepping up its efforts to improve welfare and safety across boxing and other combat sports, with its ambitions now extending beyond a group of former fighters campaigning for change.

The association's chair-designate is Simon Bazalgette, former chief executive of the Jockey Club and an independent non-executive director of the English Football League (EFL). His experience across professional sport brings an additional perspective to the GFA's mission.

Bazalgette said: "Over the last decade or so, professional sports such as rugby, football and horse racing have raised their game when it comes to the welfare of the participants."

"They recognise that the brand of the sport increasingly depends on demonstrating that best-in-class welfare underpins any aspiration to grow the audience and the commercial value of the sport.

"It is striking that combat sport is one of the last workplaces with no professional representation for its workers, meaning little protection, no minimum health and safety standard, no independent pension scheme, no insurance floor, and no single body a fighter can turn to when something goes wrong."

Jonathan Caplan KC confirmed he intends to conduct several inquiries, with the first focusing on health and safety in boxing and other combat sports. He hopes to publish the initial report by spring 2027.

Speaking to Sky Sports, Caplan said he would examine how other sports have approached similar challenges and consider the lessons that could be applied to combat sports.

"I'm certainly very interested in looking at comparative sports, the conclusions they've reached and the regulatory overviews that have been carried out.

"For example, a DCMS select committee inquiry into concussion in sport is going to be of great interest to me. I'll be looking at what's already been done elsewhere and, where it's relevant, we want to learn from it and apply those lessons."

Caplan stressed that the inquiry would be evidence-led, with fighters and their families contributing first-hand accounts to help inform its findings and recommendations.

"The whole purpose is to produce a comprehensive and reasoned report. We'll be speaking to fighters and their families to gather case studies. Those who choose not to follow the proposals we put forward will have to explain why, particularly if the evidence supports them.

"I suspect the evidence is going to point to quite a lot of innovations that are necessary and long overdue. I don't think anybody wants to see those recommendations ignored, and I don't think anyone wants to be the person who fails to act on sensible recommendations."

#148. A 28-Year-Old DOGE Operative Gutted USAID. HR Had No Record of Him Working There

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On March 18, 2025, Secretary of State Marco Rubio handed the reins of the US Agency for International Development to Jeremy Lewin, a 28-year-old graduate of Harvard Law School, former investment banker, and affiliate of Elon Musk’s so-called Department of Government Efficiency. At the time, Lewin’s appointment drew criticism because of his lack of experience in government, diplomacy, or foreign assistance. But for career staff inside the agency, the issues surrounding Lewin’s new role were much more alarming than his thin résumé.

For at least two months, USAID senior staff had no knowledge of how Lewin, who appeared at the agency one day as a policymaker on an obscure detail from the General Services Administration, could have suddenly begun wielding the powers of the agency’s top official.

Internal USAID emails reviewed by WIRED reveal that during the three-week period of March 18 to April 4, during which Lewin began canceling lifesaving aid programs and personally signed off on the termination of thousands of USAID employees, he did not sign the paperwork typically required by law to begin work at the agency, despite repeated requests. In fact, his appointment was so mysterious that USAID security was unable to issue him an ID card because they had no records of his employment.

Lewin, currently director of policy planning at the State Department, tells WIRED that prior to Rubio delegating powers to him, he was “both detailed and dual appointed to work at USAID by Secretary Rubio according to well-established authorities and with the advice of State Department and White House counsel.”

Experts, though, say that while Lewin performed the duties of a top USAID leader under the title Rubio handed to him—deputy administrator of policy and programming and chief operating officer—it isn’t clear that he had the legal authority to do so. Questions about how DOGE affiliates like Lewin stepped into top roles at many federal agencies, including USAID, remain key to dozens of active lawsuits targeting DOGE and the Trump administration over federal workforce reductions, firings, and agency dismantlings.

Lewin has said, in a declaration submitted to the court in J. Doe 4 v. U.S. DOGE Service, that he began serving at USAID as a senior adviser as well as a director for strategy and programs on January 28, 2025. On March 18, according to court filings, Rubio, in his role as USAID’s acting administrator, delegated the authority of the deputy administrator of policy and programming and chief operating officer to Lewin, and also signed a document appointing Lewin an “officer of the United States.”

According to the emails reviewed by WIRED, on March 20, two days after Rubio placed Lewin atop the USAID hierarchy, White House liaison to USAID Adam Korzeniewski notified HR to begin the process of onboarding Lewin as a senior adviser performing duties as deputy administrator. Four days later, the emails reveal, after prompting from HR, Korzeniewski provided a proposed start date—March 18—and the next day, Lewin was sent a temporary job offer.

By March 26, the emails show, Lewin still hadn’t accepted the job offer, and had not filed his OF-306, the federal government's standard pre-employment background screening form; without this document, a federal agency cannot lawfully issue a final job offer or consummate an appointment. An HR executive noted in an email dated March 31 that Lewin had not completed the onboarding tasks required to initiate “the security reciprocity process,” which recognizes the transfer of an employees’ security clearance from one agency to another. (A detailing agreement between the GSA and USAID dated February 14—two weeks after Lewin says he began at USAID—specifes that “commencement of the detail is contingent upon the Detailee’s successful adjudication and receipt of any necessary security clearances and other pre-employment screening required by USAID.”)

On March 28, Lewin signed a letter terminating all remaining USAID staff. And as he executed sweeping powers to liquidate all 60 of USAID’s overseas missions and cancel thousands of lifesaving projects worldwide, HR repeatedly followed up with Korzeniewski.

More than a week after Lewin’s appointment, the emails show, a USAID official told Korzeniewski that the agency’s office of security was “unable to issue a USAID ID card to Jeremy Lewin as SEC [the agency’s secure database] did not have a record of Jeremy as an employee.”

“Until you’re onboarded at USAID, you aren’t even in the system,” says Natalia Machuca, a 15-year agency veteran who hired many staff. “You can’t get a badge, get into your computer, or even walk around the building.”

Lewin tells WIRED, “I was a regular government employee with a valid security clearance held by the State Department during this entire period. USAID HR was confused about this paperwork for a period of days, but this issue was cleared up once they were made aware of the dual appointment and my State Department position.”

But according to a top executive in HR, no one from their office saw any delegation documents until late April 2025. As one longtime HR executive at the agency, speaking on the condition of anonymity, said of Lewin: “He was the equivalent of an administrative ghost.”

“I think this creates a huge accountability problem if you have these actors who are making really consequential life-or-death decisions who didn't go through any standard confirmation process,” says Don Moynihan, a public policy professor at the University of Michigan, “or in this case didn't even complete their paperwork to become a formal employee.”

Lewin—who a White House official says submitted all the paperwork required for employment and clearance—provided WIRED with two documents, both dated March 18, 2025. One is an appointment and compensation waiver, signed by Rubio, naming Lewin director of strategy and planning, and stating that he would not be paid “pursuant to, among other provisions of law, 22 U.S.C. § 2385(b).” The other is a standard appointment affidavit naming Lewin USAID’s director of strategy and programs. A signature on the form appears to be Rubio’s, with the title given as “HRO”—usually an abbreviation for human resources officer. The State Department did not answer questions about this document.

To one former senior USAID official, Rubio’s use of 22 U.S.C. § 2385(b)—an obscure provision traditionally reserved for rapidly deploying emergency-response staff to disaster zones abroad—to appoint Lewin looks like a legal contortion.

“It is so unconventional that I’d never seen it used before I got to USAID,” the official said. “It was created for the purposes of scaling up really quickly if there was some outbreak of a virus somewhere and they needed to bring people on, then let them go.”

Brian Feinstein, a law professor at the Wharton School of Business, acknowledged that while using 22 U.S.C. § 2385(b) to appoint Lewin is complex, it appears to him to be legal. “Because Lewin's appointment is consistent with this law regarding USAID personnel,” he says, “his unusual status should not be a factor.”

Outside of this point, legal questions also remain about Lewin’s actions at the agency before he was appointed by Rubio, and his failure to complete the onboarding required to begin his initial detail period at USAID. During this time he was part of a team that issued sweeping orders, including one to then acting administrator Jason Gray to lock all USAID staff worldwide out of email and all communication systems, which could have put many staff’s lives at risk, and to terminate 57 of the agency’s top officials.

David A. Super, professor of law and economics at Georgetown University, describes Lewin’s failure to complete his onboarding as much more than an administrative slip-up. “You can’t just walk in the door of a federal agency and become an employee,” he says. Under federal law, a cabinet secretary can only assign the authority of a Senate-confirmed position such as USAID administrator to a subordinate official—an agency employee or officer who works under the supervision of a higher-ranking officer. These regulations, Super says, are a way for Congress to guarantee that “someone walking off the street should not be given these enormous powers.”

Nina Mendelson, who teaches law at the University of Michigan, agrees. She says the administration appeared to be trying to bypass the Federal Vacancies Reform Act, a federal statute that dictates how temporary vacancies in Senate-confirmed executive branch positions are filled. “The FVRA specifies who may fill in, for a limited time, when a Senate-confirmed post is vacant,” she says. “The possibilities are a first assistant, someone holding another Senate-confirmed post, or a longtime civil servant in the particular agency. Lewin doesn’t seem to have been any one of those.”

Lewin being placed atop USAID came in a specific context. In February 2025, shortly after the arrival of Lewin and other DOGE affiliates at the agency, two dozen current and former USAID employees and contractors sued Musk and DOGE in Maryland District Court. They successfully secured a preliminary injunction on March 18, on the grounds that special government employees—temporary hires from outside USAID and the government—lacked the authority to execute sweeping federal actions. That same day, Rubio delegated powers of the agency’s top job to Lewin.

On March 28, the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals granted the Justice Department an emergency stay, pausing the injunction based on the administration’s argument that the actions were taken by Lewin in his role as a federally appointed official. In his declaration, Lewin wrote: “I am not, and have never been, an employee of Elon Musk or USDS,” adding, “I report directly and only to Acting Administrator Rubio” in his role at USAID.

The ruling gave Lewin the green light to fire thousands of career civil service and Foreign Service staff that very day, but the legal battle didn’t end there. The district judge has since allowed thousands of affected employees to sue DOGE and the administration as a group and ordered the government to hand over a trove of internal documents.

Kevin Byrnes, a veteran federal employment law attorney representing more than 170 former and current career USAID staff in a separate suit challenging DOGE’s role in the mass firings, says the fact that Lewin had not completed mandatory onboarding paperwork raises even more questions about the authority of nonconfirmed agency leadership, and potentially exposes Lewin personally.

“If Lewin didn’t even fill out the papers and was not authorized to take these actions, then he could be exposed to personal liability,” says Byrnes, who is asking the court to reinstate all the plaintiffs to their USAID jobs, with back pay.

#4365. The Federal Government Is Working to Create New Tire Safety Standards

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Updated testing methods and more modern tech such as puncture-proof tires are on NHTSA’s wishlist for safety upgrades.

They may not be particularly sexy, but tires are one of the most critical safety and performance features on every car. Where the rubber meets the road matters—and federal safety regulators plan to overhaul tire safety standards in coming years.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration announced on Monday, October 26, that it intends to modernize the U.S. government’s tire safety standards in order to to enhance driver safety and lower automotive costs. Regulators plan to modernize outdated tire safety tests, including tire strength, rim resistance, and endurance, to account for modern technology. Federal officials said these updates will be the first significant changes to tire standards in nearly 20 years.

“As American tire technology and vehicle design continue to evolve, our regulatory framework must keep pace,” said NHTSA administrator Jonathan Morrison. “By bringing key industry stakeholders and other technical safety experts to the same table, we can modernize outdated standards to boost safety and reduce costs for you and your family.”

Federal regulators will also evaluate new technologies, including puncture-proof tires and modernized tire sidewall markings. Weight limit guidelines for trucks and SUVs will also be reviewed. U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy will lead the process and appoint 18 stakeholders from tire manufacturers, automakers, safety organizations, research institutions, and engineering associations. NHTSA is soliciting public comments on the scope, committee membership balance, and formal nominations for membership.

Tire industry leaders largely applauded the updated standard initiative. “Modern standards make for safer roads. USTMA applauds Administrator Morrison for taking on work the tire industry has urged for years: bringing federal tire standards in line with the tires Americans drive on today. Outdated tire testing standards designed for the tires of the 1960s tell us nothing about whether today’s tires are safe,” Anne Forristall Luke, president and CEO of the U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association, said. “In fact, those outdated standards prevent American drivers from accessing the latest technology currently available in much of the rest of the world. Bringing our standards into line with modern tire technology will ensure Americans have access to the highest-performing tires made today. USTMA’s members look forward to contributing their engineering expertise to the process.”

Tire regulations have been a surprisingly hot-button topic this year. Earlier this summer, U.S. senators Roger Wicker (R-MS) and Raphael Warnock (D-GA) introduced the Tire Safety Modernization Act of 2026, which potentially prompted the U.S. Department of Transportation to begin updating federal tire testing standards. California, meanwhile, recently enacted new tire efficiency standards, forcing replacement tires to be as or more efficient than the factory-installed set. Golden State regulators said the rule—which will go into effect in 2029—will reduce carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions by 2 million metric tons per year.
