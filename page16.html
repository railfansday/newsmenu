#3348. If You're Worried Inflation Will Wreck Your Retirement, Here Are 3 Must-Dos

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Inflation can be a serious threat to your retirement plans. And while inflation doesn't always hit people smack in the face like it does today, it can still be a problem.

Even when prices rise by relatively modest amounts, the cumulative effect over 20 or 30 years can significantly reduce the purchasing power of your savings. Expenses that seem manageable at the start of retirement, from groceries to utilities to healthcare, could consume a much larger share of your retirement income in the future.

The good news is that you don't have to accept inflation as an unavoidable threat to your retirement. Here are three important moves that could give you a leg up on inflation.

Your Social Security benefits might play a meaningful role in your retirement income. Once you turn 62, you can begin collecting benefits at any time. If you wait for your full retirement age to arrive, you'll get your monthly benefits without a reduction.

However, if you delay your Social Security claim past full retirement age, your benefits are eligible for an 8% boost for each year you wait, up to age 70. With a full retirement age of 67, which is the case if you were born in 1960 or later, you can potentially score a 24% increase to your monthly checks.

A larger monthly benefit can be particularly valuable when inflation starts to soar. Social Security benefits receive annual cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) that are designed to help those payments keep pace with inflation. Starting with a larger monthly benefit can mean receiving larger dollar increases over time.

Of course, delaying isn't right for everyone. Your health, life expectancy, and other factors need to be considered in your Social Security claiming decision. But if you're able to cover your expenses from retirement savings or other income sources while you wait, delaying Social Security until age 70 could make it easier to manage inflation throughout your senior years.

Keeping all of your retirement savings in cash or low-yielding investments might feel like the safest move. But it could expose you to substantial purchasing-power risk over time. If inflation averages 3% annually, but your savings only grow 2% annually, you're going to fall behind.

Stocks have historically provided long-term growth that outpaces inflation. Although the stock market can be volatile, a diversified portfolio of high-quality companies or index funds could help your savings keep up without exposing you to undue risk -- especially if you limit stocks to a portion of your portfolio only.

The right percentage and investment mix depends on your risk tolerance and income needs. For some people, a 65% stock allocation might work. For others, 50% may be optimal. Either way, the goal is to balance growth potential with stability so that your portfolio can support near-term and long-term withdrawals.

Even with a strategic portfolio and a boosted monthly Social Security benefit, inflation can strain your finances if your spending plan is too rigid. It's important to be flexible when costs skyrocket because a few months of cutting back could help your savings last longer.

Start by separating essential expenses such as housing, food, utilities, and healthcare from discretionary costs such as travel and entertainment. That distinction could help you identify where to adjust your spending if prices rise faster than expected or your investments underperform.

Also revisit your budget regularly. If markets decline or essential expenses surge, temporarily reducing nonessential purchases could help preserve your savings.

Inflation has the potential to erode your buying power over time. But that doesn't mean you have to sit back and let it. By delaying Social Security, choosing the right investments, and being willing to adjust your spending as needed, you can set yourself up to thrive even as costs increase throughout your retirement.

#4762. Quantum fears already limiting Bitcoin’s growth, Stacks founder says

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Speaking on Cointelegraph’s Chain Reaction podcast, Muneeb Ali warned that uncertainty over Bitcoin’s quantum security could deter institutional investors.

Concerns about quantum computing are slowing institutional investment in Bitcoin, according to Stacks founder Muneeb Ali, who warned that the network needs a credible roadmap for security upgrades to restore investor confidence.

“There is already a ceiling on the growth of Bitcoin because of quantum fears,” Ali said during an interview on Cointelegraph’s Chain Reaction podcast. He argued that uncertainty could lead risk-averse institutional investors to delay large Bitcoin purchases until developers establish a credible path toward quantum-resistant security.

Stacks is a Bitcoin layer-2 network designed to bring smart contracts and decentralized applications to Bitcoin. Ali co-founded the project, which uses Bitcoin as its underlying settlement layer.

Chain Reaction Podcast. Source: Cointelegraph

Ali warned that Bitcoin could enter a riskier period if developers fail to make meaningful progress toward quantum-resistant security by 2028. He said upgrades typically take years to implement, with additional time needed for users to move their holdings to new addresses.

Ali pointed to 2030 as a target for transitioning to quantum-resistant security, citing guidance from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). He suggested advances in artificial intelligence could accelerate the development of quantum computers, potentially bringing that timeline forward to 2029.

Ali acknowledged that some Bitcoin proponents believe the quantum computing threat remains distant and concerns are overblown. He argued, however, that disagreements over the urgency of the threat could themselves undermine investor confidence, particularly without a credible upgrade roadmap.

Related: Justin Sun says Tron’s post-quantum cryptography has gone live on testnet

Despite his concerns, Ali said a sufficiently powerful quantum computer would more likely emerge from a major research laboratory than an unknown attacker. He suggested companies such as Google or Microsoft would have little incentive to use the technology to steal Bitcoin, given the potential legal consequences.

Ali also said a quantum security threat to Bitcoin would affect the broader cryptocurrency industry, even projects developing their own protections. “If Bitcoin gets hit, everyone gets hit downstream as well,” he said.

Meanwhile, developers are exploring ways to protect Bitcoin against quantum attacks. In September, StarkWare reported that researchers had reduced the estimated computing cost of preparing an experimental quantum-resistant Bitcoin transaction by 79%, following a weeklong optimization challenge.

The approach requires no changes to Bitcoin’s consensus rules, but the latest cost reductions have been demonstrated only in benchmark tests. StarkWare has described the technology as a last-resort measure and continues to advocate for a protocol-level upgrade as a longer-term solution.

#4682. Robinhood's crypto revenue fell 55% from last year, but topped Street forecasts

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Robinhood's HOOD$94.61·Market Closed third-quarter cryptocurrency trading business was down 55% from last year but still topped Street forecasts.

Crypto transaction revenue dropped to $120 million in the third quarter, according to the company's earnings report released Tuesday. That was down from about $267 million in the same period last year, but ahead of Wall Street analysts’ expectations of $114.1 million.

That left crypto accounting for just 8.4% of Robinhood's $1.42 billion in total quarterly revenue, compared with roughly 21% a year earlier.

The decline came as customers traded less crypto across Robinhood's platforms. Total crypto trading volume fell 38% to $50 billion, including $21 billion through the Robinhood app, down 48%, and $29 billion through crypto exchange Bitstamp, down 28%.

The slump in crypto trading revenue stood in contrast to Robinhood's broader business. Total revenue rose 12% year-over-year to $1.424 billion, helped by growth in options, equities and prediction markets. Street estimates had been for $1.43 billion.

Options trading generated $399 million in revenue, up 31%, while equities revenue climbed 41% to $121 million. Prediction markets brought in $173 million, more than six times the year-earlier figure.

Third-quarter EPS of $0.51 was less than consensus forecasts for $0.55.

Despite weaker crypto trading, Robinhood continued to build out its digital asset business.

The company said Robinhood Chain, its blockchain network, processed more than $80 billion in decentralized exchange trading volume and generated over $50 million in chain fees during its first quarter operating on mainnet.

Those fees reflect activity on the blockchain and should not be confused with Robinhood's reported crypto transaction revenue. The company did not disclose how much of the chain fees it recognized as revenue.

Shares of the company were down about 5% in post-market trading.

Re:Build, the Massachusetts-based manufacturing accelerator that spun out EV startup Slate Auto in 2023, has raised $2.1 billion

The company said Tuesday that it will use the funding to “expand Re:Build’s capacity to manufacture hard tech products and launch new industrial startups,” which will “ensure the most innovative products can be made in America for generations to come.” The investment was led by General Catalyst and JPMorganChase’s Strategic Investment Group. Other investors weren’t disclosed.

The funding round comes as increasing attention and capital is flowing to the idea of building up manufacturing and supply chains in the United States, especially as a hedge away from China.

“We started Re:Build because the deindustrialization of America is an economic failure and national security risk,” Re:Build co-founder Jeff Wilke said in a statement. “This capital allows Re:Build to go faster and further than would otherwise be possible, and build a great manufacturing business and a platform for American industrial leadership. The products and companies emerging from our ecosystem prove we can help restore America’s industrial might.”

General Catalyst’s CEO Hemant Taneja echoed those sentiments in a statement saying, “General Catalyst has long believed that rebuilding American industrial capacity is one of the most important economic and national security imperatives of our time.”

Taneja added: “To compete with China, re-industrialize the U.S., and revitalize the towns and jobs that have been left behind, we need platform companies that can operate at genuine scale—and we need our innovation ecosystem to learn how to build hardware again.”

Founded in 2020, Re:Build bills itself as “America’s answer to Shenzhen,” and it has numerous ties to Amazon. It was co-founded by Wilke, who was Amazon’s former commercial CEO. Wei Gao, a longtime Amazon VP and technical adviser to Bezos, is Re:Build’s chief digital and product officer. Multiple other Re:Build executives also worked for the e-commerce giant.

Re:Build claims to have “launched hundreds of products and production systems” over its six-year existence. The company tends to operate two ways. It enters partnerships with industrial companies to “design, engineer, and manufacture products at scale across verticals including aerospace and defense.” It also incubates startups in “critical, underserved markets.”

Re:Build says its network of companies employs more than 2,100 people in the U.S. and houses more than 2.4 million square feet of manufacturing capacity.

Wilke and his co-founder Miles Arnone came up with the idea of Slate Auto in 2022, and decided to spin it out in order to help it attract more capital. The company has since gone on to raise at least $1.4 billion, including funding from Bezos’ family office, in an attempt to build an ultra low-cost, bare bones pickup truck that can be customized to a customer’s liking.

With plenty of new shows arriving on the 2026 TV premiere schedule, I've still got a soft spot for the Disney Channel shows that dominated my afternoons in the early 2000s. Even Stevens and Lizzie McGuire were two of the network's biggest hits, and I always assumed they coexisted pretty peacefully. Apparently, somebody forgot to tell Shia LaBeouf. According to his former co-star Christy Carlson Romano, he had a one-sided rivalry with Hilary Duff, and I genuinely had no idea.

Romano, who played LaBeouf's older sister Ren on Even Stevens, recently appeared on The Joe Vulpis Podcast, where the conversation turned to whether Disney Channel stars had beef with their Nickelodeon counterparts. She said they rarely saw those actors and that the real competition was happening within Disney, especially when it came to ratings. That's when Romano brought up LaBeouf's feelings about Duff's hugely popular show:

Shia was definitely beefing with Hilary. Hilary had no clue about that. I'm sure she was doing her own thing, but he would always be kind of like, trash-talking Lizzie McGuire. And I was always like, ‘We don't know anything about them.’ Like, why?

The fact that Duff apparently didn't even know there was a rivalry is what gets me. Imagine minding your own business, playing a teenager whose animated alter ego occasionally narrates her problems, and learning decades later that another Disney Channel star was apparently trash-talking your show. That's some remarkably specific after-school drama.

There was a real ratings competition behind it, though. Even Stevens arrived in 2000 and turned LaBeouf's troublemaking Louis Stevens into a breakout character. Lizzie McGuire debuted the following year and quickly became one of the channel's defining hits. Interestingly, Duff's journey to landing the role was pretty wild, though she probably had no idea she'd eventually become part of an alleged rivalry with another Disney star.

Romano suggested the competition mattered especially deeply to LaBeouf, whose childhood and family circumstances had been difficult. She wasn't excusing the comments, exactly, but she offered some perspective on why he might have taken the rivalry so seriously:

He was very competitive. He wanted to be the number one show on the network. He had to be the number one show because I think his life depended on that show doing well. He had a rough upbringing and I think he needed that show to do well for him.

That puts a different spin on Romano's story for me. We're talking about kids carrying two of Disney Channel's biggest shows, and she believes LaBeouf saw Even Stevens as something he couldn't afford to lose. He's spoken about his complicated childhood before, even exploring parts of it in Honey Boy, although he later acknowledged that elements of the film's depiction of his father were fictionalized. Of course, this is Romano's recollection of his behavior more than two decades later, and from the sound of it, Duff didn't even know there was a rivalry.

Romano hasn't exactly sugarcoated her own experience working with LaBeouf, either. In a previous interview, she described their Even Stevens relationship as competitive and sometimes difficult, particularly as the show began focusing more on his character. The two aren't in contact today, although she's said she still wishes him well.

Here's another funny little Disney Channel connection: Romano and Duff actually starred together in the 2002 movie Cadet Kelly, which Duff recently discussed revisiting for a potential sequel. So while LaBeouf was apparently keeping score, his co-star was making movies with the very person he considered competition.

As for Duff, her relationship with Lizzie McGuire has continued long after the original series ended. She's even spoken candidly about Disney's decision to scrap the planned revival, making it clear that she still cares about the character and what the show meant to fans.

Neither Duff nor LaBeouf had publicly responded to Romano's latest claims when the initial reports broke. Still, I know I'll be thinking about this the next time I revisit either show. It's pretty funny to imagine that while the rest of us were flipping between Even Stevens and Lizzie McGuire after school, one of their stars was apparently keeping score the whole time.

#4316. Tesla's Cybercab Fixes A Big Repairability Problem With Its Structural Batteries

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Tesla helped pioneer a shift toward structural battery packs that eliminate traditional modules to save weight and squeeze more energy into a smaller space. But with its latest vehicle, America's EV leader is moving in the other direction in the name of repairability.

Instead of embedding hundreds of in-house 4680 battery cells into a single, tightly integrated assembly, as Tesla did with the structural battery packs found in some Model Ys and the Cybertruck, the Cybercab contains four separate modules of those cells. That means a faulty module can be swapped out without replacing the entire battery pack. Tesla executives explained the decision in a video on YouTube channel of the engineering firm Munro & Associates.

"It's good to be self-critical," said Robert Sumpf, the director of mechanical design engineering for Tesla's battery modules and packs. "I'm going to give our Model Y structural battery a grade D," he added, noting that it was an "awesome product" to the customer but was hard to build.

Tesla 4680 battery cells assembled in a structural pack, without the potting shown.

The other issue with the structural approach is serviceability, he said. Since all the cells are bound together in the pack using a hard potting material, it's practically impossible to repair the battery if one cell is defective, for example. Indeed, after tearing down a Model Y with a structural pack a few years back, Munro & Associates concluded that its battery had "zero repairability."

There are advantages to getting rid of conventional module structures (the building blocks of a battery pack) and making the battery itself structural to the vehicle, in that the design lets manufacturers stuff more cells into a given area and save some material costs. It's why automakers including BMW, Porsche, and Ford are now designing cars with cells that go directly into packs or even directly into the vehicle's body structure.

Tesla's top brass, though, didn't think the serviceability tradeoff was worth it in the Cybercab. Sumpf described the Cybercab's design as "semi-structural." "You're going to get maybe 10% more volume for cells in a structural pack when you delete the structure," Sumpf said. "You're going to save a little cost as a result, but the issue is the service cost."

In general, studies show that EV batteries rarely fail. Degradation is typically predictable and manageable over the lifetime of a consumer EV. However, the Cybercab is supposed to hold up hundreds of thousands of miles of round-the-clock abuse as a robotaxi. That may help explain the decision to switch things up battery-wise. Sure, a structural, module-free pack may help eke out a few extra miles of range, which is great for selling EVs to regular people. But for a fleet-based business, total cost of ownership (TCO) is everything.

"When you think of TCO as we've talked about, replacing an entire pack before half a million miles is a killer on cost," Eric Earley, the Cybercab's chief engineer, told the Munro Live YouTube channel. "So we have to think of [remanufacturing], we have to think of pack serviceability, we have to think of all those things that actually get us to our cost target for the product."
