#33. UK poised to miss reduced aid spending target of 0.3% of national income

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Calculations from the Center for Global Development say spending next year is likely to be £410m less than anticipated

The UK is on course to miss its target of spending 0.3% of national income on international aid by an estimated £400m by the end of next year.

Calculations from the Center for Global Development (CGD) found the UK was on course for a large shortfall in promised official development assistance at the end of 2027.

The researchers said the central forecast from the Treasury’s comparison of independent experts implied UK aid would be £410m less than anticipated – at only 0.29% of national income.

However, they said it was “very plausible” that the shortfall would be £580m, at only 0.28% of national income. Even the most conservative forecast implies a £250m shortfall, according to the estimates.

Ian Mitchell, a senior fellow at the CGD and co-director of its Europe programme, described these as “material shortfalls in the spending power of UK aid”.

Making up a shortfall of even £400m would be enough to halve cuts to Africa that are likely to cost thousands of lives and mean the UK’s de facto exit from the south and east of the continent, he said.

The CGD attributed the shortfall to higher than expected inflation, which means national income will be higher than forecast at the spending review, and a 2025 policy change around the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) budget.

The researchers called on ministers to consider allocating additional funds to international aid in this week’s budget, to avoid the embarrassment and criticism from missing a target.

The 0.3% spending target for 2027 is far below the 0.7% promised in Labour’s election manifesto, having been downgraded by Keir Starmer to boost spending on defence.

Fleur Anderson, a former minister who sits on the foreign affairs select committee, said she would like to see higher spending on development aid in the budget.

She said: “I’d like to see this government going further and reversing these cuts altogether in time, but at the very least we should be hitting the reduced target that was set.

“Falling short isn’t an abstract accounting issue. It means communities not having clean water, girls pulled out of school, vaccines that don’t get delivered, peace programmes in war zones cut.

“The upcoming budget presents real opportunity for the Treasury to address this and for the government to show that its words on Britain’s place in the world are matched by action.”

Charities have repeatedly said that the government’s deep aid cuts will hit children’s education and increase the risk of disease and death in some African countries.

The latest figures released by the FCDO in September showed that British aid cuts under Starmer’s government hit the world’s poorest countries the hardest, while higher-income countries’ share of aid increased. The data showed a 6.7% decrease in funding to the least-developed and low-income countries, amounting to cuts of £118m.

The UK’s overall official development assistance (ODA) budget in 2025 decreased to 0.44% of gross national income, compared with 0.5% in 2024, a drop of £987m.

The government has prioritised spending through multilateral agencies such as the World Bank’s development arm, the International Development Association, and the global vaccines initiative Gavi. It has also continued to fund humanitarian support.

The Conservative party and Reform UK have pledged to slash aid even further, from about £9bn a year to £1bn – a level that would leave the UK unable to cover existing contributions to the UN and World Bank.

An FCDO spokesperson said: “The publication of the FCDO multi-year ODA allocations in March 2026 set out our spending plans and priorities for the next three financial years based on GNI [gross national income] at the time of the 2025 spending review.

“Moving to a fixed budget reduces volatility and provides the necessary stability to deliver high-impact aid programmes.”

#5153. South Korea to Delay Crypto Tax Yet Again

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It looks like there will be a fourth delay to the controversial crypto tax in South Korea, which could mean another two years before the country's crypto investors will have to start paying taxes on the profits from their digital asset investments.

As reported by local media, the country's presidential office is leaning towards a delay in the introduction of the country's crypto tax. The tax is currently set to take effect on Jan. 1, 2027.

The tax proposes to tax annual crypto profits above 2.5 million won (around $1,888) at a rate of 22%, which includes a 20% income tax and an extra 2% local tax.

However, there has been increasing pushback from lawmakers and the country's crypto industry calling on the country to hold off on the crypto tax because they say the country isn't ready to implement it.

A major concern is that the government doesn't have the ability to effectively monitor cryptocurrency transactions done on foreign exchanges.

The concern is that if the country goes ahead and imposes the tax without having an effective international reporting system in place, it will put South Korean crypto exchanges and their users at a disadvantage.

The crypto tax was first approved in South Korea in 2020 with plans to bring the tax into force in January 2022.

But the implementation of the tax has been delayed three times since then. First from 2022 to 2023, then to 2025, and then it was finally set to take effect in 2027.

Now it seems it could be delayed again until 2029.

Earlier this month, Democratic Party lawmaker Park Min-kyu proposed a bill that ties the implementation of the crypto tax to international information-sharing agreements.

The bill would see crypto taxation delayed until two years after the first exchange of cryptocurrency transaction information under the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF).

CARF aims to allow tax authorities to track cross-border crypto transactions.

The U.S. is set to join the system in 2029. And there's concern that South Korean authorities might find it difficult to access data on transactions involving U.S. trading platforms prior to that.

#3592. Rosettes and Other Hidden Traces of Hamburg's Tram

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Almost 50 years after Hamburg’s last tram ran, I went looking for what it left behind, from rosettes on house walls to rails under the asphalt.

When I was writing about the Fuhle, I came across a small detail: in 1895, the first tram line in Hamburg built for electric operation from the start ran along Fuhlsbüttler Straße. I knew that Hamburg once had trams, but I never knew much about them. Berlin and Munich still have tram networks, so why is Hamburg the largest city in Germany without one?

Digging deeper, I realised the network was much bigger than I thought. In 1954, Hamburg’s trams carried about 251 million passengers. To put that into perspective, all Hochbahn buses together carried 261 million passengers in 2025, and the U-Bahn 291 million. The network was so big that, almost 50 years after the last tram ran in 1978, its traces are still around us.

So I went looking for them. I wanted to put these traces together like pieces of a map, so that when you walk past one of them, you can picture the tram network that once connected the whole city.

Hamburg’s tram story starts with horses. In 1866, the first horse-drawn tram started running from the city centre to Wandsbek, which back then was still a separate town.

In 1894, the first electric tram started running around the old town. The overhead wire was hung from masts on wide streets and fixed to house walls with cast-iron rosettes on narrow ones. Altona, still a separate city then, had its own tram company, with its own design of richly decorated cast-iron masts. Over the following decades, the network grew from Harburg in the south to Schnelsen in the north.

The bombing of 1943 destroyed large parts of it, and some routes never came back. In 1958, the SPD and FDP Senate decided to expand the U-Bahn and phase out the tram. Hamburg was planning for cars, and buses were seen as the modern alternative. Line after line closed, and the rails were often just paved over. On 1 October 1978, the last tram ran from Rathausmarkt to Schnelsen while crowds lined the street to say goodbye.

To see how big the network was, I rebuilt it on today’s map. There was no single source with all the lines, so I combined old network maps from Wikipedia with the work of people who have spent years on this topic, like Horst Buchholz, who wrote a chronicle of every line on his website horstbu.de, and fredriks.de, which has collected the old routes and traces of the tram for more than two decades. The result is a map of the old tram network along with the stations on top of today’s city.

Seeing all the lines together shows how far the tram reached: from Eidelstedt and Lurup in the west to Jenfeld and Billstedt in the east, south towards Harburg, and, of course, along the Fuhle.

The tram has been gone for almost 50 years, but once you know what to look for, you find it on walls, poles, pavements and buildings all over the city.

In narrow streets, there was no space for masts, so the overhead wire was anchored directly to the houses. For this, cast-iron rosettes were fixed to the façades: round, decorated wall plates that held the wire above the street. They were cheaper than masts, and the decoration made them look like part of the building rather than a piece of technical equipment.

While cycling around the city to hunt for rosettes, I quickly realised they are harder to spot than I expected. Some are plain and black, and once you know where to look, they are easy to find. You can find many of them along Ottenser Hauptstraße. Others are painted in the same colour as the façade and blend into its decoration, so you can pass them many times without noticing.

On Hein-Hoyer-Straße in St. Pauli, you can probably see the widest range on a single street, from very plain ones to really fancy ones. I still wonder why. Were the simple ones put up first and replaced over time, or did some building owners pay extra for a nicer design? What we do know is that Hamburg had several tram companies, five different rosette designs are documented, and later rosettes became simpler, until in the end a plain wall hook was enough.

In the old town, there’s one at the corner of Börsenbrücke and Trostbrücke, on the red brick building of the Patriotic Society. The building stands where Hamburg’s old City Hall was, until it was blown up during the Great Fire of 1842 to stop the flames from spreading. A few steps away, on Rathausmarkt, you can even see three pink/white rosettes on a single façade, right above the French café Bouillon.

The rosettes are not listed as monuments. Whether they stay is up to the building owners, and when a house is renovated, they can quietly disappear. A few of the ones I went looking for were already gone.

On wider streets, the wire hung from masts instead. When the trams were gone, many of these masts were simply kept and used for street lighting. The easiest way to recognise them is the lamps: instead of sitting on top of a pole, they hang on wires stretched across the road, exactly where the tram wire used to be.

Luruper Chaussee is a good place to see this, with a long row of old masts still carrying the lights. In the east, you can follow the former Line 8 along Straßburger Straße and the first part of Friedrich-Ebert-Damm by its masts. Further along Friedrich-Ebert-Damm, the city even put up new masts in the same design as the old ones, so not every tram mast you see there is actually from the tram era. Sometimes it is just a single mast left on its own, like the one in front of Horner Landstraße 194.

The most beautiful ones are on Königstraße in Altona. Opposite Lessers Passage, five richly decorated cast-iron masts from 1896 stand between the street trees. They belonged to Altona’s own tram company, they are the last of their kind in Hamburg, and unlike the rosettes, they are protected monuments.

When a line closed, the rails were usually not taken out. To save money, they were just covered with asphalt, and many of them are still under Hamburg’s streets. Every now and then, road works bring them back to the surface, as happened on Rathausstraße next to City Hall in 2023 and on Max-Brauer-Allee in 2024.

In a few places, you can see real rails without any digging. On Straßburger Straße in Dulsberg, a stretch of track is still visible, and in front of Gymnasium Hochrad in Othmarschen, a few metres of rails filled with bitumen lie in the pedestrian crossing.

Even where the rails are gone, the cobblestones sometimes remember them. On Bei der Reitbahn in Ottensen, the stones that replaced the tracks were laid in a slightly different pattern, so you can still follow the curve of the old line in the street.

I find it fascinating to follow these lines, because it makes me imagine how these streets would look with a tram running through them: probably busier with people, and with fewer cars.

When I first came to Hamburg for an internship, I often took bus line 5, and I was surprised that it drove in its own lanes in the middle of the street. Only when I learnt about the tram did it start to make sense.

After Line 2 closed in 1978, the tracks between Grindelberg, Hoheluftchaussee and Lokstedter Steindamm were removed, and bus lanes were laid in the old track bed in the middle of the road. So along this stretch, the 5 follows the path of Hamburg’s last tram. At some junctions, the bus lanes also follow the wide curves of the old tracks, which were designed for trams, not buses.

The oldest trace of all is in Wandsbek. The depot on Wendemuthstraße was built in 1866 for the very first horse tram, with stables for the horses. Today, it is a bus depot.

Many of the later depots found new lives as shops. The depot in Lokstedt is now a hardware store, the one at Krohnskamp in Winterhude a supermarket, and the old depot on Max-Brauer-Allee in Altona is a supermarket too. Near Ohlsdorf Cemetery, the small terminus building where the trams once turned is now a private art studio.

The biggest site was the Falkenried works in Hoheluft, where most of Hamburg’s trams were built. The workshops closed in 1999, and the area was turned into a residential neighbourhood that kept parts of the old industrial halls. Two of its streets are called Straßenbahnring and Straßenbahnstieg.

Some of the trams themselves have survived too, often in unexpected places. One stands at the hardware store in Lokstedt, another in front of the supermarket at Krohnskamp. If you want to see Hamburg trams in action, the museum at Schönberger Strand near Kiel has the largest collection, and some of them still run on its museum line.

A few travelled much further. Hamburg trams ended up in museums in Denmark and even in the USA: car 2710, built at Falkenried in 1921, now stands in the Seashore Trolley Museum in Maine.

Since 1978, the tram has almost returned twice, and both times politics decided its fate. In 1989, the SPD and the Greens started planning a modern Stadtbahn, but after they lost the 2001 election, the new CDU-led government stopped it. In 2008, the CDU and the Greens brought the idea back, until the Greens left the coalition in 2010 and the plans were put on hold again. A few months later, the SPD won the election with a promise to stop the Stadtbahn, and in 2011 it was officially cancelled.

Today, Hamburg is building the U5 instead, a new U-Bahn line from Lurup to Osdorf, planned to be finished by 2040 at an estimated cost of up to 16,5 billion euros. That keeps the tram in the debate: a recent study suggests a tram network could cover much of the same route for around 1,5 billion euros, while the Hochbahn argues a tram could never carry as many passengers.

Personally, I have a love-hate relationship with trams. I really like them as a form of public transport, especially compared to buses. On the other hand, the wires and the noise feel like a kind of pollution of their own. I can still hear the trams in Lisbon; they were so loud. Buses are slowly becoming electric, but I still don’t find riding a bus a delightful experience.

While writing this, I was curious whether trams have moved on, and it turns out they have. In Nanjing, battery-powered trams run without overhead wires on most of their lines, and Birmingham chose battery trams so it wouldn’t have to fix cables to its historic Town Hall, a modern answer to the same problem Hamburg once solved with rosettes.

If Hamburg ever brings the tram back, I hope it will be a quiet one without wires, maybe even floating silently like a maglev train. Until then, I’ll keep looking up at the rosettes and down at the cobblestones, imagining the network that once ran through the city.

#4131. A simple liquid can stop cavities without drilling, major study confirms

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A cavity doesn't always have to mean drilling, injections, or an uncomfortable visit to the dentist. A major clinical trial has confirmed that a simple, inexpensive liquid can stop tooth decay in young children, potentially preventing painful infections and the need for dental surgery.

The treatment, called silver diamine fluoride (SDF), takes only seconds to apply to each affected tooth. Rather than removing damaged tooth material and placing a filling, a dentist uses a tiny sponge tipped applicator to paint the liquid directly onto the cavity. The process requires no drilling, injections, or sedation.

The findings could have enormous implications for childhood dental care. Every year, thousands of young children in the United States visit emergency departments because of untreated cavities. Emergency physicians generally cannot provide the dental procedures needed to address the underlying problem, leaving some children with ongoing pain and infections. Others ultimately need dental surgery under general anesthesia, which makes them completely unconscious during the procedure.

Now, researchers led by the University of Michigan have produced compelling evidence that a much simpler approach can stop many cavities from getting worse.

Major Clinical Trial Confirms Silver Diamine Fluoride Works

Published in JAMA Pediatrics, the Phase III clinical trial included 830 children younger than 6. Participants were recruited through dental clinics, pediatric medical practices, and Head Start and Early Head Start programs in Michigan, New York, and Iowa.

Head Start and Early Head Start are federally supported programs that provide early childhood education and related services to eligible families.

The results showed that a solution containing 38% silver diamine fluoride successfully stopped decay in more than half of the affected baby teeth when treatment was provided at six month intervals.

Dentists refer to this as arresting tooth decay, meaning the damaged area stops deteriorating. Unlike a traditional filling, the treatment does not replace lost tooth structure. Instead, it helps prevent existing decay from progressing.

SDF works through the combined effects of silver and fluoride. Silver helps combat bacteria associated with tooth decay, while fluoride helps strengthen the mineral structure of teeth and resist further damage.

"This is a very effective and safe treatment -- even in children as young as 1," said Margherita Fontana, professor of dentistry at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry and the study's lead investigator.

A Simple Treatment Could Help Millions Avoid Dental Pain

Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease among children, affecting more than 40% of American children. Although cavities may initially cause few noticeable symptoms, untreated decay can eventually lead to severe toothaches, infections, problems eating and sleeping, missed school days, and repeated medical appointments.

For young children, conventional dental treatment can also be particularly challenging. Some are too young to remain still throughout a procedure, while others experience significant fear or anxiety.

SDF offers an alternative for many of these patients because it can be applied quickly without the equipment and procedures required for a traditional filling.

Fontana said the benefits could extend well beyond young children. Older adults, people with developmental or physical disabilities, individuals with severe dental anxiety, and patients who have difficulty obtaining conventional dental care could also benefit.

There is, however, one important tradeoff. SDF permanently turns the decayed portion of a treated tooth dark, which can be especially noticeable on front teeth. The discoloration is primarily cosmetic, but it may influence whether patients and families choose the treatment.

For many people, avoiding pain, drilling, or more invasive procedures may outweigh that drawback.

Why This Decades-Old Treatment Could Soon Reach More Patients

Silver diamine fluoride is not a newly invented substance. Dentists have successfully used it to manage cavities in numerous countries for decades.

In the United States, the FDA approved SDF as a medical device for treating tooth sensitivity in 2014. Dentists have also used it off-label to stop cavities, meaning they apply an authorized product for a purpose beyond its original approved indication.

What has been missing is large-scale clinical evidence from U.S. populations establishing its safety and effectiveness for treating tooth decay, a key step toward obtaining FDA approval for that specific use.

The University of Michigan trial was designed to help fill that gap.

"If we want more children and families to benefit from this treatment, we need rigorous evidence showing both that it works and that it's safe. From a public health perspective, if we want broader implementation across the United States, including in medical settings, we need carefully collected data in U.S. populations, and we now have that," Fontana said.

The investigation began in 2018 and continued despite the major disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.

The researchers believe stronger clinical evidence could make SDF more widely available, including in pediatric medical settings where children often receive care long before their first dental appointment.

"In medicine, clinicians want high-quality evidence before changing practice," Fontana said. "It is important to have data they can refer to because young children often see pediatricians years before they ever visit a dentist, broader acceptance could allow many more cavities to be treated while a referral to a dental home is successful, and before they become painful, infected or require surgery."

A dental home refers to an ongoing relationship with a dental care provider who can deliver preventive care, treatment, and follow-up services.

Expanding access to SDF could allow more children to receive early intervention before relatively manageable cavities develop into serious dental problems.

FDA Approval Could Expand Access to Cavity Treatment

The trial brought together researchers from the University of Michigan, New York University, the University of Iowa, and Indiana University. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, part of the National Institutes of Health, provided more than $12 million in funding.

The product tested was Advantage Arrest 38% SDF, supplied by Elevate Oral Care.

The findings provide clinical trial evidence that the manufacturer can use in an application seeking FDA approval of SDF as a drug for arresting dental caries, the medical term for tooth decay.

Amr Moursi, a professor of pediatric dentistry at New York University College of Dentistry and a co-principal investigator, emphasized the potential importance of that regulatory change.

"Our results support FDA approval of SDF for managing arrest of tooth decay in young children. Removing SDF from off-label status would be an important innovation which could lead to increased utilization by providers, enhanced payments by insurers and more consistent product quality," said Moursi, a co-principal investigator on the study.

For some children, receiving another application of SDF every few months could be sufficient to keep a cavity under control until the affected baby tooth naturally falls out.

Adults could also benefit, either by using SDF to manage decay over a longer period or as a temporary solution until a filling or another restorative procedure becomes affordable or practical.

The treatment is not suitable for every cavity, and stopping decay does not necessarily eliminate the need for future dental work. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that a procedure requiring only seconds to perform could help many patients avoid more complicated treatment.

"For almost anyone, this can arrest the decay and stop the infection and the pain it causes," Fontana said. "This could benefit many people."

Ex-Premier League star Jermaine Pennant was left needing 50 stitches on a leg wound after he was viciously mauled by a dog.

The former Liverpool midfielder, 43, had been walking his own dog - an American Akita named King - when he was pounced on by a German Shepherd.

Pennant said it came after he 'stepped in to protect' King when the other dog 'had a go at him', only for the footballer to eventually be attacked himself.

He told The Sun: 'It was really scary. The other dog had a go at King, so I stepped in to protect him.

'The next thing I know, it was attacking me. I was on the floor and it was on my leg for at least five seconds.

'I felt this huge pressure. Imagine the force of a dog bite. King jumped in to protect me.'

Gory images showed the extent of Pennant's injuries, with his right shin left bloodied and scarred in the aftermath of the incident.

The former Stoke City star was treated for four bite wounds on his leg and told he may need surgery for one larger one.

The ex-Liverpool midfielder was attacked by a German Shepherd while walking his American Akita, named King

Pennant later thanked his dog for 'saving his life' in a heartfelt Instagram post

'My hero still protecting me and trying to look after me, KING you’re a legend love you, man’s best friend, thanks for taking care of us both.'

#2546. Why Michael Carrick must be sacked by Man United and why Jim Ratcliffe and INEOS must be held accountable

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Lewis Nolan | 26 Oct 2026 02:27

Manchester United manager Michael Carrick is on the verge of being sacked, and he can have few complaints about the impending decision.

The Red Devils are widely expected to announce the departure of the head coach following the team's 3-0 Premier League defeat against Bournemouth on Sunday.

Carrick is set to become the seventh boss dismissed by the club since the retirement of Sir Alex Ferguson in 2013, and supporters are disillusioned by the direction of the team under the guidance of co-owners INEOS, who are led by Sir Jim Ratcliffe.

The billionaire promised a new era for United, but his three-year tenure has been disastrous, and there is an argument that he has been more influential in the downfall of the Red Devils than any manager.

Here, Sports Mole explains why Carrick must leave, but also why Ratcliffe and INEOS deserve more blame for the club's malaise.

The Red Devils are currently 16th in the Premier League with seven points, but they are fortunate to have as many points as they do given they have not played well in any top-flight game so far.

United's only victories in any competition this season have come against Ipswich Town in the league, as well as against Sabah FK in the Champions League, but both teams had opportunities.

One of the most concerning aspects of the team's displays this term has been their lack of intensity, though perhaps fans should have expected their players to be outfought after Youri Tielemans remarked that Carrick's pre-season programme was focused primarily on working with the ball.

The Premier League is home to arguably football's best athletes, and failure to prepare his squad physically for the campaign may prove to be costly for the head coach.

United's inability to compete out of possession was exposed against Hull City in matchweek one, though there were also concerns about the club during Carrick's interim spell as boss in 2025-26 despite the fact he guided the team to third place.

The Red Devils struggled in many areas of the pitch last campaign, only for the likes of Casemiro to rescue points from set pieces.

United have failed to score from any dead-ball situation in the 2026-27 Premier League, so it is no surprise that they have routinely dropped points.

Additionally, the critical comments of captain Bruno Fernandes regarding Carrick's tactical choices following the team's 4-1 defeat against Como in the Champions League on October 21 revealed that there was a rift in the squad.

Once a manager loses the faith of his players, irrespective of whether they are justified in their position, it becomes nearly impossible to restore trust, making remaining in charge untenable.

When Sir Jim Ratcliffe officially became co-owner of United through INEOS in December 2023, he was broadly welcomed by the fanbase because of the Glazer family's neglect.

The team's principle owners were often criticised for their failure to adequately back managers, their poor transfer policy and lack of direction, especially once they had sacked Sir Alex Ferguson's anointed successor David Moyes in 2014.

Louis van Gaal was seen as a disciple of Johan Cruyff, but his possession-oriented playstyle was then abandoned for the pragmatist Jose Mourinho, only for the current Real Madrid boss to be ousted for the familiar face of Ole Gunnar Solskjaer.

The former player Red Devils star was sacked in 2021 and eventually replaced by Erik ten Hag, who was seen as a disciple of Pep Guardiola, and there were hopes that he would bring the total football philosophy so popular in the Netherlands to Old Trafford.

His time in England was turbulent despite winning the EFL Cup in 2022-23, and INEOS had reportedly decided to part ways with him at the end of 2023-24, but the club's FA Cup success was said to have changed their mind.

United then predictably sacked Ten Hag in October of the following campaign, with disciplinarian Ruben Amorim appointed in November, though he was thought to have been reluctant about taking over mid-season.

INEOS and Ratcliffe saw Amorim guide the club to 15th in 2024-25 and lose the Europa League final against Tottenham Hotspur, yet they decided against firing him at the end of the season.

However, the Portuguese would then be dismissed in January 2026, before another former player in Carrick was given the reigns.

INEOS did not have to appoint the Englishman permanently at the end of 2025-26, and they could have instead recognised the worrying underlying numbers from his spell in the dugout, but they made yet another mistake by sanctioning significant expenditure on the head coach.

Ratcliffe has expressed the importance of being shrewd, even going as far as to make hundreds of employees at United redundant, but the club have wasted millions with their foolish managerial decisions.

The truth may be that none of those managers were good enough for United, but each of them should have been able to leave behind strong foundations for their successors to inherit.

Carrick's successor will inherit a broken squad filled with players that are either not good enough or disciplined enough, while they will also have to wrestle with a seemingly restricted transfer budget.

To make matters worse, the Old Trafford crowd has never been so disillusioned, and it may prove impossible to get them back onside under the current ownership.

Ratcliffe promised to restore United to their place at the pinnacle of English football, but the controversial billionaire has failed at every turn.
