#3310. Silicon Valley AI political groups turn on each other in the 2026 election

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Silicon Valley's bid to dominate artificial intelligence politics has devolved into a fight with itself.

With one week until the midterm election, political groups favoring faster AI development and lighter, more uniform rules have spent roughly $8 million in general-election congressional races, besting groups pushing for stronger safeguards and more room for states to regulate that have spent about $6.9 million, according to a CNBC analysis of Federal Election Commission filings through Oct. 20.

In Florida's 25th Congressional District, that split has produced a direct face-off. During the primary, Think Big, an affiliate of Leading the Future, the main super PAC network pushing faster AI development and lighter regulation, spent $310,000 supporting Democrat Jared Moskowitz, whose district changed after Florida redrew its congressional map ahead of the 2026 election. On the other side, Defending Our Values, a super PAC favoring stricter safety guardrails and state-level authority, dropped almost $857,000 to boost Republican Scott Singer and an additional $740,000 to keep one of his primary opponents from being elected.

Now, Moskowitz and Singer face off in the general election.

It's a microcosm of the breakdown in Silicon Valley's bid to build a unified AI political machine. Both sides favor AI innovation and agree on the need for guardrails, but they remain locked in a costly tug-of-war over which candidates to back — and which will best align with their preferred deployment speed, regulatory reach and balance of state power.

Electing more allies could give either camp an edge in shaping AI policy in the next Congress, even as the feud risks weakening Silicon Valley's clout.

"Everyone supports some form of regulation. It's just a debate about how much," said Bruce Freed, president and co-founder of the nonpartisan Center for Political Accountability and a former chief investigator for the Senate banking committee. "Money is flowing in all directions to be in the driver's seat of what happens."

CNBC identified federal committees aligned with each camp and totaled their FEC-reported independent expenditures supporting or opposing congressional candidates. The analysis excludes state races and nonprofit advocacy spending.

This was not the plan. The model was cryptocurrency.

Fairshake and its affiliated pro-crypto super PACs, backed by Coinbase, Ripple and Andreessen Horowitz, spent more than $130 million during the 2024 election cycle, according to FEC filings. Candidates it supported won 53 of 58 races, a 91% success rate, according to crypto publication CoinDesk.

AI investors took note. In August 2025, Leading the Future launched with more than $100 million in commitments from tech heavyweights including Andreessen Horowitz, OpenAI President Greg Brockman and his wife, Anna, and Perplexity and Palantir co-founder Joe Lonsdale.

Co-led by Josh Vlasto, who worked for Fairshake last election cycle, the network deployed a Democratic PAC, Think Big, and a Republican PAC, American Mission, to support candidates aligned with faster AI development and opposition to a patchwork of state regulations.

The message was clear: Back AI's agenda or risk millions being spent against you.

"The AI industry announced last year that they were going to rerun the crypto industry playbook by coming in swinging with oodles of money in order to bully lawmakers into submission," said Sacha Haworth, founder and executive director of the Tech Oversight Project, a nonprofit watchdog that advocates for stronger regulation of Big Tech and AI.

At first, it worked. In the cycle's first major primaries in March, three candidates heavily backed by American Mission — Jessica Steinmann in Texas, Chris Gober in Texas and Laurie Buckhout in North Carolina — won their Republican contests after the group spent more than $500,000 supporting each, according to FEC filings.

But a rival infrastructure was already taking shape. Anthropic committed $20 million to Public First Action in February and another $20 million in July, giving the safeguards camp a major financial backer. Anthropic says its money is restricted to policy and public education and cannot fund candidate elections. Still, separately funded super PACs tied to Public First Action have spent millions advancing similar priorities.

The clash burst into view in New York’s 12th District Democratic primary. Think Big spent $8.3 million, according to FEC filings, trying to defeat state Assemblymember Alex Bores, who authored major state AI safety legislation. The Anthropic-backed Jobs and Democracy PAC countered with about $15 million to support him, while allied safety groups spent millions more, according CNBC's analysis.

Think Big and Jobs and Democracy spent a combined $23.3 million, about 40% of the two camps' primary spending nationwide, according to the analysis.

Bores ultimately lost to Micah Lasher, 39% to 35%, giving Think Big a tactical victory.

"They helped light a flame under the backlash that's now fueling so much of the midterm elections," Haworth said. "The race showed that spending against safety-first candidates could now trigger a counterattack from within the industry itself."

The fight has carried into the general election, but the pace of spending plummeted.

Safeguards-focused groups have spent $825,000 this fall opposing two Republicans who benefited from Leading the Future-aligned spending during the primaries, according to CNBC's analysis of FEC filings. The two races alone account for more than one-sixth of the safeguards camp's general-election opposition spending.

Guardrails Alliance reported spending $412,500 apiece, according to FEC filings, against Republican House candidates Jay Feely in Arizona and Aaron Flint in Montana, both of whom benefited from Leading the Future-aligned spending in their primaries. It also reported about $412,000 against Democratic Rep. Jimmy Gomez in California, whom Leading the Future endorsed but did not report spending to support, according to CNBC's analysis.

"The big AI groups still believe that their money can help influence elections even if it's just a couple in November," Haworth said.

Yet the overall flow of AI campaign money has slowed since the primaries.

Leading the Future's network reported roughly $26 million in primary independent expenditures, compared with about $8 million in general-election races. Safeguards-focused groups reported about $32.1 million in primary spending and $6.9 million in general-election contests. The groups reported another roughly $5.5 million in runoff and special-election spending.

Combined, about 74% of the roughly $78.5 million they have spent on federal candidates this cycle went toward primary races, according to CNBC's analysis.

Some of that decline reflects the smaller universe of competitive November races. But the slowdown has coincided with AI becoming a more difficult political sell.

A new CNBC All-America Economic Survey found 52% of registered voters said companies should pause development of advanced AI until Congress passes regulations, compared with 32% who favored moving ahead. Sixty-three percent said they would oppose an AI data center within 10 miles of their home.

“When evaluating risk versus reward right now, the backlash is just too big,” said Freed, of the Center for Political Accountability.

“AI executives and groups spent heavily in the primaries, so they likely achieved what they set out to do in most races. At this point, there's no real downside to scaling back,” Freed said.

The Brockmans gave $25 million to Leading the Future. But amid objections from OpenAI employees to the group's tactics, OpenAI's chief strategy officer acknowledged internally that the company was taking "reputational hits," according to Semafor.

The Brockmans later abandoned plans for another $25 million contribution, according to The New York Times.

Meta has retreated as well. Political groups it funded returned $25 million this summer — $22 million from the AI-promoting American Technology Excellence Project and $3 million from its California operation — according to IRS and state filings. The refunds cut its planned $65 million state-level political push by nearly 40%, to about $40 million. The Times first reported the refunds.

"Something's clearly not working," OpenAI CEO Sam Altman told Politico earlier this month. "I don't think spending money on politics is what's going to make people feel better about it."

Technologies developed and tested in orbit to remove space junk and fix broken satellites can be speedily repurposed to build fleets of bodyguard spacecraft to protect important satellites from adversary attacks, space clean-up company Astroscale thinks.

Astroscale made history with its 2024 up-close in-orbit inspection of a discarded Japanese rocket stage during the pioneering ADRAS-J mission in low Earth orbit (LEO), the region of space below altitudes of 1,200 miles (2,000 kilometers).

The firm's ELSA-D mission, which ran from 2021 to 2024, was the first commercial endeavor to demonstrate orbital capture technologies in LEO. Now, this technology can come in handy as Western space assets face increasing threats from Russia.

Speaking on the sidelines of an event in London earlier this month, Alex Godfrey, Astroscale's head of defense, said the company could have a protector spacecraft ready for launch in under three years.

"In in-orbit servicing, we have to achieve very high levels of agility to be able to dock and close to a distance of zero meters to refuel something or remove it from orbit," Godfrey told Space.com. "With space control, you're rarely getting quite as close, so in some ways it's simpler because you are further away."

Space control — a military term for ensuring the space around Earth remains orderly and peaceful — is quickly becoming a hot talking point. Godfrey's remarks came just as the world witnessed in shock as Russia made close-up maneuvers around a radar satellite owned by Finland headquartered Earth-observation company ICEYE. The October incident, described by experts as the first rendezvous of two active satellites in low Earth orbit conducted without the consent of the target, raised concerns Russia may be preparing to damage the satellite, which is part of a fleet providing critical intelligence to Ukraine.

U.S. Under Secretary of State Thomas G. DiNanno described Russia's actions on X as "threatening behavior" and "consistent with on-orbit weapons operations."

The ICEYE incident is only the latest in a long string of activities conducted by Russia in space designed either to eavesdrop on or interfere with the activity of critical Western space assets. The Luch-1 and Luch-2 satellites have been stalking Europe's and America's communications satellites in geostationary orbit — the busy region 22,000 miles (36,000 km) above the planet where satellites have a fixed view of a portion of the globe—since 2014. Germany's military space command chief Major General Michael Traut admitted earlier this year that the Luch spacecraft have been spying on European strategic communications in space for years.

A bodyguard satellite would make sure no such intruder gets dangerously close. Godfrey said that the cost of a bodyguard mission would be on the order of a single percent of the value of a military-grade geostationary communications constellation such as the U.K.'s £8.4-billion Skynet fleet.

"To build a bodyguard satellite is absolutely within the capability and the budget of a single country like the U.K.," said Godfrey. "It would be similar to a carrier strike group where you have a [naval aircraft] carrier, but that carrier never leaves the port on its own. It has an escort of much smaller, lower-cost systems that move around to protect it."

Although Astroscale, headquartered in Japan but with significant presence in the U.K., is a leader in the commercial orbital rendezvous and proximity operations technology, no Western players currently match the deployed capabilities of Russia and China. Europe, in particular, is under-equipped for defense and military operations in space, according to the Secure World Foundation's Global Counterspace Capabilities Report. With Russia's accelerating orbital forays, the clock appears to be ticking.

Godfrey proposes a pan-European fleet of orbital bodyguards patrolling clusters of European assets to ward off future Russian attempts to jam or damage the satellites.

"The UK, for example, has multiple assets in different positions around the geostationary ring," Godfrey said. "That is difficult to protect with a single spacecraft. So what I encourage nations to think about is how to form a geo-patrol force through collaboration. If France has assets in a similar location to the UK and maybe Germany, then it makes sense for one spacecraft to protect two or three allied spacecraft, and then, in another region, another country takes responsibility for the protection."

Nick Shave, the UK managing director at Astroscale, told Space.com that the company sees interest in its technologies and is "in discussions" with "companies and customers across Europe" although it has not signed any contracts to develop bodyguard satellites.

"We have spent a good amount of time talking over the last few years, but now we have to go and really deliver," Shave said. "The UK government is promising funding for space control development from 2030 onwards. So what needs to happen now is really preparing the ground so that we've got the right capabilities, the right investment and the right demonstrator capability, by 2030."

In the meantime, Russia's COSMOS 2612 stalker satellite keeps lingering 298 miles (480 km) from the ICEYE intelligence satellite in LEO, keeping the Western space community on edge.

#3869. Matthew Perry Was in AA Meetings 3 Times a Day While Making Friends

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In the Netflix docuseries The One About Matthew Perry, friends and family unpack the actor's legacy—and just how much he struggled with substance abuse, including at the height of his sitcom success.

Matthew Perry's former roommate remembers the one about the Friends cast making history.

"When I moved in, they were in negotiations," pal Tim Harrington, who met the actor at a recovery meeting in 2001, shares in the new Netflix docuseries The One About Matthew Perry, now streaming. "One day, I'm sitting on the bed, and he runs from his bedroom, which is directly across the hall, and he jumps on the bed and he screams, 'I just made $26 million!' or something like that."

Mind you, this wasn't two guys sharing a struggling actor's apartment.

Harrington recalls being "hopeless" when he started hanging out with the Whole Nine Yards star. They were at a California Pizza Kitchen when he told Perry he didn't think he could go on without another drink, that his roommate was using and he was considering going to live in his car.

And Perry, who died in 2023 at the age of 54 from acute effects of ketamine use, "saved my life, right there in that moment," Harrington says, by inviting him to move into his mansion in the hills.

Hence the houseguest being around when Perry, Jennifer Aniston, Courteney Cox, Lisa Kudrow, Matt LeBlanc and David Schwimmer scored their $1 million per episode deal in February 2002 after several months of negotiations.

"He was super, super excited that they had actually pulled it off," Harrington recalls.

He says he lived with Perry for about a year, during which they became near constant companions, including making time every day for multiple Alcoholics Anonymous meetings.

"He was very happy, but he was still struggling with his own stuff," Harrington says. "We'd go to three meetings a day mostly. We'd go every morning at 7:30 to West Hollywood, to the Log Cabin, a famous AA meeting."

During the drive, their go-to jam was "Drops of Jupiter" by Train, Harrington noting they'd "just play it 100 times on the way to a meeting."

"Then we went to breakfast," Harrington continues, "and then we went to the noon meeting—and then we'd discuss after that meeting what our next meeting would be."

Perry detailed how his heady rise to the peak of TV stardom was interwoven with substance abuse and trips to rehab—he first checked into treatment for a Vicodin addiction in 1997 after wrapping Friends' third season—in his raw 2022 memoir Friends, Lovers and the Big Terrible Thing, which was published just a year before he died.

As he described in the book, "Being on Friends was one of those unicorn situations where the news just kept getting better and better. But off-screen, things weren’t going so well."

Perry wrote that, after kicking his pill habit in 1997, he stayed sober for 68 days before having a drink. After which, he "drank every night until 2001."

After another stay in rehab—which is where Perry was when "The One With Monica and Chandler's Wedding" aired on May 17, 2001—he met Harrington at a meeting.

In his quest to stay sober, Perry wrote that he had spent more than $7 million, attended 6,000 AA meetings, been in rehab at least 15 times and had gone to therapy twice a week for 30 years.

"Going to meetings," Harrington explains, "it was about putting out of our minds the pain and discomfort and the struggle. And when it got quiet, that stuff would rise to the top."

Sharing a house, he and Perry were "together all the time." Then, one day, "all of a sudden," Perry told Harrington he had to move out.

He admits, "It felt like a breakup. The hanging out didn't stop, but it was just different. It raised a red flag that [Perry] may be planning for a relapse."

And "there was a resumption of use at some point," Harrington continues. "It was pretty bad. And once he was stabilized, it was wanting to necessarily not dwell on it. Like, move forward. Like, 'What's next?'"

In his book, Perry recalled spending the summer of 2002 "getting sober and playing a lot of tennis," after which season nine—which ran from September 2002 to May 2003—was "the only year I was completely sober for a Friends season."

He noted that, during the periods he was sober, "I’d think back to the recent past and wonder why I’d ever picked up pills or drugs after getting clean."

In Matthew Perry's memoir Friends, Lovers and the Big, Terrible Thing, the Friends star revealed just how close he came to dying in 2018. While staying in a sober living home in Southern California, his colon "exploded" from years of opioid overuse. The actor ended up spending two weeks in a coma and five months in a hospital. Perry also had to use a colostomy bag for nine months, which he noted broke at least 55 times. ("Dear Colostomy Bag People: make a bag that doesn't break, you f--king morons," he joked. "Did I make you laugh on Friends? If so, don't put shit all over my face.")

"I had been on opiates, and off opiates, and back on different opiates for so long that I suffered from a situation that only a subset of the population gets," Perry wrote of his organ exploding, sharing he had 14 follow-up surgeries after the initial seven-hour emergency procedure. "Opiates cause constipation. It's kind of poetic. I was so full of shit it almost killed me."

As he was in surgery, Perry revealed his family and friends who arrived at the hospital were told, "'Matthew has a two percent chance of making it through the night.' I will have to live out the rest of my days knowing that my mother and others heard those words."

At the time the book was published, Perry had been sober for 18 months."Sobriety had now become the most important thing in my life," Perry wrote. "Because I learned that if you put anything in front of sobriety, you will lose that 'anything' anyway if you drink."In the book, Perry shared that he viewed alcohol as his "best friend," admitting, "I would have gone crazy without it...Learning to move forward in life without it was tantamount to asking someone to go about his or her day without breathing." And, after suffering an injury on the set of Fools Rush In, Perry developed an addiction to Vicodin and was taking 55 pills a day before checking into rehab for the first time in 1997.

While filming Friends in 2000, Perry spent 30 days in the hospital being "fed fluids through an IV" after he was told his excessive drinking was the cause of his pancreatitis.

"'F--k that,' I said," Perry recalled telling the doctor. "No. I don't drink too much.' Pancreatitis at the age of thirty was unheard of. Yay for me! Another record."

While recovering in the hospital, Perry was given Dilaudid, an opioid that changes the brain's relationship to pain. "It was my new favorite drug," he admitted, "and I would have stayed in that hospital for a hundred days if they kept administrating it."

Three years before they were cast in Friends, Perry and Jennifer Aniston met through mutual acquaintances and he was "immediately taken by" her. Believing the attraction was mutual, Perry asked Aniston out, an offer she declined. "But she said she'd love to be friends with me," Perry recalled. "And I compounded the compound by blurting, 'We can't be friends!'"

While he admitted he was "still attracted" to her when the Friends cast assembled for the first time, Perry wrote, "We were able to sail right past the past and focus on the fact  that we had both gotten the best job Hollywood had to offer." (Though he later admitted he was "crushing badly" on Aniston early on in the show's run, but her "deafening lack of interest" eventually brought it to an end.)

As for his first impressions for the rest of his co-stars, Perry described Courteney Cox as "cripplingly beautiful" and Lisa Kudrow as "just as gorgeous and hilarious and incredibly smart" as a mutual friend had said she would be. Matt LeBlanc was "nice and a cool customer," while David Schwimmer was "incredibly funny."

Perry said the six actors formed a close bond, writing, "From that first morning we were inseparable."

Perry shared that he temporarily lost a friend after he was cast on the NBC hit, having landed the part after close pal Craig Bierko choose to star in another pilot rather than play Chandler. The decision that led Bierko to not speak to Perry for years. In the plus column, though, the series saved him.

"It is not out of the realms of possibility that I may have ended up on the streets of downtown LA shooting heroin in my arm until my untimely death," he wrote, adding that it was "such a good and fun job" that it was worth remaining sober—"for awhile, at least."

"I was the second baseman for the New York Yankees. I couldn't f--k that up," he reasoned. "I would never forgive myself. When you're earning $1 million a week, you can't afford to have the seventeenth drink."

But it would be his friends on Friends that would help to once save his life a second time during the show's run, most notably Aniston, who confronted Perry about his drinking.

"'We can smell it,' she said, in a kind of weird but loving way, and the plural 'we' hit me like a sledgehammer," Perry wrote of the conversation, which he described as "devastating" and said led him to hire a sober companion at work (though he later revealed that the ninth season was the only one he was wholly sober for).

In the book, Perry revealed he was able to briefly leave his treatment center in Malibu to film Chandler and Monica's wedding in season seven."When I arrived, Jen Aniston said, 'I've been mad at you,'" Perry recalled. "'Honey,' I said, 'if you knew what I'd been through, you would notbe mad at me.'""With that we hugged, and I got the work done," he continued. "I married Monica and got driven back to the treatment center—at the height of my highest point in Friends, the highest point in my career, the iconic moment on the iconic show—in a pickup truck helmed by a sober technician."

His Shortlived Romance With Julia Roberts

Perry and Erin Brokovich star Julia Roberts were once an item in the '90s. At first blush, Kristin LaHargoue’s social media resembles that of many thirtysomething moms espousing aspirational lifestyles. To her 88,000-plus followers on Instagram, LaHargoue—a crunchy blonde living in central Utah—routinely posts her children, her husband, and herself traipsing around the globe on her account @veda.revival. In an April 2026 post soundtracked to a version of Taylor Swift’s “The Fate of Ophelia,” LaHargoue wrote that she and her family have thus far traveled to over 40 countries.

Flying around the world at such a clip isn’t unusual for influencers. But LaHargoue says her children, although born in the US, travel without birth certificates or Social Security numbers. Her thinking is aligned with the sovereign citizen movement: an ever-expanding group of anti-government activists that, for almost half a century, have operated on the belief that they aren’t subject to US law. “Hot take: You don’t actually need a birth certificate to raise a thriving, traveling, fully functional child,” she writes in the same post. “Yeah … I said it. Because we are living proof of it.” By virtue of not registering her two young children’s births, she says her sons are “sovereign babies”—an oft-used hashtag, on accounts such as LaHargoue’s, to describe a child bearing alternative identification documents.

With her brand Veda Revival, LaHargoue offers a white-glove concierge service to new and expecting parents, rooted in the belief that obtaining requisite birth documents is tantamount to entering one’s children into “the jurisdiction of the US corporation.” A larger sovereign citizen movement has existed in the US for decades, with its practitioners similarly eschewing traditional government documents; acts of protest can also include tax evasion, driving without a license, and in the case of Terry Nichols—domestic terrorism. LaHargoue, who says she has worked with over 1,000 parents to obtain passports for their sovereign babies, is one of a growing cohort of influencers working in the unregulated industry that’s now selling this self-taught knowledge. It’s impossible to quantify how many sovereign babies currently live in the US, given their parents’ feelings about record-taking. (According to one oft-cited estimate, 100,000 to 300,000 people identify as sovereign citizens, although it's hard to know exactly because of the nature of the movement.) But Adam Bojak, a family law attorney in western New York who is currently on the ballot for the New York state legislature, says that he’s seen an uptick in cases involving sovereign children. In decades past, he’d have maybe one client per year who’d vacate or even try to reject formal identification documents for their children; lately, that’s increased to a half dozen a year, a phenomenon he suspects is linked to the “psychic break” that happened during Covid. “So many of these things are downstream from the aftereffects of that,” he says. “Just where people were ripped apart socially and physically from each other, emotionally. [It’s a rupture] that leads into this idea that everybody’s out for themselves.”

Remote learning during the pandemic also transformed education. Homeschooling rates surged in the early 2020s and remain high: The National Home Education Research Institute estimated earlier this year that 3.4 million children in the US are homeschooled—a number that is almost certainly underreported—compared to 1.4 million back in 2003. Tess Ulrey, executive director of the Coalition for Responsible Home Education, is unsurprised that the sovereign baby movement has risen in tandem with homeschooling rates, as homeschooling can become an inadvertent gateway into sovereign thinking. Some parents might pull their child out of school and “get tied up in this ideology of like, ‘Well, it is my right to do whatever I want. Well, why would the government be tracking that?’” she says.

Indeed, peppered between posts name-checking Rumi and denouncing circumcision, LaHargoue encourages parents to reject conventional education systems. In her July appearance on the Born Free Method podcast, LaHargoue spoke about her belief that the US school system is connected to a larger government plot that, by virtue of the birth certificate, “contracts” children as human capital. “That school system is now teaching them how to be a good worker,” she said. “Let’s shuffle them right down this path.”

A recent rise in “world schooling,” or rooting a child’s instruction in travel rather than curricula, is similarly linked with the sovereign baby movement. Jemila Moore, a doula based in South Carolina, lives nomadically with her partner and four young children. Her three youngest kids have neither birth certificates nor Social Security numbers. Moore, who swears by world schooling, wanted to ensure that her children had the ability to travel with passports. Wherever her family may be, Moore accompanies her children to local public libraries to read and do math exercises. “There's nothing greater than children being able to learn about other countries while actually witnessing those countries and their culture, and let those countries tell their history versus just reading it in a textbook,” she says. “I think that it really makes a difference on our development as people and how we view the world.”

While sovereign babies are bred on the grounds of government suspicion, sovereign-minded parents still “want to travel under the banner of the United States of America without actually being subject to the United States of America,” says Bojak. “Like George Costanza said, ‘We’re living in a society,’ and these are the barriers you have to clear to live in a society.” Rob Finch, a North Carolina–based consultant who trains law enforcement officers to engage with sovereign citizens who don’t believe they are subject to the law, says this ideology is "not fact-based," adding: “You've got a lot of opportunistic people selling these packages of ‘Keep your kid off the grid, here's how you do it.’”

Without clearing these barriers, experts worry that sovereign babies are far more likely to fall through societal cracks and live insular lives. Most will be unable to drive, find jobs, or even secure housing without the crucial documents one needs to move through the world.

Birth certificates and Social Security numbers represent a relatively new standard for identification in the US. During the colonial period, American churches primarily kept birth records. But when millions of Europeans immigrated to the States in the 19th century, reformers became concerned about rising poverty rates, overpopulation, and the spread of epidemics in densely populated urban areas. In a bid to better understand public health, the US Census Bureau developed standardized certificates and began tracking births, though not every state followed suit. The process became more nationally standardized around the 1930s, and firmly de rigueur during World War II, when some factories would only employ US citizens as part of the war effort.

To some sovereign citizens, it’s no coincidence that birth certificates became rote in the 1930s, the same decade FDR signed an executive order replacing gold as the backing standard for US currency. Proponents of the “straw man theory,” a central premise in sovereign ideology, hold that when parents take out a birth certificate and Social Security number for their child, the government then opens an account in their name and uses them, in the absence of gold, as human capital to back US debt. This bogus theory is often invoked by sovereign citizens who, say, get pulled over for driving without a license, as a way of justifying their actions: They believe that every individual has two separate personas—a legal entity that is bound by the government, and themselves—and that laws thus don’t apply to them as individuals. To date, no US court has ever sided with sovereign citizens invoking these arguments, says family law attorney Christopher C. Melcher: “There's no legal basis for what they're saying, but they say it with enough conviction and consistency that they hope they can get away with it.”

The modern sovereign citizens movement has its roots in a hate-fueled paramilitary cohort called Posse Comitatus (Latin for “power of the county”). In the 1970s, an ex-Army lieutenant colonel turned “minister” named William Potter Gale founded the group, hinged on racist and antisemetic beliefs, in Southern California. Gale believed in a “citizen’s government” that would exact justice in its own way; throughout the decade, his followers violently detained and assaulted an IRS worker and attempted to “indict” a judge. When an agricultural crisis gripped the US, his conspiracy-driven ideas spread throughout the country. Posse Comitatus’ anti-government tactics, which include not paying taxes, later spread into a larger sovereign citizens’ movement, which now has dozens of different factions and chapters in various states. One such group is the American State Nationals, who align with sovereign citizen ideology but distance themselves from the moniker because of the movement’s white supremacist origins. ASNs believe one can obtain a noncitizen national passport that, by virtue of having a certain number of stars on it, gives them limited diplomatic immunity. Some even sell bundles on social media that allow people, for several hundred dollars, to exit their “contract” with the US government through a passport based on nationality rather than citizenship.

Beyond a skepticism of the US government, the parents of sovereign babies are difficult to pin down. They typically do not align with any one political party or religious group. While some live more holistic and off-grid lifestyles, and an overlap does exist between sovereignty practitioners and ardent anti-vaxers, it wouldn’t be accurate to say that this is a strain of the Make America Healthy Again movement, either. Rather than having one centralized leader or a physical community, the sovereign baby phenomenon is more akin to a vast constellation, people connected by the belief that by applying for a birth certificate and Social Security number, they are “binding” their children with the “United States corporation,” as LaHargoue likes to put it. In a series of videos included in Veda Revival’s “Born Free Blueprint,” LaHargoue argues that sovereign babies are “ushering in a new era of freedom.” (These videos are free, but before purchasing and viewing LaHargoue’s more in-depth guides, all clients are required to upload a passport/ID and sign an agreement accepting the terms of entering her member community—which threatens legal action if one does not use her information with “integrity and benevolence.”)

The US Department of State requires evidence of citizenship before approving a passport. If one doesn’t have a primary document (such as a birth certificate), the agency accepts several forms of secondary evidence, including a delayed birth certificate and a Letter of No Record—which must be issued by the state and include public record documents such as a hospital birth certificate, baptism records, school records, or a census record. Those hewing to sovereign thinking tend to go with another option: a family Bible containing written inscriptions denoting the births and deaths of relatives. Melcher says it’s difficult, but not impossible, for those born on US soil to obtain secondary evidence that’s “sufficient to convince the State Department to issue a passport, based on them claiming that they were born in a state or territory, without a birth certificate.”

She notarized each of these documents and sent them certified through the mail. After an extensive back and forth, she said the office eventually approved the passport. “I opted for the passport route because that is a way of announcing, ‘Hey, I have this child,’” she says. “I'm claiming parental rights and backing them with the paperwork they'll need to make whatever choices they want in the future.”

In recent years, parents have stumbled into the sovereign baby movement through online rabbit holes, especially on Instagram, Telegram, Facebook, and Reddit. The confluence of factors that has given way to the sovereign baby movement—including the ubiquity of influencers, the isolating aftermath of Covid, skepticism of the medical establishment, and waning trust in the government—feels utterly of this time. Yet this phenomenon clashes against another tension: the Trump administration’s brutality toward immigrants and visible show of deporting people out of the US. At a moment when undocumented immigrants, green card holders, asylum seekers, and citizens are being shuttled to remote countries with little legal recourse, it’s striking that parents of sovereign children—of babies born in the US—have consciously stacked the odds against themselves and their kids.
