Thomas Tuchel says he has “run out of superlatives” to describe Harry Kane after the England captain won the Ballon d’Or.

Bayern Munich striker Kane pipped Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe to football’s most prestigious individual prize at the London Palladium, rewarded for his staggering season in which he scored 73 goals for club and country.

The 33-year-old became the first Englishman to lift the trophy since Michael Owen in 2001.

Under Tuchel, he led his country to the last four of the World Cup, where they suffered a devastating loss to Argentina. England nevertheless achieved their best World Cup finish since 1966, coming home with bronze after victory over France in the third-place play-off.

Following Kane’s triumph, Tuchel said: “Congratulations Harry on a deserved Ballon D’Or!

“To all my England teammates, we had a special year,” he said. “It wasn't the perfect year, but we had a lot of good moments. We built a great bond together, a lot of togetherness. The way we trained, the way we played, the togetherness in the hotel in the World Cup.

#4893. Ripple wins final US approval for national trust bank

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The approval clears the way for Ripple to manage reserves backing its RLUSD stablecoin and provide digital asset custody.

Ripple announced Monday that its national trust bank has secured final approval from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC).

The approval completes the chartering process for Ripple National Trust Bank (RNTB), which received conditional approval from the OCC in December 2025.

The federally supervised trust bank is authorized to manage reserves backing Ripple USD (RLUSD), act as collateral trustee for the benefit of RLUSD holders and provide digital asset custody services, according to Ripple.

RLUSD will continue to be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company, Ripple’s limited purpose trust company regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services.

“We’ve always said crypto and banks work better together — this is what that looks like. Real innovation, real accountability, federal oversight,” Ripple CEO Brad Garlinghouse said on X on Monday.

Ripple follows Circle, which secured final OCC approval in July. Other crypto firms remain at the conditional approval stage, including Coinbase, which received its preliminary green light in April, and Crypto.com and Stripe-owned Bridge, which received conditional approvals in February.

A national trust bank is a federally chartered institution focused on holding and managing assets on behalf of clients. Unlike a typical commercial bank, most national trust banks do not accept deposits or make loans and do not carry Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation insurance, according to the OCC.

#1331. Harry Kane's Ballon d'Or triumph was vindication for a superstar brimming with quiet excellence who swerves flashy controversy, writes OLIVER HOLT - and a win for English football

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 00:01 GMT, 27 October 2026 | Updated: 10:01 GMT, 27 October 2026

It is July 10 2024, a couple of hours after goals from Harry Kane and Ollie Watkins have taken England past the Netherlands in Dortmund and into the final of Euro 2024. In the Dorint Hotel, the other side of a dual carriageway from the Westfalenstadion, a group of fans, some in England shirts, are sitting in the lobby bar.

They see a couple of us sitting by ourselves at another table and invite us over. It is Kane’s wife, Katie Goodland, and her family, her sister, her parents. They talk about Kane’s life in Munich and their lives in Essex, their favourite pubs, favourite places to eat. They talk a little about some of the criticism Kane is attracting but they hold no bitterness. They are lovely people. It is two hours well spent.

I thought of them when I got around to seeing Dear England, the play written by James Graham, which won rave reviews for its portrayal of Gareth Southgate as football’s answer to Jesus Christ but whose characterisation of Kane as a dolt and a bore was so crass and crudely drawn that it spoilt the play.

I thought of them when I saw Kane criticised for making the move from Spurs to Bayern Munich and then failing to win the Bundesliga in his first season. I thought of them when people laughed that he would never win a trophy.

I thought of them a few weeks ago when I read that Lamine Yamal had dismissed Kane’s claims to winning the Ballon d’Or, the sport’s most prestigious individual honour, and had fed into the star culture that pervades the game by saying the battle for it should be between him and Kylian Mbappe.

‘I honestly believe there are two of us who are the best in the world, and this year I’d deserve it because of what I’ve won,’ Yamal said in an interview clip shared by Spanish streaming platform Movistar. 'As far as I’m concerned, Mbappe and I are the two best in the world. It’s clear to me, and everyone who watches the matches knows it.’

Harry Kane's Ballon d'Or triumph is a success story for football's humble superstars

The Bayern Munich and England star is a model professional as well as a superb goal-scorer

Kane thanked his wife Kate, describing her as his 'best friend' and 'my biggest supporter'

But most of all, I thought of them late on Monday night when I sat in the audience at the London Palladium in the heart of London’s West End and watched Kane walk up to the stage to take that prize ahead of both Yamal and Mbappe and become the first Englishman for 25 years to be named the best footballer in the world.

Because that moment was Kane’s vindication. It was the moment that justified the way he has conducted himself throughout a career that started with him scrapping his way through loan spells at Leyton Orient, Millwall, Norwich City and Leicester City before he nailed down a place in the Spurs first team.

‘My journey has not been a straightforward one,’ Kane said on that stage in front of an audience of yelling kids and raucous fans. 'For any young boy wanting to achieve their dreams, and hoping to learn through resilience, determination and self-belief, I hope this can give you inspiration.'

It was a pitch-perfect speech that combined the emotion that spilled out when he thanked his parents and his wife and the care and the loyalty that came with a heartfelt thank you to Tottenham for ‘believing in me and my dreams.’

It was the moment that told him that he had been right to dedicate himself to the game completely and that all the years of being a fine role model to young players in the sport and to fans who idolised something they saw in him had been worth it.

It was the moment that said that football is capable of handing the highest honours to men like him, who have not sought the limelight or courted controversy or abused their fame or hired performers with dwarfism to decorate your 18th birthday party, as Yamal is alleged to have done, but have dedicated themselves to a career of quiet excellence.

Work hard in silence. Let success be your noise. 🏆

He received the award from Brazil icon Ronaldo after beating Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe

Kane, pictured embracing Women's Ballon d'Or winnger Alexia Putellas, justified the way he has conducted himself throughout his career by winning football's top individual prize

And it was the moment that told him that scoring a remarkable 61 goals for Bayern in a single season, leading England to the brink of a World Cup final and extending his astonishing goalscoring record with his country so that he now boasts 91 goals in 125 caps counts for what it should.

It is not a win for the Premier League because Kane plies his trade in the Bundesliga now but it is a win for English players. It asks more questions about Thomas Tuchel’s attempt to pin the blame on England’s World Cup exit on the poor technique of English players. In Kane and Jude Bellingham, England have players with Rolls-Royce technique.

And it is a win for English football because Kane is the ambassador for our game in the way Sir Bobby Charlton was the ambassador for our game. He is a player admired for more than just his football. He is admired for what he stands for on and off the pitch. He is a player who plays the game with respect and dignity as well as sublime skill.

‘The Ballon d’Or,’ its rules suggest, ‘is awarded based on three criteria, in order of importance: 1. Individual performances, decisive and impressive character. 2. Collective performances and titles won. 3. Class and fair play.’ Those criteria are rich justification for Kane’s win.

The truth is that for everything he has achieved, Kane has been underrated for much of his career. Because the truth is also that he is, alongside Jimmy Greaves, the greatest goalscorer England has ever produced but also a player who proves again and again that he is far more than a goalscorer.

Kane is the complete centre forward, a man who scores and scores and scores for Bayern, but whose passing ability and brilliant technique makes him stand out amid his competitors. He and Jude Bellingham drove England on and on at the World Cup in Mexico, Canada and the US last summer.

And now he is in rare company. He is the first Englishman to win the Ballon d’Or since Michael Owen in 2001. Only four of his compatriots, Sir Stanley Matthews (1956), Sir Bobby Charlton (1966), Kevin Keegan (1978 and 1979), and Owen, have been awarded the honour. Denis Law (1964) and George Best (1968), are the only other British players to have won.

Kane was warned when he left Spurs that the move would cost him his shot at immortality because it would end his chances of overhauling Alan Shearer’s mark of 260 goals that makes him the leading scorer in Premier League history.

The striker’s move to Bayern Munich from Tottenham in 2023 has paid off handsomely

Kane is the first Englishman to win the Ballon d’Or since Michael Owen (right) in 2001

Those warnings may have been right but think of what Kane has gained. Think of how much he has been rewarded for that move. Maybe the chance of the league record has gone but Kane’s name is now written in football history alongside Lionel Messi, Cristiano Ronaldo, Franz Beckenbauer, Johan Cruyff and Zinedine Zidane.

Kane’s victory is a victory for a player who has done things the right way, a player who is not a raging narcissist like Ronaldo, a player who is understated and unassuming, a player who is the ultimate professional, who has done everything to maximise his talents and devote himself to the game and who is now reaping the rewards.

It is a victory for Spurs, it is a victory for English football, which created and nurtured him. And it is a victory for his family, whose pride in their husband, son, father and brother is something we can all share.

#5617. The Israel Netanyahu Made

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

DALIA DASSA KAYE is a Distinguished Visiting Fellow at Perry World House at the University of Pennsylvania and a Senior Fellow at the UCLA Burkle Center for International Relations.

Why Bibi’s National Security Vision Will Endure

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has dominated his country’s politics for the last four decades, and his influence will not end even if Israelis vote him out of office. Over the course of his tenure as Israel’s longest-serving prime minister, Netanyahu has dramatically reset the terms of the country’s national security politics. His persistent rejection of Palestinian statehood has helped push the idea firmly out of mainstream debates, and his longtime agitation for war against Iran is now widely accepted policy across Israel’s political and security establishment.

Before his first term as prime minister, Netanyahu was already a divisive figure. In 1993, as the leader of the opposition, he likened Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin’s Oslo agreement with Palestine Liberation Organization leader Yasir Arafat to British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain’s appeasement of Hitler ahead of World War II. His inflammatory rhetoric emboldened Israelis who considered any territorial compromise with the Palestinians to be treasonous, including the assassin who killed Rabin in 1995. Today, extreme policies and positions have become normalized under Netanyahu’s watch. He has presided over a three-year campaign of mass destruction and displacement in Gaza and the killing of more than 70,000 Palestinians. His government includes one politician, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, who was barred from serving in the army in his youth because of his involvement in terrorist groups, and another, Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who has said Israel needs “to wipe out Gaza” and that it should repeat what it “did in Gaza” in the West Bank. Defense Minister Israel Katz has made similar calls for applying the “Gaza model” to the West Bank and even southern Lebanon.

Netanyahu might soon lose power. His attacks on Israel’s judiciary, corruption trials, and refusal to take responsibility for security lapses ahead of Hamas’s terror attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, have generated significant opposition among Israelis. A change of leadership could have profound consequences for domestic policy. But whether Netanyahu leaves politics this week or years down the road, his national security vision will outlast him. He has had catastrophic success in crushing domestic support for Palestinian national aspirations and gaining mainstream backing for a permanent war footing to confront Iran. Netanyahu has changed the baseline of how Israelis think about their country’s security and turned his own uncompromising positions into an institutionalized reality. Future leaders may bring their own styles to Israeli security policy, but for the foreseeable future, at least, they will be coloring within lines that Netanyahu has drawn for them.

Netanyahu is not the first Israeli prime minister to reject Palestinian national aspirations. In a 1969 interview, Golda Meir famously asserted that there was “no such thing” as a distinct Palestinian nation. Menachem Begin embraced the 1978 Camp David accords with Egypt only because the agreement did not require him to come to terms with the Palestinians. Yitzhak Shamir refused to allow independent Palestinian representation at the 1991 Madrid Peace Conference.

The difference today is that the idea of Palestinian statehood is not even up for debate. This change did not happen overnight. Rabin’s assassination led to the dismantling of the Oslo model, which intended to produce a Palestinian state but in practice produced limited Palestinian self-government and continued Israeli control over much of the West Bank. Attacks by Palestinian terrorist groups throughout the 1990s deepened Israelis’ skepticism about the prospects of peace, which helped to bring Netanyahu to power in 1996.

Still, when Netanyahu’s first term ended in 1999, some politicians sought to keep the possibility of a political settlement alive. Israelis elected leaders such as Ehud Barak and Ehud Olmert who were open to territorial concessions to the Palestinians and who engaged in serious, albeit unsuccessful, U.S.-backed peace negotiations. Barak also spearheaded a unilateral Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon in May 2000. Even the hawkish Ariel Sharon, whose controversial visit to the Temple Mount in Jerusalem later that year helped spark the second Palestinian intifada, evacuated around 9,000 Israeli settlers and initiated the withdrawal of ground forces from Gaza in 2005.

But in the absence of a negotiated solution, the conflict worsened on all fronts. Hamas won the 2006 elections in Gaza and took full control of the territory by 2007. Military conflict between Israel and Hamas became routine, with major wars erupting in 2008, 2012, 2014, and 2021. Netanyahu became prime minister again after the first of those wars, in 2009. As Israel contended with frequent rocket fire from Gaza and missile attacks by the Lebanon-based Hezbollah in the north, Netanyahu linked the deteriorating security conditions to Israel’s earlier withdrawals from these areas, bolstering his case against territorial compromise.

Netanyahu’s policies also changed realities on the ground in the West Bank. Even before his return to power, after the second intifada broke out, Israel built a concrete wall to separate Israel from the West Bank that encroached substantially on internationally acknowledged Palestinian territory. Israeli checkpoints throughout the West Bank also severely limited Palestinians’ freedom of movement and access to East Jerusalem. In office, Netanyahu accelerated policies, including continued settlement expansion, that threatened the viability of a future Palestinian state. And he encouraged a growing divide between Hamas in Gaza and the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank by facilitating Qatari payments to Hamas-run Gaza for more than a decade, further undermining the Palestinian national project.

Even the coalition led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid that briefly removed Netanyahu from power from 2021 to 2022, and which promised change, did not substantially depart from Netanyahu’s positions. It is telling that this coalition—the only one able to push Netanyahu aside in the last decade and a half—boasted strong right-wing security credentials; Bennett was a longtime opponent of Palestinian statehood. Rather than taking steps to advance peace with the Palestinians, Bennett and Lapid focused on the Abraham Accords, the agreements negotiated by the first Trump administration in which several Arab states committed to normalizing relations with Israel without requiring concrete progress toward Palestinian statehood in return.

Few vocal supporters of Palestinian statehood remained within the political establishment on the eve of October 7, 2023. The attacks that day all but ensured that opposition to a Palestinian state would become the norm. No Israeli political leader, even in the opposition, speaks anymore about ending Israeli occupation of Gaza and the West Bank. After destroying much of Gaza and killing tens of thousands of Palestinians, Israel has regained military control of nearly 70 percent of the strip. Israeli settlers in the West Bank, whose numbers have more than doubled since the Oslo accords were signed, have been attacking Palestinians with alarming frequency. The settlers have ravaged Palestinian villages as the Israeli government and military look the other way, if not outright condone the violence. Palestinians, as a result, are now more focused on survival than statehood. Whether Netanyahu wins or loses this election, there is no clear path to the two-state future he has been so determined to avoid.

Netanyahu’s hardline position on Iran has been just as consistent as his hardline attitude toward the Palestinians. As early as 1992, as a member of the Knesset, he warned that Iran was “racing to develop nuclear weapons” and that the “threat must be uprooted.” More than three decades later, Netanyahu finally found in Donald Trump a U.S. president willing to launch the military campaign against Iran he had long sought. The first attack went forward in June 2025, when the United States and Israel struck key nuclear sites in Iran. Then, on February 28, they began a larger war targeting the Iranian leadership itself. As Netanyahu announced in a video message at the start of hostilities, “Together with the U.S., we will strike the terror regime hard and create the conditions that will enable the brave Iranian people to cast off the yoke of this murderous regime.”

The Israeli public and Israeli politicians expressed near-uniform support for the war in its early months; Lapid, the leader of the opposition, declared it a “just war.” By June, polls showed more public criticism of Netanyahu’s handling of the war. But few Israeli politicians have questioned the need to maintain military pressure on Iran, and active planning for renewed attacks continues. When Yair Golan, the leader of the left-wing Democrats party, called Israel’s killing of Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khameini a “mistake” that led to an even more extremist Iranian leadership, politicians across the political spectrum condemned him.

There used to be more debate among Israel’s military, intelligence, and political elite about how to deal with Iran. When Netanyahu’s government began seriously considering a conventional military strike on Iran’s nuclear facilities in 2012, for instance—a development that drew concern in Washington—Israeli leaders did not all immediately coalesce around the idea of attacking Iran. Notable voices such as Meir Dagan, the former head of Israel’s intelligence agency, the Mossad, were arguing against a military strike and questioning what such a move could accomplish. The debate over military options became moot, however, once the Obama administration announced an interim nuclear deal with Tehran, negotiated in 2013 via back-channel diplomacy through Oman. The deal surprised and infuriated Netanyahu, who tried without success to scuttle the final nuclear agreement, which was signed in 2015.

Netanyahu’s strident opposition to U.S. President Barack Obama’s diplomacy was not supported by all Israeli politicians, either. Many worried that campaigning against the nuclear deal would damage Israel’s relations with Washington and that Netanyahu’s growing alignment with the U.S. Republican Party threatened bipartisan support for Israel. Although the Israeli government officially opposed the agreement, several prominent political leaders and members of the security establishment argued that the deal could have upsides. Benny Gantz, the former chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces, called the deal an “achievement” that could postpone Iranian nuclear capabilities for ten to 15 years. Some Israeli officials even argued against a U.S. withdrawal from the deal when Trump, with Netanyahu’s enthusiastic support, threatened to do so without first establishing an alternative arrangement. Even Barak, who had supported military strikes on Iran in the past and criticized the nuclear deal when it was signed, questioned whether Trump’s pulling out of the agreement was the “optimal” way to deal with Iran.

But these debates largely dissipated after Trump withdrew from the deal in 2018. When Iran resumed its nuclear enrichment program and with diplomatic efforts at a dead end, Netanyahu seized the opportunity to renew Israel’s shadow war with Tehran. Explosions at Iran’s Natanz nuclear facility in July 2020 and April 2021 were widely attributed to Israeli intelligence, as was the killing of Iran’s top nuclear scientist in November 2020, after the election of U.S. President Joe Biden, who had promised to restore the nuclear deal during his campaign. Even when the Bennett-Lapid coalition took over, Israeli positions on Iran remained consistent. The new government was less confrontational than Netanyahu’s, but it opposed a U.S. return to the nuclear deal. Bennett argued, as Netanyahu had done before, that an agreement would channel funds into “the Iranian terror machine.”

After October 7, with Netanyahu back in power, Israel escalated its attacks on Iran and its proxies. These actions included strikes in Tehran and Beirut that killed the heads of Hamas and Hezbollah, as well as tit-for-tat missile exchanges with Iran in April and October 2024. Aside from criticism that Netanyahu was timing the attacks to serve his own political interests, opposition figures such as Lapid and Gantz supported Netanyahu’s actions and the public backed what had become open military confrontation between Israel and Iran.

Israeli leaders have rallied behind Netanyahu’s preference for military confrontation over diplomacy, and not only with Iran. The idea that preventive military action should be used to manage any potential threat has guided Israel’s campaigns not only in Palestinian territories but also in southern Lebanon and in Syria after the fall of the Assad regime. Israel has been crossing new lines: it launched its first military attack on a Gulf Arab state in September 2025 with an airstrike in Doha targeting Hamas leaders. War has become Israel’s normal state of affairs.

Even if Netanyahu loses power, Israel’s approach to national security is unlikely to change. The opposition has articulated clear differences from the current government on matters of domestic policy, but major fissures on the Palestinian conflict, Iran, and Israel’s wider regional military campaigns are less apparent. As many Israeli analysts have put it, the political spectrum of the opposition is in essence “50 shades of right.”

Gadi Eisenkot, who has emerged as Netanyahu’s main rival, might slow settlement expansion in the West Bank and crack down on settler violence if he becomes prime minister. But those steps would amount to tactical adjustments, not strategic shifts. Eisenkot is not suggesting that Israel end its occupation of the West Bank or make a serious effort to advance a two-state solution. He has called the idea of a Palestinian state “delusional” in the wake of October 7. Other leading opposition figures, such as Bennett, not only oppose a Palestinian state but also avidly support settlement expansion.

A new government would not overhaul Israel’s policy in Gaza, either. There is widespread resentment among opposition politicians and the Israeli public over how Netanyahu waged the war, especially his resistance to cease-fire agreements that could have saved the lives of more hostages. Many believe Netanyahu prolonged the war to stay in power. But there is far less concern about the war’s effect on Palestinians; only a small number of Israeli human rights groups say Israel has committed genocide in Gaza. An Eisenkot government might be more inclined to advance the stalled Trump peace plan for Gaza, which has yet to make meaningful progress on reconstruction, Palestinian governance, or other key objectives. But like Netanyahu, Eisenkot does not support giving up Israeli control of Gaza without the full disarmament of Hamas.

More fundamentally, Israeli leaders across the board now consider control of territory to be essential to national security—whether in Gaza, the West Bank, southern Lebanon, or Syria. As the Middle East scholar Nathan Brown has observed, to the extent that debate about Israel’s security doctrine remains, it “is increasingly about how Israeli predominance should be exercised, not whether it should be maintained.” A new government might be willing, with Washington’s backing, to strike a nonaggression agreement with Syria that would require the removal of Israeli forces from border areas. Many leading Israeli politicians oppose such a withdrawal, however, lowering the odds of a deal going through. And it is even less likely that Israel would cede ground anywhere else.

No major Israeli politician is arguing for a change of course on Iran. The only significant domestic criticism of Netanyahu’s Iran policy has been about his failure to prevent diplomatic agreements, such as the short-lived memorandum of understanding the Trump administration reached with Tehran in June, which most Israelis see as favoring Iran and as failing to remove Tehran’s nuclear and missile threat. Israeli military leaders are already planning for the next round of war, including for a possible scenario in which Israel has to fight Iran on its own. Maintaining this war footing seems to have broad political support, too. Lapid has argued that Israel should go even further in its attacks on Iran, including by bombing “all energy facilities” to make sure the “Iranian economy is demolished.”

Netanyahu would have found it far more difficult to bring his hardline vision into the mainstream had he faced a political opponent who could powerfully advocate for a fundamentally different vision for Israel’s place in the region and path to peace. There have been no Rabins in the Israeli opposition, no leaders willing to pivot from their own previous positions, tell the public the truth about what a real peace would require, and invest in the painstaking work of diplomacy. Instead, Netanyahu’s governing style has stifled robust debate on national security questions and lined up Israel’s defense leadership behind the prime minister’s own uncompromising worldview that favors continuous reliance on military force.

Neither did Netanyahu face serious pushback from Washington as he pursued policies that thwarted any hope for peace with the Palestinians, including the relentless destruction of Gaza. Even though U.S. public attitudes toward Israel have grown increasingly unfavorable, on both sides of the political divide, American presidents have become less willing to use diplomatic and economic leverage to cajole Israeli leaders to end dangerous activities such as settlement expansion. The lack of pressure from the United States—and some Arab states’ willingness to normalize relations with Israel without demanding serious progress toward reconciliation with the Palestinians—made it easier for Netanyahu to pursue hardline policies without making concessions. It weakened dissenting voices within Israel, too, who could not convincingly criticize those policies on the grounds that they would damage Israel’s security or relationships with its most important partners.

Of course, regional realities complicate Netanyahu’s worldview. The plight of the Palestinians continues to resonate with foreign publics, limiting how far pivotal countries such as Saudi Arabia will go in normalizing relations with Israel. Yet future Israeli leaders may not be overly concerned about that problem, convinced that Israel, with its military might and technological edge, has too much to offer potential partners to be ignored or isolated. Many Israeli politicians today are dismissive of the increasingly common view in the region that Israel is as great a security threat as Iran.

A serious reversal of Netanyahu’s security policies will not be possible until a new Israeli leader emerges with a bold alternative plan for how Israel achieves lasting peace—and with the ability to bring the Israeli public on board. And as long as the United States remains Israel’s closest political and security partner, helping this shift along will require U.S. policies that stop enabling and start pushing back on Israeli settlement expansion, territorial occupation, and reckless military escalation.

None of this is happening during the current Israeli election season. In fact, there is little chance of it happening any time soon. Netanyahu has triumphed in squashing Israeli support for Palestinian national aspirations and normalizing military confrontation with Iran. It’s a powerful legacy. But it is not one that will make Israel, the region, or the world better off.

Trump Could Salvage the Kremlin’s Objectives in Ukraine

Vladimir Putin plays the long game. For decades, the Russian leader has worked to subjugate Ukraine. For decades, he has also cultivated ties with Donald Trump. Now, success at the latter endeavor is allowing Putin to recover from failures at the former: after nearly five years of thwarted Russian objectives in the war on Ukraine, the U.S. president is helping him win. By easing sanctions on Russia, cutting military and economic assistance to Ukraine, disrupting intelligence cooperation, and pressuring Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky to accept Putin’s war aims, Trump is making Russia’s successful annexation of substantial Ukrainian territory and de facto control over Kyiv’s future decisions more likely than at almost any point since February 2022.

Since Putin launched his full-scale invasion of Ukraine, battlefield momentum has swung back and forth between the two sides. Russian forces had the advantage at the start. But by the fall of 2022, Ukraine had seized the offensive, liberating so much territory once held by Russian troops that Washington worried Putin might use a nuclear weapon to stop Ukrainian advances. The war then settled into a tragic grind on the frontline—until last spring. Momentum returned to Ukraine after Kyiv deployed new long-range missiles capable of striking targets deep inside Russia, new midrange drones able to attack Russian supply routes in the occupied parts of eastern Ukraine, and improved interceptor drones that could better defend against Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities. Putin’s army proved unable to advance on the frontline; Ukrainian troops even made incremental progress in liberating territory.

But Trump has helped return the momentum to Russia. Stymied at the frontline, Putin has shifted his focus to terrorizing Ukrainian society through rocket and drone strikes on both civilians and the infrastructure their livelihood depends on—energy and water in particular—driven by the hope that a suffering population would pressure Zelensky to capitulate. New weaponry has made this terrorist campaign especially brutal, in particular the mass production of ballistic missiles and a new drone, the Geran 5, equipped with a powerful Chinese rocket engine that Ukrainian air defenses cannot intercept. Because Russia has more troops, greater military production capacity, and some new and better weapons, Putin believes that he can afford to persist despite the seeming stalemate.

Trump’s support has bolstered Putin’s belief that time is on his side. In just the last few weeks, Trump and his allies have expanded his help to Moscow: suspending sanctions, buying Russian diesel, disrupting Ukraine’s access to Starlink and Starshield communications systems, and even insisting that Zelensky must go. “It is equally clear that when some partners ease the pressure on Russia,” Zelensky has noted, “it only reinforces Putin’s belief that his goals are within reach.” The combination of less support from the United States, especially when it comes to air defenses, and faster Russian drones and more missiles will make the coming winter the most brutal yet for Ukrainians.

Putin’s long game with Trump began years ago, driven not so much by some specific objective, but by the hope that Kremlin influence on him might someday prove useful—a common KGB tactic. Now that day has come.

Years before Trump first ran for president, Russian actors close to Putin pursued business ties with the then real estate mogul, including most famously when Trump collaborated with Emin and Aras Agalarov to host the Miss Universe pageant in Moscow in 2013. Putin then orchestrated unprecedented foreign interference in the 2016 presidential election to help Trump and hurt his opponent Hillary Clinton. Whether the Russian theft and publication of Clinton campaign emails, combined with a comprehensive social media effort to bolster Trump, affected the 2016 election outcome is impossible to prove. That Putin was trying to influence the result is without question.

In 2017, Putin’s cultivation of Trump started to deliver some atmospheric benefits. Trump made clear his admiration for the Russian dictator. More shockingly, during his summit with Putin in Helsinki in 2018, Trump expressed doubt about the findings of his intelligence community regarding Russian interference in the 2016 presidential election and instead endorsed Putin’s denial. Yet in his first term, Trump’s rhetorical adulation of Putin only marginally changed actual U.S. policy toward Russia, in part because several senior officials on Trump’s national security team—National Security Adviser H. R. McMaster, Secretary of Defense James Mattis, Secretary of State Mike Pompeo—constrained Trump from making a full pro-Russia pivot.

After Trump’s reelection, in 2024, the Kremlin’s long courtship started to produce more tangible results. Most consequentially, Trump ended direct U.S. military and economic assistance to Ukraine. Before then, the United States was Ukraine’s biggest backer. Trump has still allowed American companies to sell some arms to European governments, who then transfer them to Ukraine. But even that channel has been disrupted periodically and only amounts to a trickle of weapons and ammunition compared with what the United States previously provided. And what Ukraine needs most—interceptors for its Patriot missile defense systems—are in very short supply, in part because the United States has launched so many of them during Trump’s war with Iran.

Other U.S. steps have further undermined Ukraine and aided Russia. Trump has insisted that Ukraine would never join NATO. He gave Putin red-carpet treatment at an Alaska summit last year, at which the two presidents talked about pressuring Ukraine to give up the rest of the eastern region of the Donbas, which Putin has been trying to conquer since 2014. Trump has periodically berated Zelensky, including most dramatically at a February 2025 Oval Office meeting, when he cut off Zelensky and yelled, “You’ve done enough talking. You’re not winning this . . . You don’t have the cards.” Since then, Trump has occasionally zagged back to more cordial relations with the Ukrainian president. In the last two weeks, Trump has increased support for Putin’s war. He lifted sanctions to allow the sale of Russian diesel exports, which will provide Moscow with billions of dollars to help finance military operations. He issued an executive order suspending new sanctions outlined in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, which he had signed just weeks earlier. There have been new lapses in sharing certain kinds of intelligence that only the United States can provide. Most consequentially, Ukraine has experienced disruptions in its access to Starlink and Starshield communications systems provided by SpaceX (a company owned by Elon Musk, a major backer and ally of Trump and a former official in his administration). Kyiv needs both services to guide its own drones and to track and defend against Russian drones and rockets. Whether the U.S. government or Musk independently ordered the disruptions is still not clear. But the results of the disruptions were clear: more Russian drones and rockets hit more Ukrainian targets, including innocent civilians; more Russian weaponry moved closer to the frontline; and more Ukrainian drones fell out of the air.

Through their teams, Putin and Trump have also been negotiating new business deals, including the purchase of assets owned by a major Russian oil company, Lukoil, by American investors; the sale of a currently dormant gas pipeline, Nord Stream 2, connecting Germany and Russia; and even secret Russian investments in a liquefied natural gas project in Alaska. If they move forward, all these projects will provide Putin with new revenues to underwrite his war in Ukraine.

Ukrainians are not giving up; according to polls, a solid majority still think that their country should keep fighting. Nor are Europeans quitting on Ukraine. Although Putin hoped that the withdrawal of American military and economic aid early in Trump’s second term would lead to a quick Ukrainian collapse or capitulation, he did not anticipate how willing and capable European countries would be in filling the American void. What’s more, Ukrainian defense tech companies are working frantically to develop new weapons to intercept the rapidly rising number of Russian ballistic missiles and speedy Geran 5 drones. Right now, the pendulum has swung in Russia’s direction, but the Ukrainians have defied expectations and regained momentum many times in the past; they can do it again.

American society does not support Trump’s embrace of Putin and abandonment of Ukraine, making it politically hard for the administration to maintain this policy pivot. If Democrats win control of one or both houses of Congress in midterm elections next week, they could very well use their new electoral mandate to investigate American-Russian business deals; introduce new legislation to restart American military and economic assistance to Ukraine and outlaw Starlink and Starshield disruptions; or deploy legislative action to enforce existing sanctions laws. Even Trump himself has stepped back from Putin before—he once wondered out loud whether Putin was “tapping me along.” He could do so again.

Putin should also realize that his cultivation of Trump is a wasting asset. No future president—Democrat or Republican—is likely to continue Trump’s Russia policy. And next week, Trump will become a lame duck, whose power to shape U.S. policy toward Russia will decline over the next two years. Putin would be wise, accordingly, to get serious about negotiating a cease-fire now. His odds of getting a favorable deal will only diminish from here.

Today, we’re excited to announce LFM3, our new generation of Liquid Foundation Models (LFMs) built for specialized intelligence. The LFM3 model family is multimodal by nature and brings text, vision, audio, omni, and decision models onto one new shared backbone. The first release of the LFM3 family covers models in the 2B parameter range.

Built on a new hybrid architecture and 22T tokens of pre-training, LFM3 is far more expressive than LFM2 while maintaining its efficiency. The extra capacity in a small, fast model makes LFM3 the ideal starting point in its class for specialized intelligence: this is because it can be fine-tuned locally, it can run on any device, and it is capable enough to become a reliable expert on a specialized task.

The base and post-trained models are open-weight and available today on Hugging Face, our Playground, and the Liquid AI API. Check out our docs on how to run and fine-tune them locally.

LFM3 instances are ideal candidates for specialized intelligence. Fine-tune them on your own data, and deploy them where your application runs. Enjoy the article.

LFM2 showed that a hybrid of short convolutions and attention could run fast on-device without sacrificing quality. Its design came from our automated hardware-in-the-loop architecture search. For LFM3, we used this search to redesign that architecture for agentic use cases, which need more capability per parameter and fast inference over long contexts.

We also expanded the hardware in the loop, from CPUs in one phone and one laptop for LFM2 to 14 device and backend combinations for LFM3: phones (Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra and OnePlus 15), a Raspberry Pi 5, laptops (Apple M4 Max and AMD Ryzen AI), and Arm and x86 cloud CPUs, plus GPUs and the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU.

Gated short convolutions still make up over half the layers. To add capability without slowing down long contexts, we tried swapping some of them for other sequence blocks, and sliding-window attention (SWA) gave the best quality–speed tradeoff. We kept the other changes described below only where they improved quality without a meaningful speed cost. The payoff shows at long context. Per parameter, LFM3-1.7B matches LFM2-1.2B on short prompts across edge devices, and with 8K tokens in context it is about 25% faster.

Tri-hybrid layer mix. LFM3 combines three operators: gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention (SWA), and global attention. Of its 24 layers, 13 are convolutions, 5 are SWA with a 256-token window, and 6 are global attention. The convolutions also use a kernel size of 7, up from 3 in LFM2.

Head-wise gated attention. A sigmoid gate scales each head’s output, so a head can switch off when it has nothing to add. This can help suppress activation outliers that hurt quantization and improve training stability.

Partial RoPE. Global attention layers rotate only the highest-frequency half of each head and leave the low-frequency half unrotated, so those dimensions match on content regardless of distance. SWA layers keep full RoPE.

Sandwich norm. Each block normalizes both its input and its output, which helps keep activations well-scaled.

LFM3 uses the same 128K vocabulary as LFM2.5-8B-A1B, built to tokenize non-Latin scripts more efficiently.

Training. LFM3-1.7B-Base was pre-trained on 22T tokens. Compared with LFM2, the mix adds more scientific papers, repository-level code, a wider range of knowledge sources, and considerably more math. The final 3.5T tokens are a mid-training stage on curated, higher-quality data, with a larger share of agentic traces and diverse tool calls to prepare the model for agentic post-training. This stage also extends the context window to 256K tokens.

#428. Scientists solve a 30-year mystery behind inflammatory bowel disease

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists from the University of Oxford, Newcastle University, and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust have uncovered a previously unexplained cause of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The discovery could change how doctors understand, diagnose, and treat certain forms of the condition.

The findings indicate that IBD is not simply one disease. Instead, it appears to include several biologically distinct conditions that develop through different processes in the body. Identifying these differences could make it possible to provide treatments tailored to the specific cause of each patient's illness.

In a study published in the New England Journal of Medicine, researchers examined more than 4,900 people with IBD and identified two important findings. First, some patients produce antibodies that interfere with interleukin-10 (IL-10), an essential protein that helps prevent excessive inflammation. Second, this autoimmune reaction explains how one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for IBD contributes to the disease.

Under normal circumstances, IL-10 acts as a chemical messenger between cells, helping the immune system regulate inflammation. However, certain antibodies can block its activity, effectively disabling one of the body's built-in safeguards against excessive immune responses. Without this protection, inflammation can persist and become difficult to control.

Hidden Antibodies Reveal a Cause of Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, two chronic conditions that affect approximately 500,000 people in the UK and millions more around the world.

Symptoms often first appear during adolescence or early adulthood, and patients may face lifelong medical challenges. Managing the disease can involve repeated hospital visits, medications that suppress immune activity, and sometimes surgery. Although treatments have improved, many people must try several different therapies without achieving lasting relief. This places a substantial burden on patients and creates significant costs for healthcare systems.

The researchers discovered that approximately 3.5% of patients with IBD had elevated levels of antibodies that neutralize IL-10. These antibodies were detected in people with both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, but not in healthy individuals.

Based on the prevalence of IBD in the UK, the researchers estimate that 15,000 to 20,000 patients nationwide could carry these harmful antibodies.

Scientists Solve a 30-Year Genetic Mystery

The investigation also revealed a strong association between the IL-10-blocking antibodies and a genetic variant called HLA-DRB1*01:03.

Scientists at Oxford first connected this genetic variant to a severe form of inflammatory bowel disease 30 years ago. However, the biological process linking the gene to the condition had remained unclear.

The new results help resolve that longstanding mystery. People who carry HLA-DRB1*01:03 are substantially more likely to develop antibodies that interfere with IL-10, providing an explanation for how this genetic risk factor can contribute to severe intestinal inflammation.

Professor Holm Uhlig, a pediatric gastroenterologist and Director of the Centre for Human Genetics at the University of Oxford's Nuffield Department of Medicine, was a senior author of the study.

"We've suspected an important role of interleukin 10 in patients with inflammatory bowel disease for decades. The study now provides clear evidence and contributes the missing link between a well-known genetic variant that had been linked to severe inflammatory bowel disease in the past and the very recently discovered autoimmunity to interleukin 10."

"Understanding what drives the inflammation, provides a clear explanation for disease in this group of people and opens the door to new treatments that target the autoantibodies themselves or cells that produce those autoantibodies."

How Earlier Discoveries Helped Explain Severe IBD

The findings build on more than a decade of research into rare inherited disorders that disrupt the body's ability to control inflammation.

Sophie Hambleton, Professor of Pediatrics and Immunology at Newcastle University, explained that studying these uncommon conditions provided important clues about the mechanisms involved in more widespread forms of IBD.

"This discovery shows how the study of rare, inherited disorders can shed new light on common conditions. The groundwork was laid over 10 years ago when genetic defects in IL-10 or its receptor were found in young children with severe IBD. Later, we realized that neutralizing autoantibodies against IL-10 were mimicking this effect in 2 little girls with colitis."

These earlier observations helped researchers recognize that antibodies blocking IL-10 could produce effects similar to inherited defects involving the same protein or its receptor. The latest study now establishes the importance of this mechanism in a larger group of patients with inflammatory bowel disease.

A Blood Test Could Lead to More Targeted IBD Treatments

Beyond explaining a previously mysterious cause of intestinal inflammation, the discovery could have important implications for patient care.

Researchers believe the results provide a foundation for developing a blood test that identifies people whose disease is associated with antibodies against IL-10. Such a test could help doctors recognize these patients earlier and select treatments that address the underlying immune malfunction.

Dr. Rainer Doffinger, a clinical immunologist at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, highlighted the potential benefits for both patients and healthcare systems.

"By identifying patients early and giving them targeted treatment, we could reduce reliance on expensive ongoing therapy and prevent complications. Even modest reductions in biologic treatment, admissions and surgery could translate into savings of many millions of pounds annually for the NHS, alongside the major benefits for patients through fewer interventions, improved quality of life or even durable remission."

Targeting the antibodies themselves, or the immune cells responsible for producing them, could offer a new treatment strategy for this particular group of patients. The approach could potentially reduce the need for repeated therapies, hospital admissions, and surgery while improving the chances of sustained disease control.

Professor Simon Travis, Professor of Clinical Gastroenterology at the University of Oxford and the Kennedy Institute of Rheumatology, emphasized the significance of identifying a specific biological cause of IBD.

"This is the most exciting discovery in a lifetime specialising in IBD. It means that we can now identify a group where we know what is causing the disease and that creates a real opportunity to change how we manage this disease."

The research marks an important advance toward personalized treatment for inflammatory bowel disease. Rather than relying primarily on symptoms to guide medical decisions, doctors may eventually be able to use biological testing to determine what is driving inflammation in individual patients and choose more appropriate therapies.

The study involved researchers from the Nuffield Department of Medicine at the University of Oxford, Newcastle University's Translational and Clinical Research Institute, and the Department of Immunology at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust.

#5245. Migration to safety: Three insights you may have missed from theCUBE’s coverage of DigiCert’s World Quantum Readiness Day 2026

Public key infrastructure, or PKI, modernization is taking on new urgency as organizations prepare for the security risks posed by quantum computing.

PKI provides a framework for creating, managing and revoking digital certificates that establish trust across digital networks. As quantum computing advances, organizations must prepare to replace vulnerable public-key cryptography across their systems.

“Prioritize the migration to quantum safety,” said Amit Sinha (pictured), chief executive officer of DigiCert Inc., during an interview with theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. “PKI modernization is long overdue. If you don’t start today, you’re already out of time.”

Quantum risk and what it takes for enterprises to prepare for a post-quantum world were the central focus of DigiCert’s World Quantum Readiness Day 2026 virtual event, with coverage by theCUBE. Security leaders and public key infrastructure teams explored how to inventory cryptographic assets, prioritize migration, evaluate quantum-safe algorithms and prepare for the quantum era. (* Disclosure below.)

Here are three key insights you may have missed from theCUBE’s coverage of DigiCert’s World Quantum Readiness Day 2026 virtual event:

Organizations need visibility into where cryptography is used before they can plan a migration. DigiCert’s Quantum Central is designed to help assess that risk, while shorter certificate lifetimes add pressure to automate certificate management, according to Sinha.

“The road to crypto agility with the 47-day mandate is kind of the same road to post-quantum cryptography,” Sinha said. “Both of those deadlines are now 2029. So, having a good system in place, bringing public and private PKI together [and] having last-mile automation capabilities that give you crypto agility are foundational steps — both for the 47-day mandate that we have today and the migration to post-quantum cryptography.”

Post-quantum cryptography will require flexibility as organizations navigate different standards across jurisdictions. Many countries are adopting standards developed by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, while others are pursuing different approaches. China has launched a program to solicit and evaluate quantum-resistant cryptographic algorithms for possible standardization.

“I think [different standards] will affect [PQC],” said Jostein Stokkan, product manager of service offerings at Atea Norge AS, during an interview with theCUBE. “But if we can help organizations to become more crypto agile, to be able to adapt to different types of encryption as they become important or just change, I think everything will be smoother and easier for all parties.”

A smoother transition will also depend on how organizations allocate resources and identify the teams and service owners who will be responsible for implementation. LGT Financial Services AG started asking vendors for supported algorithms and compatibility constraints at least two years ago, according to Christian Pfister, team leader, IT security operations, and lead for LGT’s Quantum Safe Competence Center.

“If you try to inventory, replace and validate every cryptographic use case at once, it makes the program unmanageable, and you can’t measure the outcome,” he told theCUBE. “The [post-quantum cryptography] migration is not one project and one algorithm change; it’s a series of migration waves.”

A survey released by DigiCert found that 87% of surveyed organizations were planning, testing or implementing post-quantum cryptography initiatives, while only 7% had deployed quantum-safe or hybrid cryptography across most of their digital certificates. The gap between planning and deployment leaves organizations with questions about how to proceed.

“There is no one-size-fits-all; there is no template,” said Naomi Wynn, chief executive officer of National Energy Public Key Infrastructure (NEPKI), during an appearance at the event. “But I’m hoping that by this time next year … we are seeing improved guidance as well in terms of what expectations are, what is good enough [and] what will actually meet the requirements.”

Organizations are also testing how post-quantum cryptography will perform in their existing environments. Daiwa Institute of Research Ltd. conducted a proof of concept for Daiwa Securities Group that used a post-quantum-enabled load balancer to assess Transport Layer Security performance.

Quantum computing still faces challenges in areas such as error correction, scaling, and hardware and software. For organizations preparing their security systems, practical tests and staged planning can help narrow the problem, according to Colin Soutar, managing director at Deloitte.

“I would take the word ‘quantum’ out of it,” Soutar said during an appearance at the event. “I think that the misconception is that one needs to be an expert [or] have a background in physics to understand what’s needed here. There is a threat out there in the future … and the steps to mitigate that are generally known. Working through it again is relatively straightforward, albeit large and complex.”

Microsoft Corp. has set a goal of transitioning its products and services to post-quantum cryptography by 2029. Its Trusted Root Program pilot brings certificate authorities into that effort, according to Karina Sirota Goodley, senior security product manager at Microsoft.

“Certificate authorities can test issuance and compatibility with the Microsoft platform capabilities in a controlled environment,” she told theCUBE. “That matters because no individual component can declare success. The whole chain has to work.”
