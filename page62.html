New York City prosecutors from all five boroughs have given "preferential treatment" to noncitizens who commit crimes, all to help them avoid deportation, according to a report from the GOP-led House Judiciary Committee.

The committee, chaired by Rep. Jim Jordan, R-Ohio, said nonpublic documents obtained through its oversight work revealed new details about the extent to which the city's district attorney's offices sought to limit the immigration consequences of criminal prosecutions.

Some of the report's most serious allegations came against Bronx District Attorney Darcel Clark. The report claims she "trains prosecutors in her office to conceal certain facts" from defendants' conviction records and pursue charges that would not trigger deportation.

Internal materials from the Bronx DA's office explicitly instruct prosecutors to leave references to firearms out of conviction records to maintain "immigration-conscious plea bargaining," according to the report.

In cases of domestic violence or crimes against children — offenses that are often deportable under the Immigration and Nationality Act — Bronx prosecutors were told to leave out defendants' relationships with their alleged victims and pursue charges that did not specify the ages of child victims.

Clark, who has been the Bronx DA since 2016, was also accused of encouraging prosecutors to charge foreign nationals with "nonexistent, legally impossible" crimes to skirt deportation.

The report's authors offered the example of Clark's staff getting someone to plead guilty to "attempted reckless assault," a crime they described as legally impossible because someone cannot intend to act recklessly. A plea to such a crime, they said, could allow a noncitizen defendant to stay in the country indefinitely.

New York courts nevertheless allow defendants to plead guilty to such hypothetical offenses as part of negotiated plea deals. The Court of Appeals, the state's highest court, upheld the legal concept this year.

The House Judiciary Committee also pointed to similar practices within the Queens DA's office, led by Melinda Katz since 2020.

It cited a May 2026 email from a Queens prosecutor informing an NYPD detective that the DA's office had "basically made a [plea] deal which is ‘immigration safe’ while still having [the defendant] plea to multiple crimes and do some jail time."

Citywide sanctuary policies led the NYPD to decline 20,255 immigration detainers between July 2018 and June 2026, the report said. Detainers are federal requests asking local authorities to hold certain noncitizens briefly so Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) can take them into custody.

These figures are not necessarily surprising, given that each mayoral administration throughout that period has limited city agencies from cooperating with ICE.

From the August 1976 issue of Car and Driver.

I’m old enough to remember driving the Dauphine, so serious contemplation of any vehicle carrying a Renault label brings a certain anguish. That rectangular little nightmare was pressed into the unwitting hands of over two million customers between its introduction in 1956 and its merciful cancellation in 1968, and there are still Americans who can be brought to the edge of hysterics at the mere mention of the car. Even the fanatical chauvinism of the French gave way to the Dauphine’s realities. Edouard Seidler, whose book The Romance of Renault was a serenade to the marvels of the Billancourt firm, stumbled against the hard truth of the Dauphine. He wrote: “It was essentially a French car quality-wise . . . ideal for 5000 miles a year on French roads but incapable of 16,000 miles in the less favorable driving conditions often found abroad, particularly in the United States.”

Yes, its Frenchness—abetted by a chaotic dealer organization, inefficient spare parts distribution, and feeble performance from its tiny, rear-mounted four-cylinder engine—made the Dauphine a legend among bad automobiles.

The Régie Nationale des Usines Renault, however, is one of the world’s oldest (1898) and most honored automobile manufacturers. During the early years, founder Louis Renault and family produced superb sports and luxury machines before turning, in the 1930s, to the sale of prosaic, middle-class sedans for the masses. Then in 1945, poor old Louis, a suspected Nazi collaborator, died shortly after being released from prison, and the company was nationalized. It took nearly 20 years—and the agony of the Dauphine—before the physical and spiritual devastation of World War II was corrected.

Happily, Renault is back in business with its old French passion. The past seven years have seen the introduction of a superb new generation of front-wheel-drive sedans: the Renault 12 in 1969, the 15/17 series in ’71, the R5 in 1972, and the R20 and 30 model lines within the last year. With these has come erasure of the bad memories of the 1950s and ’60s and a new sense of purpose within the company, not only in solid products but also in adventurous forays into endurance racing and even Formula 1 competition.

The most exciting development for Americans is the Renault 17 Gordini, a muscular version of the regular four-seat, two-door 15/17 series that may be the best value in sporting machinery available in America. With a price tag under $7000, the Gordini provides power, speed, quality, and handling generally unknown in automobiles costing less than $10,000. Its smooth, easy-revving 1647cc, single-overhead-cam, four-cyl­inder engine pumps out a lusty 90 horse­power, thanks to a higher compression ratio and electronic fuel injection. The ef­ficiency of its induction system permits the Gordini to operate without a catalytic converter (though premium gasoline is required), which further enhances per­formance and keeps mileage in the 22-to-24-mpg range.

You might fault the car for its styling­—like the Chevrolet Monza, its essentially pleasing lines are nullified by a cloying sense that the body is two inches too narrow and sits just a bit too tall on its wheels to be truly pretty. Other niggling complaints can be generated as well: The dash panel is an inkwell of black vi­nyl, punctured by deep instrument cavi­ties that make some of the dials difficult to read; it has a less-than-perfect driving position, at least for six-footers (the throttle is angled so you have to con­sciously bend your foot at the ankle to maintain cruising speed); and the prosa­ic, oversize steering wheel is the same one employed on the Renault 12.

But these failings fade in the face of the Gordini’s truly invigorating behavior on the road. (For history buffs, the Gordi­ni name refers to the French specialty-­car firm operated by Amédée Gordini in the 1950s. In addition to the campaign­ing of successful Grand Prix and sports/racing cars, Gordini produced a modified version of the Dauphine. As an employ­ee of Renault, his name has become symbolic of the firm’s involvement with high-performance production cars.) And the 17 Gordini is certainly one GT car that delivers what its prestigious name­plate implies. In all too many cases, a fine engine like the Gordini’s is hooked to an awful gearbox that destroys both efficiency and driving pleasure. Quite the opposite is true here. The Renault’s five-speed is a marvel of smooth linkages and pre­cisely placed ratios. Coupled to a firm but forgiving clutch, it is a delight, either in fuming downtown jams or hard runs through winding back roads.

Being a front-wheel-drive automobile, the Gordini has certain endemic limitations, but Renault has done a good job in shielding the driver from them. The heavy, low-speed steering is still present (hence the big wheel), and there are traces of torque steer when accelerating out of tight corners. Conscientious de­velopment of the suspension (front and rear stabilizer bars; wider, steel-belted radial tires) cannot totally eliminate the final understeer that marks all front­-wheel-drive automobiles. Nevertheless, the neat balance of engine, gearbox, and brakes produces a machine that is a pleasure to drive in all situations. On more than one occasion we undertook effortless 100-mile journeys with four adults at cruising speeds of 75 mph. The completion of the trips inevitably left ev­erybody puzzling over two questions: 1. Why can’t all cars be this efficient? and 2. Is this car really French?

There is a certain perverse justifica­tion to the second question when you consider the long Gallic tradition of deliv­ering plastic whackies to these shores. Based on misadventures with cars like the Dauphine and the Peugeot 403, Americans have come to accept French automobiles as weird, flimsy devices with praying-mantis suspensions, poly­styrene interiors, and hand-grenade en­gines. To recall Monsieur Seidler’s words once again, these were “essen­tially French cars, quality-wise,” with all of the attendant ways of institutionalizing otherwise sane automotive enthusiasts. Not so with the new-generation Re­naults. In fact, anyone with built-in preju­dices about French cars will find the Gordini a revelation. This is one solid piece of machinery, devoid of the gim­mickry that plagued its forebears. The coachwork matches all but the most carefully buttoned-up luxury cars, and af­ter several months of hard bashing over all sorts of highways, not a single nut or bolt on our Gordini came loose. Regard­less of the weather, which included ev­erything from sub-zero snowstorms to balmy spring days, the car operated flawlessly, virtually without complaint.

Renault is definitely on to something and, thanks to a range of high-value products, increased company morale, and renewed efforts to beef up its Amer­ican distribution and dealer situation, it figures to significantly increase its Amer­ican presence in the near future. If that means more cars like the superb Re­nault 17 Gordini, that’s nothing but good news.

Nelson is in the final year of his current deal. He signed a four-year, $80 million extension to remain with the Colts ahead of the 2022 season. Seattle will pay the remaining $9.777 million he is owed, per The Ringer's Tom Pelissero.

If Seattle doesn't sign the guard to an extension, he would net the Seahawks a 2028 compensatory draft pick if he leaves in free agency. Seattle is also expected to receive comp picks for Kenneth Walker III, Tariq Woolen, Coby Bryant and Boye Mafe, who left in free agency this past offseason.

Nelson's trade isn't a shock, as Indianapolis is projected to undergo a fire sale ahead of the Nov. 10 trade deadline. His expiring contract made it more likely he'd be moved.

#5405. XPENG Tops Customer Satisfaction Ranking For Second Year, Beating Tesla

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

XPENG recently ranked highest in the European USCALE “EV Satisfaction Study” for the second year in a row. Not only did XPENG top the chart again, but their score improved over the previous year. The leading 82% recommendation rate ranks significantly above the 53% industry average, positioning XPENG ahead of all American, European and Asian manufacturers.

The Stuttgart-based study surveyed over 4000 customers from Germany, Austria and Switzerland. Consulting firm USCALE uses Net Promotor Score (NPS) methodology looking at factors such as charging speed, range and overall product features. While other brands had a varying proportion of detractors, XPENG only had promotors. XPENG ranked above average across all categories with customers particularly satisfied with charging performance, charging management, range and app integration.

While XPENG topped the charts, EVs are leading to increased overall customer satisfaction, with Tesla taking the runner-up position. But customer expectations are evolving. As explained by Dr. Axel Sprenger, Founder & Managing Director of USCALE GmbH:

EV drivers are more satisfied than ever. But they are also becoming more demanding. What was exciting yesterday is standard today. Manufacturers have done their homework when it comes to range and charging. Now, the competition is decided by software, connectivity, and a seamless charging experience. Those who fail to deliver here will fall behind, and this is clearly visible in the ranking.

As part of the shift in expectations, not all EV brands have risen above the fray and some past leaders have been knocked off the top. As noted by Dan Caesar, Founder, Electric Vehicles GLOBAL:

We often get asked which are the best battery EVs on the market, but our subjective opinion is significantly outweighed by the mass-market’s real life experience of EVs on sale. Germany’s USCALE survey has once again shown in 2026 which brands are best regarded by EV drivers; the differences are stark. Notably, Opel, Fiat and MG are the poorest performers. Meanwhile, while Tesla has long been considered ‘the brand to beat’, it has finished behind fellow tech leader XPENG for the second year running, albeit far ahead of other Asian brands. What’s more, at the recent Paris Motor Show, XPENG demonstrated beyond doubt that its NGP self-driving capability is directly comparable to Tesla’s much-heralded FSD technology. With strong sales in Europe, we think many English-speaking nations are sleeping on XPENG’s potential and once its full range of EVs is on sale in all territories, it will be the one to watch.

XPENG didn’t get to the top of these rankings by chance. It is a manifestation of a more human approach to technology that permeates throughout the company, from their employees to their services to their products.

Some Chinese automakers have taken the approach of holding back their latest technology for the for the home market, while bringing older tech to Europe. Models introduced as “new” in export markets may already be discontinued in China. While using older platforms can save on costs and might be good enough to compete in Europe, customers are not getting the best available. In addition, many other automakers are marking up prices far beyond the cost of importing the vehicles.

XPENG is taking a different approach. They are rolling out their latest models with their most advanced technology globally. In terms of intelligent driving, XPENG may soon have the most global system. It was designed from the start to be global and meet anticipated UN DCAS regulations, while being adapted to different driving circumstances around the world. Those local driving circumstances are shaped just as much by the customs of human drivers as they are by any rules. And XPENG is not marking up products and technologies significantly more than needed to cover the associated taxes, tariffs and other costs. Making the best technology available at an accessible price helps to increase customer satisfaction. Ensuring that technology meets the needs of each customer where they live increases satisfaction further. When I interviewed him in Paris, I asked XPENG Chairman, CEO and co-founder He Xiaopeng to explain the strategy (auto translated with Google):

Let me start by saying this: who wouldn’t want the best technology at a reasonable price, right? As a product manager with a technical background, I have always been driven by a desire to bring superior technology to as many people as possible—at a fair price point. For the past six or seven years, our approach to autonomous driving at XPENG has been guided by a core philosophy: how can we deploy our technology globally without needing to manually collect high-definition maps or hard-code region-specific driving rules for every single location? Instead, we aim for a system capable of generalizing across the diverse driving habits found around the world—habits that go beyond mere laws and regulations. This is the focus of much of our work. That is why you see us currently conducting tests in Europe and beyond; we aim to launch our next-generation NGP (Navigation Guided Pilot) in the European market next year. This is the culmination of the foundation we’ve built over the last six or seven years, and I am truly excited to bring our best technology to European customers at an accessible price.

Building upon their focus on the human side of technology, XPENG has been putting much of their work into adapting their technology to local culture, customs, rules and needs. When people feel respected, satisfaction increases. When technology addresses human needs, people are more satisfied with technology. Technology adds value. In addition, XPENG has been expanding local development and localizing manufacturing. XPENG has also partnered with companies like Volkswagen to supply technology and is integrating technology from companies like Google into their systems. I asked He Xiaopeng about the value found in different cultures and perspectives and  how he sees this influencing the evolution of technology:

XPENG cannot rely on a long history to influence customer opinion. Customers will not be showing support for the brand based on memories of their youth. Many may not be familiar with XPENG as a company or their vehicles and technology. Many Europeans outside of enthusiast circles may not have even heard of XPENG or the other recent entrants into an automobile market that has traditionally been dominated by brands that extend back generations.

As such, they need to focus on their customers. They need to meet and exceed expectations. They need to make friends.

High customer satisfaction does not always show up immediately in quarterly sales results. However, it does create friendships and build advocates for your brand. Those advocates then talk to their friends and influence purchase decisions over the long term. Friends of the brand become more likely to buy from that brand in the future. The technology rollout and market response may be fast, but customer ecosystems need to build over time. By offering the best technology to global markets and focusing on the needs of their customers, XPENG is not just topping customer satisfaction surveys. By focusing on long-term customer relationships, XPENG is setting itself up for long-term success.
