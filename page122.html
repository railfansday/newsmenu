#208. How solid-state transformers could help power the AI boom

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New technology could help cut power losses and reinvent a staple of the grid.

Transformers are a crucial but nearly invisible part of our grid. The devices help efficiently move power across long distances, but they’re complicated and in short supply. And given that their basic design hasn’t changed much for about a century, they could use an upgrade.

As the world races to meet rising electricity demand from EVs, data centers, and more, researchers and companies are looking to modernize transformers. New, solid-state versions could help relieve some of the supply concerns and make sure the grid can handle the growing pressure.

Conventional transformers generally rely on huge amounts of specialized steel alloys and miles of copper wire to do their work of transferring electricity between circuits. Solid-state models, in comparison, combine magnetic components with high-power semiconductors, making them more compact and allowing for more control over the flow of electricity.

These devices are still in development, and can’t replace all traditional transformers today, especially the largest ones that work at the high voltages necessary for long-distance transmission. But a new generation of transformer technology could be a crucial part of our future grid.

Moving electricity through the grid isn’t perfectly efficient: As electricity flows through power lines, some gets lost as heat. How much varies—the higher the voltage, the lower the current, and the less electricity gets lost. So a key function for the grid is stepping voltage up and down.

Consider, for example, your local solar plant. For that electricity to efficiently travel to your home hundreds of miles away, it needs to reach a higher voltage than what’s generated by the solar plant. Enter transformers.

In its path from the power plant to your outlets, electricity might go through one transformer at the solar farm and another at a substation nearby, stepping up the voltage twice to reach a high enough level to travel on high-voltage transmission lines. Solar panels also produce direct current (DC) electricity, which needs to be converted to alternating current (AC) via an inverter before it flows into a transformer. Closer to its destination, the voltage will be stepped down again, probably at least twice: once at another substation and again at a smaller transformer in your neighborhood.

If you’re in the US, electricity from a solar plant might be converted to AC by an inverter at around 480 volts, get stepped up to 115,000 volts for the transmission lines, and finally get stepped back down to 120 volts and 240 volts for your home.

Conventional transformers do this work using what essentially amount to loops of wire. The core of a transformer is typically made of a special type of steel called grain-oriented electrical steel. Coils of copper wire sit on either end of that core.

The number of loops in each coil determines the voltage on either end of the transformer: If there are more loops on the secondary coil than the primary, the voltage steps up, and if there are fewer, it steps down. When incoming electricity passes through loops on the upstream end of the transformer, that forms a magnetic field in the core. That field induces a voltage in the loops on the other side of the device, which is what allows the energy to continue flowing at a new voltage. This process is called magnetic induction.

Transformers form the backbone of electricity transmission and distribution, says Srdjan Lukic, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at North Carolina State University. In addition to doing the job of stepping up and down voltage, transformers also often provide what’s called galvanic isolation, meaning there’s no direct electrical path flowing through them. That can help limit disturbances from moving through the grid, improving safety.

The problem is that today’s transformers are big and bulky, and they’re also difficult and expensive to replace, Lukic says.

Solid-state transformers essentially add so-called power electronics—semiconductors that convert and control electricity to the typical blueprint of today’s transformers.

While a conventional transformer operates at the frequency of the part of the grid it’s connected to, a solid-state transformer actually controls the frequency of the current. Typically, a solid-state transformer first converts incoming AC power from the grid to DC power, and then to a higher-frequency AC power using switches made of a semiconductor like silicon carbide.

From there, a magnetic component changes the voltage. Because the advanced semiconductors allow transformers to exert so much control over the frequency and stability of the current, the magnetic core can be much smaller than in a conventional transformer. Some come in at roughly one-tenth the size but have the same capacity, Lukic says.

Finally, additional power electronics convert the electricity from high-frequency AC to its required form—generally back to DC power and finally to lower-frequency AC.

Today, stepping voltage up and down is many transformers’ only job. But solid-state transformers can do much more. They absorb the jobs of devices like rectifiers and inverters (which convert AC to DC power and vice versa), shrinking the total footprint of the electrical equipment and potentially cutting the amount of power lost in the system.

In August, the New York Power Authority and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) tested a one-megawatt solid-state transformer under realistic utility conditions at EPRI’s test site. The device was built in Lukic’s lab, and it was the first such test for a transformer of that capacity, he says.

The prototype was roughly 98% efficient—meaning it lost only 2% of the energy fed in. An equivalent conventional transformer and rectifier, by comparison, would be about 96% efficient together, Lukic says. A difference of 2% might seem small, but it is significant, since it means the solid-state transformer could cut power losses from conventional equipment in half, Lukic says.

One major goal of the project was to help utilities gain more confidence in these new components, Lukic says. That’s crucial, since these devices could be used on the power grid in the future. In the near term, solid-state transformers could be used within data centers to provide electricity for server racks.

The prototype is still a relatively low-voltage system, operating at about 15,000 volts. Industrial facilities like larger data centers often require higher-voltage connections, and transmission lines typically operate at hundreds of thousands of volts. Adapting solid-state transformers to these higher voltages will require further engineering development. But the test was a successful demonstration that solid-state transformers can work under real conditions, Lukic says.

Companies developing solid-state transformers are largely focused on data centers right now, Lukic says. There’s an influx of money to support developing the new technology, and the voltage requirements aren’t as high as they will be for larger transformers that will be needed for long-distance transmission.

In February, Heron Power raised $140 million to commercialize its solid-state transformer, which the company calls the Heron Link. The technology could be used in data centers, solar power arrays, and grid batteries, says Drew Baglino, the founder and CEO. The company is starting to test its technology at larger scales now, Baglino says. Ten megawatt-hours’ worth of batteries at Heron’s headquarters in Scotts Valley, California, will soon undergo testing with Heron transformers. The company is also planning a pilot with a nearby solar farm, Baglino says.

One challenge for solid-state transformers is that their power electronics have more failure modes than conventional transformers. In a traditional device, a failure means that the entire device will typically be taken offline, resulting in power interruptions. But Heron designs with that in mind, Baglino says: The company uses a modular design, with many small devices that work together. If one fails, the rest of the system keeps working until the affected bit can be replaced, which doesn’t require shutting the whole thing down.

Heron Power is currently building a $100 million factory in California to make advanced transformers. The facility should begin production in 2027 and reach its full production rate of 40 gigawatts’ worth of transformers per year in 2029.

In recent years, companies that make grid equipment have put $1.7 billion into the US supply chain for power transformers, which are used in high-voltage transmission, says Benjamin Boucher, a supply chain analyst at Wood Mackenzie. There’s been an additional $1.3 billion invested for distribution transformers, the smaller devices that help transform power for homes and businesses, he adds.

But even this investment isn’t keeping pace with the rapid growth of the grid. Wait times for large conventional transformers—the kind needed to connect new power plants, long-distance transmission, and high-demand sites like data centers—are stretching as long as five years.

“Transformers have become one of the biggest bottlenecks for utilities, generation developers, and even data centers,” Boucher says. The big challenge is that it’s difficult to get new transformer manufacturing up and running, because it’s a quite technical process that requires a lot of specialized labor. So even facilities announced years ago are still years from opening, Boucher says.

Solid-state transformers could help ease some of that pressure. But high-voltage devices needed for power plant connections and transmission lines are the holy grail for solid-state transformers, Lukic says. Existing solid-state technology can’t be used for those applications yet, so the industry will need to develop new materials and designs to support high voltages and handle electricity safely in a compact device.

“We’re facing a big risk related to grid infrastructure,” Boucher says. “Without the transformers, you don’t really have an electrical grid.”

#4932. Bitcoin Mining Firm Luxor Raises $16 Million to Scale AI Compute Trading and Hardware

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Luxor, a bitcoin miner turned AI compute firm, raised $16 million led by CMT Digital to expand its AI hardware brokerage and compute trading business.

Seattle-based Luxor Technology Corporation announced a $16 million strategic investment round on Tuesday. CMT Digital, the venture capital arm of Chicago-based CMT Group, led the round. Canaan, MicroBT, Halo, Steelhead, and angel investors also participated.

The round includes both primary and secondary capital. Luxor describes each investor as an operating partner in the compute supply chain it serves, spanning hardware, capital, and power.

The raise follows the September launch of Luxor AI, a business segment that extends the energy, hardware, software, compute trading, and data stack Luxor built for bitcoin mining into artificial intelligence and high-performance computing.

Luxor said its hardware brokerage desk has traded $1 billion of ASIC hardware for bitcoin mining. Its energy arm services more than 75 megawatts of load across ERCOT and SPP. Its agentic cloud platform had reached 1.8 million workloads year to date as of September.

The firm plans to use the new capital in four areas: scaling its agentic cloud platform, providing working capital for its AI hardware business, expanding its compute derivative trading and market making, and hiring engineering and go-to-market staff.

The hardware push responds to tightening supply chains. The firm’s AI hardware desk sources GPUs and AI servers through a tiered network of partners. As a Value-Added Reseller to major OEMs, the company says it can access abandoned orders, special allocations, and used markets.

CMT Digital brings nearly three decades of electronic trading and market-structure experience. Sam Hallene, a partner at CMT Digital, said Luxor understands the energy and infrastructure landscape behind compute, as well as the financial tools customers need to manage risk. He said the team has built that skill over eight years in digital assets and is applying it to AI.

Luxor’s over-the-counter desk already offers cash-settled options, forwards, and backstops on compute-rental indexes. CMT’s backing, the company says, positions Luxor to scale that business as AI compute becomes a tradable market.

Canaan CEO Nangeng Zhang said his company is pursuing long-term power resources in North America as part of its compute-plus-energy strategy, and that joining Luxor’s cap table aligns with those goals. He called the company an important partner across bitcoin mining and the energy industry.

Nick Hansen, Luxor’s CEO and co-founder, summed up the thesis: “Compute markets are converging: the same miners, power sites and capital that served digital mining are now powering AI.”

Earlier this year, the company unveiled Commander, a fleet management platform designed to help Bitcoin mining operators monitor operations, manage energy use, and optimize profitability from a single system.

Integrated with existing infrastructure, the platform offers real-time fleet monitoring, remote machine controls, and tools that help miners adjust to changing hashrate and electricity market conditions.

Public bitcoin miners had signed more than $70 billion in AI and high-performance computing contracts by the end of March, according to CoinShares data.

#4544. In 1828, serial killers sold their victims’ bodies to science

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 8:53 AM EDT

For as long as surgeons cut open living people to keep them alive, they have needed dead people to practice on. Today, donation programs keep medical schools supplied with the bodies they need to teach their students. But in 1820s Scotland, donating your body to science wasn’t legal yet. Nonetheless, one doctor found a way to get the bodies he needed. But it came at a terrible cost. This is the grisly story of the Anatomy Murders.

In the early 1800s, surgery was booming. A few decades earlier, Scottish surgeon John Hunter had helped bring a scientific view to Western medicine, transforming surgery with experimentation and careful observation. The Napoleonic Wars had boosted the demand for trained surgeons, Alun Evans, a retired epidemiologist at Queen’s University in Belfast, tells Popular Science.

One of the leading lights in this burgeoning field was Hunter’s compatriot Robert Knox, who ran an anatomy school in Edinburgh. But by 1827, Knox had hit a roadblock to his teaching ambitions. A change to regulations meant that dissection had become an important part of the medical curriculum. Knox’s school would require more than 400 bodies the following year. What made this such an overwhelming ask was that citizens had no legal way to donate their bodies to medical science. But where there’s a demand, supply will follow.

Edinburgh Medical School first opened in 1728, and for much of the next century, allegations of grave robbing linked to the school had been rife. Newly buried corpses would be exhumed and then presented at medical school doors by so-called “resurrectionists”.

The provenance of these bodies was unimportant to the corpse-strapped medical profession. But families, desperate to protect their loved ones, had taken extreme steps to stamp out the practice. Relatives would stay at their loved ones’ graveside for weeks, waiting for their bodies to lose their medical value. Factories produced devices like mort safes, heavy iron cages placed over newly dug graves. Knox had been forced to source his bodies from further away, even bringing some from across the Irish Sea.

Across the city, two other men also had a body problem. Irishman William Hare owned a lodging house with his wife near Edinburgh’s Grassmarket. One of his tenants was a fellow Irishman, William Burke. Burke and Hare became friends.

One day in early 1828, Hare discovered one of his tenants had died overnight. Even worse, the dead man still owed him rent. The pair came up with a plan to recoup the money, and they carried the corpse to Knox’s door. There, he paid them £7 and 10 shillings; more than a month’s wages.

Their eyes opened by greed, Burke and Hare hatched a plan to get Knox the bodies he desperately needed. Over the next 12 months, they brought him 16 fresh corpses. They had acquired each one by murdering their way through the poor of Edinburgh. They had lured the victims to Hare’s guesthouse, and then suffocated them in the night, lying on their chests to minimize any damage to their valuable bodies. The majority of their victims were women.

Burke and Hare’s rampage was only stopped in November 1828. A tenant at Hare’s guesthouse reported to the police that they had found a corpse under their bed. When the police raided the premises, they only found a single spot of blood where the body had been.

But under interrogation, Burke and his wife gave inconsistent accounts. Eventually, Burke, Hare, and their wives were all arrested.

Despite their equal guilt in their crimes, the two men had very different fates. Hare offered to confess everything to the authorities in exchange for his freedom. His evidence condemned Burke.

Two months later, Burke was hanged in front of a baying crowd. Ironically, his crimes meant that, under the Murder Act of 1752, he was not allowed a burial. His body was instead donated to the Edinburgh Medical School, where his skeleton is still on show today.

Hare disappeared after being freed. Evans says that research suggests he returned to Ireland, where some think he ended his days in Kilkeel Workhouse in County Down.

Knox faced graver consequences than Hare. He was never tried for his link to the murders, but his reputation was buried. He was forced out of a curatorial role at the Royal College of Surgeons and eventually moved to London. He was unable to secure another university teaching post, but continued to work as an anatomist at a hospital in the city.

Evans says the taint of the scandal went beyond Knox. “The Burke and Hare scandal really stymied medicine in Edinburgh considerably, and it explained the rising popularity of the Dublin schools and the Paris schools of medicine,” he says.

One fact that may have haunted Knox is that if he had avoided the lure of Burke and Hare’s corpses for just a few more years, he would have benefited from the Anatomy Act, passed in 1832, which gave surgeons the green light to procure any bodies from hospitals, prisons, or workhouses that had gone unclaimed for 48 hours. Voluntary donation was also legalized at the same time.

Instead, Knox became part of a black legend in Scotland’s history. Burke and Hare’s crimes have become an indelible part of Edinburgh’s grim past.

#5195. Is the Housing Affordability Crisis Becoming a Generational Wealth Fight?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jasmyn Carter thought she had done what prospective homebuyers are supposed to do.

She had a good debt-to-income ratio, she paid her student loans, and by 2022, she had saved $30,000 in cash toward a home.

Carter, whose family has lived in Texas for eight generations, told Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico at an Oct. 14 CNN town hall that when she went house hunting, a family from California put an additional $80,000 down in cash.

“We cannot afford a house,” she told him.

Talarico said he was hearing the same frustration from young Texans who had been promised that working hard, going to school, and playing by the rules would deliver the American dream.

With the Nov. 3 midterms just days away, new polling suggests that frustration is rippling across how young Americans see their economic future.

The Harvard Youth Poll reported on Oct. 22 that, for the first time in its history, Americans aged 18 to 29 are more likely to expect to be financially worse off than their parents—27% to 25%. Two years ago, optimism led pessimism by 8 percentage points.

That negative sentiment is shared across generations. Nearly 9 in 10 registered voters want Democrats and Republicans in Congress to work together to make housing more affordable, according to an April Bipartisan Policy Center/Advocus survey.

But the harder question is what “affordable” should mean—and for whom.

Housing makes that question especially difficult to answer because it's both a necessary expense and an asset—compare that to a lower grocery bill, which is just cheaper.

A lower home price, meanwhile, also means someone else's asset—and often, their greatest source of wealth—is worth less.

And the generations enter that conflict from very different positions.

Just 24% of adults aged 18 to 29 owned their homes in 2025, according to the Federal Reserve, compared with 83% of adults 60 and older. Among homeowners, median home equity was $90,000 for those under 35 in 2022, compared with $250,000 for those 65 and older.

Some of that gap reflects age: Older households have had more time to let their equity grow. But today's younger households also face a worse entry point.

From 2019 to 2024, inflation-adjusted home values rose 30% while incomes for households headed by someone under 40 rose 9%. Over that period, the share of renter households under 40 earning enough to afford the monthly cost of a typical home fell from 56% to 37%.

And once accumulated, housing wealth can become an advantage in the next purchase. Repeat buyers put a median of 23% down in 2025, compared with 10% for first-time buyers, and 30% of repeat buyers paid entirely in cash.

It can cross generations, too: 26% of younger millennial buyers received down payment help through a gift or loan from a relative or friend, according to the National Association of Realtors®.

So the divide is not literally old versus young. It's between households already connected to housing wealth and those trying to enter the system without it—a divide that maps heavily onto age and can be reinforced by what families have available to pass down.

That leaves policymakers trying to solve two problems at once: Help younger households acquire the asset without eroding the wealth older households have accumulated in it.

“From a home price perspective, these goals are incompatible,” explains Joel Berner, senior economist at Realtor.com®. “Prices can't both go up for existing homeowners and down for prospective ones.”

An August working paper from economists Joshua Coven, Sebastian Golder, Arpit Gupta, and Abdoulaye Ndiaye shows just how complex delivering relief is.

The researchers found that lower property taxes make homes cheaper for existing owners to hold, but some of the benefit can become capitalized into higher prices while giving older owners more reason to stay put.

In their model, raising California property taxes to Texas levels lowered prices and required down payments while increasing holding costs for older owners. Young homeownership rose; elderly homeownership fell.

That same economic conflict is reinforced at the ballot box.

More than 6 in 10 people aged 50 and older voted in the 2022 midterms, according to AARP, compared with roughly 3 in 10 people younger than 30. An October survey across 43 competitive congressional districts found 83% of voters 50 and older were extremely motivated to vote, compared with 66% of those under 50.

Affordability is motivating older voters, too. AARP found rising prices and affordability were their top concern in those districts.

But affordability looks different once someone owns the asset.

“The ancillary costs of homeownership are more impactful on existing [homeowners] than home asking prices or current mortgage rates,” Berner says.

Property taxes, insurance premiums, and HOA fees can make a home more expensive to keep even as the owner accumulates equity. Slowing those costs can help incumbent owners, Berner says, although there remains a risk that some of the savings eventually become reflected in higher home values.

Voters of all ages will be weighing that confluence of immediate and knock-on effects across the country in November.

In Florida, voters will consider Amendment 3, which would, among other changes, expand the state's homestead exemption for nonschool property taxes.

An AIF Center for Political Strategy poll conducted by McLaughlin & Associates found 36% support among voters younger than 30, compared with 55% among voters older than 40. A separate Mason-Dixon survey found a smaller gap in the same direction: 48% support among voters aged 18 to 34 versus 56% among those 65 and older.

Of course, those polls don't explain why voters split by age. But they're telling in a state where the economics of ownership already depend on when someone entered the market.

In Miami, the owner of a newly purchased median-valued home would pay about $10,024 a year in property taxes, according to the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy. Someone who bought an equally valuable home in 2012 would pay just $3,166, largely because assessment limits restrict how quickly taxable values rise for existing owners.

Even so, NAR estimates that Amendment 3's tax savings could move roughly 40,000 additional renter households under 45 above its income-qualification threshold for a starter home.

It's a clear illustration of why the generational conflict is so difficult to untangle.

California's Proposition 37, meanwhile, starts from the other end. It would authorize up to $25 billion in revenue bonds for loans covering as much as 17% of the purchase price of qualifying newly constructed homes. Participating homeowners would repay the loans.

“Every day, we hear from middle-class Californians who simply want a fair shot at homeownership for themselves and their families—but affordability continues to stand in the way,” California Association of Realtors President Tamara Suminski told Realtor.com in October. “By providing down payment assistance loans—at no cost to taxpayers—this measure tackles one of the biggest barriers and brings the dream of homeownership closer to reality.”

President Donald Trump has stated that goal explicitly.

At a January Cabinet meeting, Trump said he wanted to make homes easier to buy without reducing the wealth existing homeowners had accumulated.

“I don't want those values to come down,” he said. He later argued that lower interest rates could “keep the values up for the people that have housing and let other people buy housing.”

Berner agrees lower mortgage rates would quickly improve first-time-buyer affordability. But stronger demand would likely push prices higher, he says, eroding some of the gain.

“The longer answer is housing supply growth and income growth,” he says.

Income growth offers the least direct conflict among generations. If earnings rise faster than inflation and home prices, prospective buyers gain purchasing power without requiring existing owners to lose equity.

Supply attacks the other side of the equation by reducing scarcity—but its success can make the trade-off more visible.

“As far as proven results, Austin, TX, is a great example of supply responding quickly to demand, especially in the new-construction starter segment,” Berner says. “However, home price softness in the metro is illustrative of the trade-off for existing homeowners.”

He also points to Montana, which is trying to make that supply response easier, with reforms allowing more accessory dwelling units, multifamily development, and other forms of housing that Berner says could create more starter inventory.

The two routes suggest the generational goals are not completely irreconcilable. But Carter’s experience points to one more complication: Affordability is relative.

Remember that California family who put down an additional $80,000 in cash? They weren’t alone.

California Policy Lab research found San Francisco Bay Area movers landed in neighborhoods where home values were about 50% lower than those they left, and became homeowners at higher rates.

So a buyer priced out of one market may become the stronger bidder in another—shifting affordability pressure rather than eliminating it.
