#3166. Amazon's anti-tokenmaxxing memo says engineers should be pickier about using AI

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon laid out its AI usage guidelines for software engineers in a memo to staffers, and discouraged employees from turning to "tokenmaxxing" in their push to embrace artificial intelligence tools.

The memo, which was circulated internally in September and posted to the company's website on Tuesday, revises how the company defines the role of software development engineers and managers in the age of AI.

"We're just seeing the beginning of AI's effect on software engineering," the company wrote. "While we have a vision for what the future looks like, there's a lot that's still emerging. We are certain that whatever future best practices are, AI will be at the center of software engineering practice."

Under CEO Andy Jassy, Amazon has rapidly integrated AI across the company's sprawling business operations. Jassy has previously predicted that AI will "change the scope and speed" of day-to-day work, and potentially shrink its corporate workforce in the next few years through automation, though he's noted it will also create "other jobs."

As employees rush to adopt AI tools, companies have grappled with the technology's surging costs and how to reliably measure whether the technology leads to a productivity boost or other valuable returns on investment.

Companies have recently cracked down on tokenmaxxing, in which developers use AI as much as possible with little regard for output. Some companies have set limits on employees' usage of tokens, or the units of data that are processed and generated by AI models, in a bid to slow rising AI bills. Others have switched to cheaper, open-source models for certain tasks.

An Amazon spokesperson said the company hasn't instituted caps on employees' token usage. Amazon in May shut down an internal leaderboard after discovering employees were tokenmaxxing to climb the ranks.

In the memo posted Tuesday, Amazon said metrics like token usage, the rate of adoption of AI tools, and code changes or reviews remain "deeply flawed" ways of measuring teams or organizations' AI usage, adding that they can lead to higher costs and too much complexity.

It stopped short of telling staffers to ignore those metrics altogether, however. Token usage and code generation should be considered "with care and judgment" alongside other data, Amazon said.

"We believe that it is best to measure, and be measured by, long-term outcomes," the company wrote.

Marc Brooker, a vice president and distinguished engineer at Amazon Web Services, told CNBC in an interview that the company has looked to metrics such as "feature velocity" to measure how AI is improving engineer productivity.

The metric looks at things like, "how many launches are we doing, how many successful changes are we making in production," he said.

"Broadly, we think about this as a return on investment," Brooker said. "If there is an important problem to solve for our customers, important problem to solve for our business, we're going to invest tools, time, tokens, human effort, etc. into those."

He added, "What we don't want to see is wasted effort where we are going and spending those tokens solving problems that don't matter and sometimes it's hard to know, so we do need to have some appetite for experimentation."

Amazon also discouraged staffers from using AI when it doesn't make sense for a given task.

"If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing," Amazon said in the memo.

The company added, "An engineer who can explain where AI does not yet help their work is demonstrating the judgement we are asking for."

If Seattle is anywhere close to contention, it's a good bet that John Schneider is going to seek out a trade that can give his team a midseason boost.

This marks the fourth consecutive year that Schneider has engineered a significant swap ahead of the deadline, with defensive lineman Leonard Williams, linebacker Ernest Jones IV and wide receiver Rashid Shaheed coming aboard in recent seasons. Williams and Jones became staples of Mike Macdonald's defense, but Shaheed has yet to spark the receiving corps as Schneider might have hoped he would.

There shouldn't be much question as to Nelson's potential impact on an uneven offense.

Having immediately placed himself among the NFL's elite tier of blockers upon his entry to the league and then remained at that level throughout his career, the eight-time Pro Bowler might still get overlooked at times. Seattle, however, is sure to appreciate the consistently high standard he's seldom strayed from.

Through seven games, the Seahawks rank just 27th in run-block win rate, according to ESPN. While the backfield was clearly hampered by first-round Jadarian Price being sidelined by a chest injury and Zach Charbonnet remaining out while recovering from January's torn anterior cruciate ligament, the most pervasive problems were up front. And with left tackle Charles Cross set to miss time after suffering a high-ankle sprain against the Chiefs, Seattle was liable to have too many weak links capable of being exploited by opposing defenses.

Sam Darnold also stands to benefit greatly, as Nelson's penchant for keeping interior pressure at bay should provide massive dividends in keeping the quarterback from pressing too much.

Figuring out how to deploy this reconfigured interior line to the best of its ability will be a challenge for Macdonald and first-year offensive coordinator Brian Fleury, as left guard Grey Zabel likely will have to shift to another spot. Still, Seattle now has a chance to replicate last season's trajectory, when the flagging run game finally took off down the stretch and in the playoffs to power the title run.

How Schneider approaches negotiations with Nelson after this season could be fascinating, as the decision-maker who famously once said guards were widely "overdrafted" and "overpaid" now might have to grapple with the position's wild top-of-market deals. But Schneider's past acquisitions haven't been rentals, and it's hard to believe he approached this move merely expecting a short-term bump.

This grade might need to be revisited after the Seahawks figure out their long-term commitment to Nelson. For now, though, the acquisition makes Seattle significantly more formidable as it remains among a select few leading Super Bowl contenders.

Turning a franchise cornerstone into a third-rounder and change might not seem like much, especially for anyone who was counting on a compensatory pick at that level in 2028 if Nelson walked this offseason. But Indianapolis couldn't be sure of that return, and bolstering their resources for an immediate rebuild will help expedite the process of pivoting from the Chris Ballard era.

Is this the indication of a sell-off over the next two weeks? With the Colts seemingly letting go of any chance of clawing back this season under Shane Steichen, it certainly stands to reason that more moves to part with established veterans should follow. Defensive tackles DeForest Buckner and Grover Stewart should be no-brainers to send to contenders. And as much as it might pain the organization to part with running back Jonathan Taylor, selling high on the three-time Pro Bowler might be a necessary step for a team still facing a shortage of draft capital in light of the Sauce Gardner deal.

If this ends up signaling the start of a full-on fire sale, the grade warrants elevating. Even in isolation, however, this was an appropriate move for a franchise facing a reckoning.

#36. Everything Apple announced so far: M6 MacBook Pro, M6 iMac, and an OLED iPad mini

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple's M6 Macs finally arrived on Tuesday, two weeks after the company's "Welcome Home" smart home launch. The announcement, part of another slate of press releases Apple dropped ahead of its private Pro and Tell event on Wednesday, Oct. 28, includes a new 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6, a refreshed iMac with M6, and a round of AI features for Apple Creator Studio, the company's subscription bundle of creative apps.

The timing isn't a surprise. When the M6 MacBooks were missing from the Oct. 13 home announcements, Mashable reported that Bloomberg's Mark Gurman still expected them in October. For the time being, though, outlets like MacRumors report that the expected touchscreen MacBook Pro won't be announced until Wednesday — so check back here after tomorrow for more details.

In the meantime, Apple's M6 chip is the main upgrade here. Both machines keep their existing designs, and Apple's pitch leans hard on running AI locally. According to Apple, M6 adds two CPU cores and two GPU cores over its predecessor, puts Neural Accelerators in every GPU core, and pairs them with a new dual 16-core Neural Engine and up to 170GB/s of memory bandwidth. Apple frames the result as a platform for running large language models (LLMs) on-device while keeping data private.

It’s the same AI push Apple made with its home devices. As Mashable noted earlier this month, Gurman has argued the AI boom caught Apple off guard. Both Macs ship with macOS 27 Golden Gate, which brings Siri AI, Apple’s rebuilt assistant. Apple says it can draw on personal context from messages, email, and photos, answer questions on virtually any topic, and act across apps.

Here's everything Apple announced for the Mac on Oct. 27:

The headline product is the 14-inch MacBook Pro, which gets M6's 12-core CPU. Apple says M6 features the world’s fastest CPU core. Apple claims the 12-core GPU is up to 30 percent faster at graphics and up to 25 percent faster at AI workloads than M5, while the Neural Engine is up to 1.9x faster. Owners of an M1 model could see bigger jumps: Apple cites up to 11x faster graphics and up to 13x faster AI performance.

Apple's AI benchmarks are the more telling numbers. It says LLM prompt processing delivers a first token up to 1.3x faster than the M5 model, with similar gains in Blender rendering and Xcode builds.

Battery life is rated at up to 24 hours, which Apple calls the longest ever in a Mac, and fast charging reaches 50 percent in 30 minutes with a 96W adapter. Memory starts at 16GB and tops out at 32GB. Wireless connectivity upgrades to Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6, and the laptop supports up to two external displays.

Speaking of displays, the MacBook Pro has a Liquid Retina XDR display (with up to 1,600 nits of peak HDR brightness and a nano-texture option), a 12MP Center Stage camera, six speakers, and the same ports, including HDMI, MagSafe 3, three Thunderbolt 4 ports, and an SDXC slot.

The 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6 starts at $1,999, or $1,899 with education pricing, and comes in space black and silver. Apple Upgrade leases start at $38.99 per month for 36 months. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, in the U.S. and 34 other countries and regions, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.

The iMac skips M5 entirely, jumping from M4 straight to M6. Apple says the CPU and GPU each gain two cores, with configurations offering up to 12 cores apiece, delivering up to 2x faster graphics than M4. Again, Apple’s big claim is the iMac’s ability to run AI locally. The company says the iMac runs on-device AI up to 5x faster than M4, including prompt processing in LM Studio. For anyone still on an Intel iMac, Apple reports an overall performance gain of up to 18x.

Connectivity gets some of the more practical upgrades. Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 come standard, available 2.5Gb Ethernet is up to 2.5x faster than before, and Apple says SSD speeds have doubled. Up to four Thunderbolt 4 ports support two 6K external displays.

The rest of the iMac is unchanged, including the 24-inch 4.5K Retina display, 12MP Center Stage camera, color-matched accessories, and seven colors: blue, green, pink, yellow, purple, orange, and silver.

The iMac with M6 starts at $1,499 ($1,449 for education) with a 10-core CPU and GPU, and $1,699 ($1,599 for education) with a 12-core CPU and GPU. That base price matches what the M4 iMac currently sells for after a $200 increase in June, according to MacRumors. Apple Upgrade leases start at $28.99 per month for 36 months. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.

Apple also gave its new Macs more AI software to run. Apple Creator Studio adds intelligent assistants (in beta) to Logic Pro for Mac and to Keynote, Pages, and Numbers. Notably, Apple says those assistants are built on a combination of its own models and OpenAI’s. Keynote, Pages, and Numbers also gain Model Context Protocol (MCP) support, which lets third-party AI agents read documents, make edits, and run automated actions inside the apps.

Elsewhere, Final Cut Pro for Mac now includes MotionVFX's library of effects, transitions, and templates, and Pixelmator Pro adds Generate Fill for editing selected parts of an image using text prompts. Logic Pro's Stem Splitter can now separate drums.

Apple's week of announcements technically started Monday with the iPad mini, which Apple calls the tablet's "biggest update ever." The headline change is a larger 8.5-inch OLED display that fits in the same footprint, thanks to 24 percent thinner bezels. Apple says peak brightness reaches 1,200 nits, more than double that of the previous model, and a nano-texture option is available.

The A20 Pro chip follows the same local AI script as the M6. It has Neural Accelerators in each GPU core, and Apple claims up to 4.5x faster AI performance than the previous generation. The tablet is also thinner, at 5.4mm, and adds IP67 water and dust resistance, a 12MP front camera on the landscape edge, and Apple's N1 wireless and C2 cellular chips.

The iPad mini starts at $699 for Wi-Fi and $849 for Wi-Fi + Cellular (education pricing starts at $649), and comes in midnight and starlight. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.
