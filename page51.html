#5401. Future Cleantech Architects Has a Shipping Factsheet Worth Reading

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shipping decarbonization presents policymakers with a bewildering range of choices. Batteries, biofuels, methanol, ammonia, hydrogen, wind assistance, shore power, nuclear propulsion and onboard carbon capture all compete for attention, despite serving very different vessel types and operating requirements. Future Cleantech Architects (FCA), a German nonprofit think tank that evaluates climate technologies and translates technical research into guidance for policymakers, has done a good job of bringing those differences into focus. Its October 2026 shipping factsheet is part of The Basics & The Gaps series. Three visually rich pages cover the industry’s emissions, fuels, technologies and infrastructure challenges with considerably more care than many much longer reports.

FCA commissioned TFIE Strategy to conduct a paid, independent peer review of a July draft. The version reviewed was already well researched and thoughtfully designed. The feedback mostly involved the way readers could interpret comparisons drawn from different system boundaries, along with the prominence given to technologies at very different stages of commercial development. FCA’s published edition incorporates substantial improvements in several of those areas. Its analysts deserve the credit for the final editorial choices.

The marine fuels table is particularly useful. LNG and LPG are identified as fossil fuels, while lifecycle emissions, local pollution, costs, technology readiness and toxicity receive more consistent treatment. These distinctions matter when methane leakage, synthetic fuel production pathways and the source of electricity can materially change the emissions associated with a particular fuel. Policymakers considering infrastructure subsidies need to understand those differences before treating a nominally alternative fuel as a climate solution.

I also welcomed the stronger treatment of batteries. FCA uses a 1,100 TEU container ship operating between Spain and North Africa to explore propulsion options beyond the familiar short ferry route. Charging infrastructure, modular batteries, port calls and vessel operations all influence the practical opportunity. Ammonia toxicity, nuclear propulsion and onboard carbon capture also receive more appropriate qualifications. The result is a factsheet that takes engineering possibilities seriously while acknowledging the considerable work required to turn them into commercial systems.

FCA’s accompanying website raises an important question that deserves even greater prominence in future work: how much fuel will the fleet actually need? Today’s vessels are overwhelmingly dependent on oil-derived fuels, but the future mix of cargoes, ships and routes will change. Efficiency improvements and electrification will also affect the amount of liquid fuel that must be supplied. Calculating the alternative fuels required to replace today’s consumption provides a useful reference, although it is a different exercise from forecasting the market that will exist after those changes.

That distinction can affect decisions about fuel-production capacity, port infrastructure and public subsidies. In my longer TFIE Strategy Briefing assessment, I examine how FCA’s figures should be interpreted, what my maritime transition scenarios suggest about future fuel requirements, and how evidence of orders and technical capability differs from demonstrated emissions reductions.

#3656. Netflix's Matthew Perry Documentary Is The Tribute The Friends Star Deserves

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Matthew Perry's death at the age of 54 in 2023 came as a shock to everyone, including fans who had only recently witnessed the actor reveal the depths of his struggles with addiction. But the new Netflix documentary "The One About Matthew Perry" is here to pay tribute to the beloved "Friends" star in a way that not only does justice to his legacy, but reminds us all that his life was not entirely defined by his personal struggles.

In 2021, we saw a version of Perry we didn't quite recognize appear on the "Friends" reunion special. The year after, he released his candid memoir, "Friends, Lovers, and the Big Terrible Thing," in which he revealed the extent of his battles with addiction in a book that was, at times, a harrowing read. The following year, he was gone.

Perry's death was a deeply sad end to a life that had brought so much joy to generations of TV audiences. For those who grew up with "Friends," and therefore had an element of Perry's inimitable comedic sensibility permanently ingrained in their psyches, all of this felt like a kind of grim alternate reality — as if we were living in the wrong timeline. Not only was Perry gone, but our collective memory of his lovable comedic persona had been darkened by his bravely honest account of having been living in a personal hell for much of his life. To be sure, it was crucial that Perry spoke out about that personal hell. It's just that it was inescapably sad to hear. But while "The One About Matthew Perry" is also a hard watch that doesn't shy away from its subject's hardships, it's also a beautiful reminder of his impact on his friends, family, and fans.

Despite being world famous and beloved by an entire generation who grew up on "Friends," Matthew Perry still doesn't get the credit he deserves. His comedic style is often boiled down to his signature cadence, which has taken on a catchphrase-like aura in the years since "Friends" went off the air. But as Chandler Bing, Perry was as influential to a generation's developing comedic sensibility as any other comedy icon of the era.

When he passed away, it wasn't just sad. There was a vague sense of foreboding about the whole thing, but it was due to more than just the criminal circumstances surrounding his death. Perhaps it was the fact it came at a time when so much of the culture in which Perry came to prominence was rapidly fading. It's no secret that the world in which millennials grew up simply doesn't exist in the 2020s. Modern day culture is more fractured than it's ever been, and many institutions of our youth have been undermined, as have many figureheads of the era. The worst revelations of the documentary "Quiet on Set: The Dark Side of Kids TV," for example, were emblematic of this erosion of a time that, viewed from the 2020s, seems like a dream — or perhaps, given the aforementioned revelations, a nightmare hidden beneath a veneer of contentment.

Perry's passing almost seemed like a symbol of all this. Not only was he gone, it turns out he was dealing with "the big terrible thing" the whole time. But with "The One About Matthew Perry," director Mary Robertson, who co-directed "Quiet on Set" alongside Emma Schwartz, reminds us that Perry's legacy can never truly be defined by his struggles.

Matthew Perry always projected a gregarious and outgoing energy, which somehow made it sadder to discover he was struggling so much. As much as Perry infused "Friends, Lovers, and the Big Terrible Thing" with his sense of humor, and as much as you had to admire his bravery and forthrightness in revealing the details of his journey, the overwhelming sense you got from the book was that Perry had truly been through hell. But "The One About Matthew Perry" not only acts a valuable lesson for other streaming documentaries, it serves as an important addendum to the actor's memoir, reminding us that however bleak things were for Perry, he did ultimately make so many people happy.

The most powerful moments of the doc are his family and friends' recollections. Perry's memoir was, of course, all in his voice. But hearing his story told by those who loved him lends a more uplifting aspect to the narrative. That's something his memoir simply couldn't provide, and "The One About Matthew Perry" acts as a fitting tribute to the man by allowing those who truly cared for him to tell us just how happy he made them.

That's not to say the documentary isn't hard to watch at times. But it is a celebration of Perry's life, and in some ways acts as an antidote to the darkness that surrounded his final years. For fans, it's a reassurance that the warm spirit we felt emanating from our TVs every time he was on screen was real, and something that can't ever truly be eroded by time. Perry meant a lot to so many, and after a few years of bleak revelations about his life, it's nice to be reminded of that.

Here are the qualifications for practice squad eligibility and some rules to remember.

A 17th player is permitted as long as he qualifies for the NFL's International Pathway Program.

Players with no accrued seasons (rookies) and those with fewer than nine regular-season games in their lone accrued season.

A maximum of ten players are allowed who have earned no more than two accrued seasons.

Up to six players with unlimited accrued seasons are permitted on the practice squad.

However, the number of players in the last two categories cannot exceed 10 total players.

Teams can elevate up to two practice squad players for each game, but they can still only have 48 players active on game day. After the game, the elevated player(s) will revert to the practice squad. A player can be elevated up to three times, but if he's needed after that, the team would be required to sign him to the 53-man active roster.

Players on practice squads are free agents who can be signed to another team's active roster. A player's current club can't block him from leaving; they can only make a counteroffer to keep him. Ultimately, it's the player's decision. If a player is signed away, his new team must keep him on its 53-man roster for three weeks, but if he's released, he'll revert to his previous team. Players signed to another active roster are also guaranteed three weeks' salary.

A practice squad player can't sign with his team's upcoming opponent unless he does so six days before the upcoming game or ten days prior if his team is on its bye week.

Trey Amos hasn't played much football in the last calendar year.

The second-year Commanders cornerback fractured his fibula on Nov. 9, 2025, during a Week 10 contest against the Detroit Lions. He was placed on injured reserve and embarked on a long, arduous rehabilitation. It lasted through the offseason and into the summer, keeping him away from practice even into training camp.

He returned to practice on Aug. 7, but was put back on the shelf the next day with soreness in the same ankle. He missed the rest of camp and was put on injured reserve on cut-down day, prolonging his absence well into the regular season.

The Commanders eventually got him right, opened his practice window, and activated him before a Week 7 contest against the 49ers. Amos was clearly excited by the prospect, as he showed on Twitter.

And, with that single action, the Commanders secondary got a whole lot better. Washington's most talented cornerback is finally back in the fray. He was on a pitch count against San Francisco, playing 36 snaps while splitting time with Rasul Douglas.

He allowed two receptions for 25 yards on three targets, with two tackles to his credit. That was a good start to his second season.

"I'm telling you, one of the coolest things was there was no flinch in this competitor. And it's easy to drift when you're gonna be out for an extended period, and there just wasn't. The safeties were a mess during Nick Cross' three-game absence. He also returned in Week 6 and made an immediate impact.

"But no, nobody has to put the cape on," Quinn said. "We have to play like we're capable of, for sure. But it definitely is a big deal for him to be back. We were close the week prior, but we knew, man, we want him back from here on. And we took a good first step with that."

Circle Pushes Deeper Into Latin America With USDC Settlement in Colombia

Circle is extending its reach across Latin America, teaming up with payments-technology firm ACI Worldwide to fold USDC settlement into the region’s existing banking rails, starting with a pilot in Colombia.

Announced Tuesday, the collaboration pairs Circle’s USDC infrastructure with ACI Connetic, ACI’s cloud-based payments orchestration platform, to show financial institutions how stablecoin settlement can run alongside traditional payment systems rather than replace them.

The initial focus is Colombia, where ACI built the central infrastructure behind Bre-B, the country’s interoperable instant-payments network. The idea is to test how a digital dollar like USDC can slot into that real-time, account-to-account system to create what the companies call a multi-rail ecosystem.

The framing reflects a shift in how banks are approaching stablecoins. Rather than debating the technology itself, institutions increasingly want to know how digital dollars fit into the treasury operations, governance and settlement processes they already run.

“The conversation around stablecoins is moving from possibility to practicality,” said Craig Ramsey, ACI’s global head of account-to-account payments, describing the effort as a way to integrate regulated stablecoins into workflows banks already trust.

Kash Razzaghi, Circle’s chief commercial officer, said financial institutions across Latin America are increasingly looking at how stablecoins can support the evolution of payments “within the infrastructure they already use,” framing USDC as a bridge between traditional finance and the digital economy.

The move builds on Circle’s steady regional expansion. The company has pushed into dozens of emerging markets as stablecoins go mainstream, and earlier gained a Latin American foothold when Argentina’s Mercado Pago added USDC.

Colombia is a logical next target, given high cross-border remittance volumes and a population well-acquainted with dollar-denominated savings as a hedge against inflation.

Circle positioned the pilot as technology-agnostic: while it runs on USDC, ACI’s platform is built to support other payment networks and digital-asset providers over time. #3291. Ravens discussion: should Eric DeCosta have traded for Quenton Nelson?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Ravens made a blockbuster trade for offensive line a few weeks ago, but they may have been outdone by the Seahawks on Tuesday. Eight-time Pro Bowl guard Quenton Nelson is heading from Indianapolis to Seattle in exchange for a 2027 third-round pick and a 2028 fifth-round pick, per The Ringer's Tom Pelissero.

Baltimore's offensive line issues have been glaring (again) this year, and rookie guard Vega Ioane is now set to miss time with a sprained MCL. Nelson would certainly upgrade the unit, potentially at right guard initially before taking over for John Simpson at left guard after Ioane's return. But after sending a 2028 second-rounder to the Eagles for Cam Jurgens a few weeks ago, does trading even more draft capital make sense considering the Ravens' current and future salary cap picture? Here are Baltimore Beatdown's thoughts

I have always loved Quenton Nelson, so in my heart of hearts, I would have loved to make this trade. But, partially because I am expecting my colleagues to be all for it, I'll play devil's advocate. Seattle's 2027 third and 2028 fifth are valued somewhere in the second-round range – higher or lower depending on how you value the Day 3 pick. But the Seahawks can absorb all of the roughly $10 million Nelson is owed over the remainder of the season. The Ravens cannot, which would force them to restructure current contracts and/or ask the Colts to eat some of Nelson's salary. The former exacerbates an already-untenable 2027 cap situation; the latter would drive the trade price up, likely requiring a second-round pick and potentially a Day 3 kicker to complete the deal.

Nelson is also on an expiring deal, and like it or not, Baltimore has $5.5 million in guaranteed money committed to John Simpson next year. Nelson is definitely an upgrade this year, but can Baltimore get a long-term deal done? If not, second-round value is a high price for a half-season rental, especially considering the sobering reality that the 2026 Ravens may not be a Super Bowl-caliber team. They may need another year to remake their roster under Jesse Minter's new coaching staff to reach their full potential.

The upshot of a rental, though, is worth mentioning. Nelson decidedly pushes Baltimore closer to championship contention, and if he walks in free agency, the Ravens should be in line for at least a fourth-, if not a third-round compensatory pick in 2028. Eric DeCosta absolutely could have gotten this done. But I see why he didn't.

Yes, I believe the Ravens should have traded for Nelson. Nelson has been one of the best offensive linemen in the NFL for his career and should still have several high-end seasons left in him. With Baltimore's offensive line woes and difficulty developing guards in recent years, Eric DeCosta could have solved both guard spots for the foreseeable future with Nelson and Vega Ioane.

I understand wanting to hold on to draft capitol after sending a 2028 second-rounder out the door for Cam Jurgens already, but players of Nelson's caliber are extremely hard to come by, especially for this price. Nelson will demand a top-of-the-market deal after this season, but he is a player worth paying for if you are serious about fixing your offensive line. I was out on the idea when I expected Nelson to net a second or even first-round pick, but I believe this is a missed opportunity to protect Lamar Jackson.

Yes – obviously there is a contractual component that made the Cam Jurgens trade attractive for Baltimore, but in retrospect, it's looking like a fairly significant overpay relative to what the Seahawks gave up for Nelson. A trade for Nelson would've fortified the Ravens offensive line for years to come and given Eric DeCosta the flexibility to address other areas of need on the team.

Unfortunately, I think it was more of a contractual issue for Baltimore. They only have ~$2M in cap space and Nelson is due $10M this year. That's what happens when you pay Marlon Humphrey and Roquan Smith big money! It wouldn't surprise me if a future trade involves off-loading one of them to create more flexibility.

I've been clamoring for the Ravens to grab Quenton Nelson for a while now. He was my biggest trade target at this deadline to turn this team into a contender. Reinforce the offensive line, go all in on protecting Lamar Jackson and running the ball, be an elite offense to win playoff games. The price only costing a 3rd and 5th makes this sting even more.

That being said, I think there's a multitude of reasons the Ravens probably didn't do this trade. They might have been worried about not reaching a long term extension with Nelson and didn't want that price on a rental. They also just might not think this is the year to go all in. The more feasible reason are likely cap cost and priorities. Nelson is due $10M and tbe Ravens only have $2.1M in cap space. While they could have been doing with restructures, extensions, or paying a higher draft capital to get Indy to eat cost, I think there's Ravens could be saving that cap space for the defense. They might be targeting a different Colt in DeForest Buckner or Chargers edge rusher Khalil Mack to bolster their 4-man pass rush since their coverage unit is so iffy. The Ravens might be ok with John Simpson being the weakest link on offensive line. While I know fans will hate it and I would have loved Nelson, provided Ioane comes back, this is not a bad line, especially in pass protection. Jurgens looked good in his debut against Dexter Lawerence, the tackles are steady, and Ioane has been a OROTY-level blocker. They can survive and score 30 points a game with Lamar Jackson, Zay Flowers and that line. They might not survive if defensive improvements don't happen.
