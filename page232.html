#5570. Tesla skipped the most important shareholder question on its earnings call

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla skipped one of its top shareholder questions on its Q3 2026 earnings call: when Hardware 3 owners will find out if they get a free computer upgrade for the unsupervised “Full Self-Driving” they paid for.

The question ranked fifth by shares on Say. Tesla read and answered questions ranked below it. It went past this one without a word.

Tesla takes retail shareholder questions through the Say platform before every earnings call, and shareholders upvote them with their shares. When we previewed the Q3 2026 earnings as submissions closed, the HW3 question sat fifth with 557 votes and 652,900 shares.

“Tesla has acknowledged that HW3 vehicles cannot support unsupervised FSD. When will owners know whether they can upgrade their hardware for free, and when will Tesla provide a clear plan?”

Head of investor relations Travis Axelrod read nine Say questions on Wednesday’s call. Tesla answered the Cybercab scaling question, unsupervised FSD in customer cars, Cybercab ownership, and robotaxi and Optimus unit economics. Then it went on to questions about Optimus training data, Optimus revenue, FSD mapping, and factory production rates.

The Optimus revenue and FSD mapping questions both ranked below HW3 in every ranking published before the call. The Optimus training data one didn’t even make the top 10.

Axelrod closed the segment with “That’s it for the Say.com questions.” No analyst asked about HW3 either, and HW3, AI3, or a retrofit didn’t come up once during the entire call.

The second question Tesla took was “When can we anticipate FSD unsupervised in customer cars?”

Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla’s head of AI, answered that the same software running in Model Y robotaxis and the Cybercab will come to customers with FSD v15, and that “somewhere around Q2 next year” the first customer vehicles could join the Robotaxi fleet.

That’s another delay, a new timeline that history suggests will likely be pushed back, and an HW4 answer. Musk admitted on the Q1 2026 call that “Hardware 3 simply does not have the capability to achieve unsupervised FSD,” citing an eighth of HW4’s memory bandwidth. So every time Tesla gives a date for unsupervised FSD, it’s a date that doesn’t apply to millions of cars Tesla sold as having all the hardware needed for full self-driving.

And HW3 owners have been promised something by Musk on each of the last two calls. In April, he talked about “microfactories” in major cities to swap computers and cameras, and a discounted trade-in. In July, when a Truist analyst asked about it, Musk said upgrading would make sense “at some point” and floated skipping HW4 for a next-gen board or AI5, which goes to Optimus first.

No cost, no timeline, no process. And this quarter, not even a mention.

Tesla sold FSD for up to $15,000 on the promise that its cars already had the hardware. Now it’s facing class actions in the US, China, and Australia, and a Dutch collective claim that grew to nearly 7,000 HW3 owners with a law firm behind it. Documents from Dutch regulator RDW show Tesla only submitted HW4 for FSD approval in Europe.

HW3 owners got a stripped-down FSD v14 “Lite” this year. It’s a supervised driver assist, and it doesn’t come close to what they bought.

We don’t think this was an accident. Tesla picks which Say questions it reads and in what order, and it chose to answer a question about Optimus revenue it said it had “kind of already answered” over one backed by 650,000 shares about the product its customers already paid for.

Of course they will avoid the question so they can delay this as long as possible and hope the existing owners will just give up or the cars will just work themselves out of the market with write offs over time. That is a lot of money Tesla would have to spend to upgrade the HW that they never intend to.

Any answer Tesla gives on HW3 has a dollar figure attached, and it’s probably a big one: replacing the computer and cameras in what we estimate to be around 4 million cars, or compensating owners. Every statement on it can also end up quoted in court. Saying nothing is cheaper, at least this quarter.

But Tesla can’t have it both ways. It can’t tell shareholders that unsupervised FSD is coming to customer cars by Q2 2027 while ignoring the owners who bought that exact feature years ago and whose cars will never get it. Many of them paid up to $15,000 upfront for it.

The HW3 upgrade isn’t an engineering problem at this point. Musk already said the hardware can’t do it. It’s a question of whether Tesla will pay to honor what it sold, and right now the answer looks like silence. Will an HW3 owner ever get a real plan before a judge forces one? I bet on nope.

#5306. JPMorgan's Tokenized Money Market Funds Go Live on Solana

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The investment management arm of Wall Street banking giant JPMorgan Chase has formally launched its money market funds on the Solana blockchain.

Two of them (My OnChain Net Yield Fund (MONY) and JPMorgan OnChain Liquidity–Token Money Market Fund (JLTXX)) are now accessible to qualified U.S. investors, per an October 22 press release. Investors can access the funds via JPMorgan's Morgan Money.

JPMorgan's launch on Solana follows its initial tokenized fund launch on the Ethereum network, marking further progress on the bank's blockchain-based financial products.

It enables institutional investors to manage tokenized investments through one platform, without the need to use various platforms for different blockchains.

Tokenized money market funds are blockchain-based tokens that represent conventional investment fund ownership. The goal is more efficient asset transfers and record-keeping.

They differ from cryptocurrencies like SOL: these tokens represent an interest in investment products, not native blockchain currencies.

MONY and JLTXX cater to different groups of institutional investors.

Launched on Ethereum in December 2025, MONY is a private placement fund that offers eligible investors U.S. dollar yields.

JLTXX is a U.S.-registered government money market fund meant to facilitate stablecoin issuers' reserve management needs under the GENIUS Act.

Ethereum received its first tokenized JLTXX launch in May 2026, when JPMorgan invested $100 million of its own money into it.

With the two products, JPMorgan is now expanding the availability of its tokenized financial services on various blockchains, including Solana.

JPMorgan's tokenized funds launch on Solana is the latest development for the blockchain as it attracts traditional institutions to tokenize real-world assets.

JPMorgan Asset Management had $4.8 trillion in assets under management as of September 30, 2026, which reflects the financial scale of the institution behind the latest Solana integration.

The company has not shared its capital allocation numbers for either fund on Solana.

That era feels a million years away for many reasons, including the complete obliteration of the moviemaking landscape. With the Disney-Fox merger, the historic Big Six studios shrunk down to five, and now, with Paramount’s acquisition of Warner Bros., down to four. Meanwhile, the streamers—Netflix, Apple, Amazon—entered the game and changed all the rules.

What has also changed just as dramatically is who sits on top. Amid a flurry of cuts the past couple of years, the number of women in the highest ranks at the major studios has dwindled, and grows fewer by the day. And in just the past few weeks, it’s been a bloodbath.

“It is hard to see so many accomplished women being let go. Yet this is bigger than any one executive’s departure. If you look beyond individual headlines, there’s a larger pattern,” Women in Film CEO Kirsten Schaffer tells Vanity Fair. “We’re deeply concerned that with all of the consolidation we’re losing ground for women and people of color to attain and retain decision-making power.”

Last week, Netflix launched its largest round of job cuts since 2022, laying off about 1,000 staffers, 6% of its global workforce. The most surprising sack was chief content officer Bela Bajaria, who had been with the streaming giant since 2016. After being promoted to the role in 2023, she oversaw Netflix’s TV series, movies, and live events globally. She will be replaced by former Disney and Fox executive Peter Rice.

The Netflix layoffs included other high-profile firings, like head of film Dan Lin, though his exit had been rumored for some time. Bajaria was a far bigger shock, given her long tenure and her success expanding the streamer’s global footprint and live events business. She seemed like a company girl.

Netflix’s cuts come on the heels of a slew of high-level executive exits as the Paramount and Warner Bros. merger finally comes to fruition. A week before the close of the October 6 merger, Cindy Holland announced in a staff memo that she would be departing Paramount Skydance. Holland, a former Netflix exec, hadn’t been there long: She was brought in as chair of direct-to-consumer by David Ellison in August 2025 when the Paramount Skydance deal closed. Per Deadline, she stated in the note that Ellison was “optimizing for HBO stability,” which, reading between the lines, meant that HBO chief Casey Bloys would run streaming for both Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery when the merger closed.

By October 1, Pamela Abdy and Mike De Luca were reportedly out as well. The former co-chairs and CEOs of the Warner Bros. Motion Picture Group had brought the studio 30 Oscar nominations and 11 wins last year with hits like Sinners and One Battle After Another. After that huge year, their contracts were renewed that October, but 2026 has been much rockier for the pair, with The Bride! and Digger both stumbling at the box office. Paramount film heads Dana Goldberg and Josh Greenstein will oversee films for both movie studios after the merger, with a goal to release 30 movies each year. They hit the ground running after the merger in 2025, with a new film from Timothée Chalamet and James Mangold, a first-look deal with Wicked director Jon M. Chu, and a four-year deal with Stranger Things creators Matt and Ross Duffer. But with Warner Bros. coming into the fold, they’ll have even more oversight as they’re tasked with shaping the landscape for both studios.

Yes, there were high-profile layoffs of men too. Along with Warner Bros.’s De Luca and Netflix’s Lin, Netflix’s film VP Doug Belgrad was also let go. But with so few women in greenlight positions already, the loss of three influential leaders is a sting to both Hollywood and audiences alike. “It’s like a brutal game of musical chairs,” says Hunger Games producer Nina Jacobson, who was previously president of the Buena Vista Motion Pictures Group before launching her production company Color Force. “There have always been more men at the very top than women, and you feel that more than ever as the chairs start being taken away.”

Mergers always seem to hit high-ranking female executives hardest. After Disney acquired Fox in 2019, chairman and CEO of 20th Century Fox Stacey Snider stepped down. And after the 2022 WarnerMedia-Discovery merger, Ann Sarnoff, who in 2019 became the first woman chair and CEO of Warner Bros., was also pink-slipped. Neither of them worked at another major studio again. “Films are so hard to make that most of them are the result of at least one person on the studio side who is willing to fight for it,” says a producer. “And I have watched many of the female studio executives around town get tired of fighting.”

The ink is barely dry on the Skydance merger, and there will inevitably be many more layoffs to come as Skydance executives attempt to deliver on a promised $6 billion in cuts. One peek at the Skydance leadership website, and it’s easy to spot that Goldberg is currently the only female executive working in entertainment at the top ranks. (Of the 16 other execs listed, three others are women: chief marketing officer Rebecca Mall, CBS News editor in chief Bari Weiss, and chief communications officer Melissa Zukerman.)

Along with Goldberg, there are only a few other women left standing with that level of control in the film space now. Chairman of NBCUniversal Entertainment Donna Langley remains one of the most powerful female studio execs with a proven record of hits, including The Odyssey and The Super Mario Galaxy Movie this year. At Disney, when Josh D’Amaro became Bob Iger’s successor, Dana Walden became president and chief creative officer, a new position that included the addition of film studio operations to her already expansive purview of TV, streaming, gaming, and global marketing. And Courtenay Valenti, head of film, streaming, and theatrical at Amazon MGM, just promoted two women—Julie Rapaport and Alana Mayo—as heads of film, production, and development.

Tom Cruise, Pamela Abdy, and Michael De Luca at the Digger world premiere in London.

The consolidation of power into fewer hands (with most of those being male) is not without consequences. Individuals who have greenlight power not only decide which stories get told, but at what budget, and who gets hired. Women are still horribly underrepresented as directors. Last year, according to the USC Annenberg Inclusion Initiative, the proportion of women working as directors on the 100 top-grossing movies of the year hit a seven-year low, with only nine women out of 111 working as directors on those films. Dr. Stacy L. Smith and Dr. Katherine Pieper, the authors of the initiative, called it a “complete reversal of any progress that was achieved behind the camera over the last few years.”

Even though women are directing independent movies outside of those top 100 of the year (48% of the features in the 2026 Sundance lineup were directed by women), they aren’t directing the big-budget movies that only studios can make. Having more female executives at the top gives more opportunity to female storytellers. “[Women in Film’s] research shows that when women are in leadership positions, they hire more women,” says Schaffer. “Women executives in decision-making roles open doors for the next generation. When those seats disappear, so do the opportunities.”

Back in 2015, Lucasfilm president Kathleen Kennedy told The Guardian that “there’s nothing we’d like more than to find a female director for Star Wars.” It wasn’t until 2023 when two-time Oscar-winning documentary filmmaker Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy was hired to helm a spin-off about Daisy Ridley’s character, Rey. But the project has languished in development. Kennedy gave up her 14-year post earlier this year, handing over the reins to Dave Filoni and Lynwen Brennan.

And having women leading studios isn’t just about giving more women a chance to work on a $200 million movie, or making female-centered stories. It’s about a more inclusive group of decision makers who can therefore make movies that reach more diverse audiences. Hollywood is supposed to be the land of endless stories and possibilities, but without a more inclusive group of people deciding which stories get told, those possibilities will keep shrinking to whatever a narrow few already believe audiences want to see. “I am reduced to counting which of the male studio heads have daughters to understand whether they’ll see value in female-driven propositions they don’t endemically understand,” says the producer of this new landscape.

Hollywood has a history of pushing women out of the executive suite, only to watch them thrive elsewhere. After Pascal was fired as co-chairman of Sony, she went on to become a top producer behind films like Little Women, Challengers, Project Hail Mary, and Greta Gerwig’s next movie, Narnia: The Magician’s Nephew. Perhaps Bajaria, Abdy, and Holland will have just as fruitful next chapters.

The question is whether the industry will build the pipeline to replace them, or if the next generation of women who might have run a studio will simply never get the chance. “In truth, I worry more for the younger folks trying to make their mark in the business than I do about those of us who have stayed in the game for decades,” says Jacobson. “The fewer jobs there are, the less people want to give up their spot, or fear it will be taken, and the fewer opportunities there are for the next generation to rise and get their chance.”

#3895. So What Do We Want From a 'Glee' Reboot?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Did you hear whispers of four-part harmony rustling in the trees? Did you get the urge to do intricate choreography in a fluorescently lit room? Did you suddenly start believing, and did you even manage to hold on to that feeling? Well, that would make sense, because on Wednesday Disney+ and Hulu announced that Glee—Ryan Murphy’s zeitgeisty show-choir series—is officially being rebooted, with Variety reporting that none other than Lea Michele will reprise her star-making role as Rachel “She May Be Difficult, but, Boy, Can She Sing” Berry. Naturally, musical-loving millennials lost their collective minds at the news.

With the news that Girls is returning to HBO for a five-episode revival series, Glee is the second touchstone show from the 2010s to announce a revival in as many weeks. (Millennials: 2. Zoomers: 0.) Whether either of these things is a good idea creatively is sort of beside the point. The wheels are in motion, and they are hurtling our way faster than a slushy headed straight toward Rachel Berry’s headband-adorned face.

As someone who was voted “most likely to appear on Glee” in their 2011 high school yearbook (true story) and who occasionally listens to a “Glee Cast Version” of a song privately on Spotify (sorry, sometimes they’re better than the originals!), I do feel I have earned the right to make a few demands of what we want to see—and don’t want to see—from a Glee reboot series.

Yes, the series ended with a flash-forward to 2020 (spoilers, I guess?). Rachel Berry is a Tony winner and surrogate for Kurt and Blaine’s baby, and Sue Sylvester is vice president of the United States. (Actually, it’s a bit scary how much Sue Sylvester and JD Vance have in common—two Republican blowhards from the Midwest who ascend to our nation’s second-highest office.) According to Will & Grace reboot rules, none of that is canon. So we can disregard all of the happy endings from the series finale and throw the Gleeks back into the mess where they belong…

Chris Colfer, Lea Michele, Amber Riley, Cory Monteith, Jenna Ushkowitz, Glee, 2009.

Glee is about so many things: Finding your tribe. Hitting a flawless high F while nailing a double pirouette in a peplum top. Faking a pregnancy (we still talk about you, Terri Schuester). It’s also about being a kid with big dreams stuck in a small town. So for that reason we’ve gotta bring the action back to where it all began: William McKinley High School.

I am aware that Glee trades the Buckeye State for the Big Apple in season four, choosing to follow Rachel and company as they try to make it in New York City. It made sense at the time. They had to graduate eventually. But everyone knows it led to diminishing returns. Splitting time between the hustle and bustle of NYC and the glee club back at William McKinley just didn’t quite work. (Although the Sarah Jessica Parker–led mash-up “Let’s Have a Kiki / It’s Turkey Lurkey Time” remains one of the all-time craziest “they did what???” moments in the series.) Also, the Girls revival will have plenty of New York content for millennials to be nostalgic for.

Cory Monteith, Lea Michele, and Dianna Agron, Glee, 2009.

Let’s find a natural way to get them back to where it all began. Maybe some of them never left: Maybe Brittany, the secret math genius she was, is teaching at a local university. And perhaps Blaine and Kurt have settled in a nearby Midwest town and are micro-influencers for their growing antique collection.

Rachel Berry is the trickiest to crack, and as such will be the engine of the show. I think it’s fine to keep her as a Tony-winning diva, who gets run out of New York for bad behavior—maybe she’s caught poisoning her competition before a final callback for a site-specific production of Rent in the East Village—and winds up back at home, Tony in hand, tail between her legs and demanding to be the coach of New Directions. Imagine what a nightmare she’d be as a show-choir director.

Glee had so many cast members and guest stars over the course of its six-season run that it would be impossible to try to get them all back for the revival. Plus, sadly, they aren’t all still with us to return. There are some truly devastating losses from the original Glee cast (RIP Cory Monteith and Naya Rivera), and some deeply disturbing ones as well (the less said about Mark Salling the better). Figuring out who should come back is easier said than done.

Lea Michele, Chris Colfer, Amber Riley, Kevin McHale, Jenna Ushkowitz, Glee, 2009.

With Lea Michele confirmed and Brittany Pierce (Heather Morris) and Sam Evans (Chord Overstreet) teasing on social media that they’re involved, it’s clear they are going to be reaching out to some stars of the original series. Let’s throw in the other day-ones with her: Artie. Jenna. Kurt. Mercedes. Quinn. And Blaine (he basically counts at this point). As for teachers, we gotta have Jane Lynch’s Sue Sylvester, because what is Glee if Jane Lynch isn’t delivering some of the nastiest reads you’ve ever heard? Mr. Schuester is on the fence…would he really still be principal of William McKinley all these years later? (I fear in our current economy the answer is yes.) After much deliberation, Matthew Morrison’s Mr. Schue can return as well on the condition that in his spare time he keeps making Cameos that ultimately become viral TikToks.

Beyond that, I think it’s okay to be picky and choosy about who returns for a prominent role. The Glee reboot should pepper the series with some fun cameos from beloved characters, many of whom got cast in the later shakier seasons, as well as some of the most fun guest stars. Thank God Gwyneth Paltrow started acting again so she can reprise her Emmy-winning role as Holly Holliday, and NeNe Leakes is free to play bronze-medal-winning synchronized-swimming coach Roz Washington.

We also need to leave room for actors who obviously would exist and thrive in the Glee Cinematic Universe (the GCU, if you will), but never got the chance to. Ryan Murphy once teased he was writing a Glee reboot for Beanie Feldstein and Ben Platt. Congrats, you’re both teachers at McKinley High. Rachel Zegler, you’re on tap to be the second coming of Rachel Berry, as frighteningly talented and ambitious as your foremother (I mean, it’s literally in her name).

I also want to leave room for four to six of the best singers that TikTok has to offer, of any age. Ryan Murphy, it’s time to get to scrolling. Which brings us to the next point…

I don’t just want songs, I want mash-ups. The good, old-fashioned 2010s style of mixing up two completely different songs that Glee was famous for. (Matter of fact, Murphy should hire DJ Earworm right now.) We’ve lost this ridiculous art form in the modern era, and Glee can bring it back. And the music should reflect what’s happening in music today. I want to hear Mercedes belt out Chappell Roan’s “The Subway” while Kurt croons “Choosin’ Texas” by Ella Langley. Imagine Blaine singing Noah Kahan’s “Stick Season” on top of Quinn Fabray singing Stella Lefty’s “Boston” and what that might do to the internet. Hell, let’s have Rachel Berry take on a Taylor Swift–Olivia Rodrigo collab in one rousing musical number and destroy the internet once and for all. Mash-ups aside, maybe Lea Michele will be the first person to figure out if it is technically possible to “sing” a Katseye song.

The possibilities for a Glee reboot are endless, and they will scare you. Ryan Murphy, the ball is in your court. Just don’t let our fragile, millennially cringe hearts down, I beg.

The total solar eclipse on Aug. 2, 2027, has an ancient feel. It will certainly be a celestial event for the ages — at 6 minutes 23 seconds, it will feature the longest totality on land of the 21st century and the longest anywhere until 2114. However, its point of greatest eclipse occurs just a few miles from Thebes, the magnificent religious and political capital of ancient Egypt's Middle Kingdom and New Kingdom.

On the east bank of the mighty Nile is Luxor, the great city of the living, with its massive temple complexes of Karnak and Luxor. On its west bank is the city of the dead, which includes the Valley of the Kings and the Temple of Hatshepsut. Modern Luxor, population 285,000, is set to become a focal point for eclipse chasers on Aug. 2, 2027, and will attract many thousands of international visitors. They'll come for the forecasted 0% chance of clouds and the era-defining duration of totality, but also to tour ancient Egypt's temples and monuments.

The chances of actually witnessing totality at any of the major temples are probably slim. Still, almost every eclipse chaser headed to Luxor will want to tour them before or after the eclipse. Visitors should be prepared for extreme August heat and check official opening and access arrangements before planning to watch the eclipse at any archaeological site. Certified eclipse glasses are essential during the partial phases; only during totality, when the sun is completely covered, is it safe to look without them.

Not all of ancient Egypt will experience totality. Egypt's top-ranking monument, the Pyramids at Giza, near Cairo, lies north of the path of totality and will get a 95% partial solar eclipse. Abu Simbel, on the shores of Lake Nasser south of Aswan, will also see a 92% partial solar eclipse. No matter, Luxor is home to some of the world's most impressive and well-preserved ancient monuments. Here are its temples, necropolises and palaces — and those nearby, also in the 160-mile (257-kilometer)-wide path of totality — that will have the moon's umbral shadow fall upon them on Aug. 2, 2027.
