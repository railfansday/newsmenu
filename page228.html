#4568. ADHD Medications May Help Ease Menopausal Brain Fog

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Medications commonly used to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may help reduce certain symptoms of menopausal brain fog, according to a review presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The research suggests that both lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) and atomoxetine (Strattera) may improve self-reported executive function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal people. However, they showed little to no effect on objective cognitive tests.

While this scoping review showed promising results, experts caution that it may be too early to begin recommending these medications to females experiencing menopause.

Sara Vaughn, MD, Chief Medical Officer at Clair Health, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are interesting and hypothesis-generating, but they do not yet establish ADHD medications as a treatment for menopausal brain fog.

Many middle-aged females report experiencing forgetfulness, slow thinking, reduced concentration, and difficulty finding words. This is often referred to as “brain fog,” and is a common occurrence during various stages of menopause.

These symptoms of brain fog also mirror the symptoms of ADHD.

Brain fog may be due to declining or fluctuating levels of estrogen during perimenopause, which affects dopamine and norepinephrine signaling in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus. This can impact memory, focus, and executive functioning.

Despite how disruptive these symptoms can be to quality of life, there is currently no medication approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to manage them. This often leaves clinicians in a position of trying to differentiate between brain fog from menopause and undiagnosed ADHD.

The review analyzed five studies involving females ages 45–60 with their last menstrual period within the past 10 years. The studies all focused on how ADHD medications affect outcome measures of cognition and ADHD severity.

The researchers found that with lisdexamfetamine, scores on the Brown Attention Deficit Disorder Scale (BADDS), a self-report rating scale that measures ADHD symptoms, dropped from 35.7 to 21.2, compared with 29.8 with a placebo. With atomoxetine, BADDS scores decreased from 38.6 to 25.5, compared with 30.1 with a placebo.

There was no significant benefit observed on objective cognitive tests (ex., memory and processing speed).

These findings suggest that certain ADHD medications may improve perceived cognitive symptoms during menopause, although benefits were not demonstrated on objective cognitive tests.

Jennifer Tat, a medical student at the University of Tennessee Health Sciences Center and lead author of the study, noted, “In the population data, symptom severity, not a formal ADHD diagnosis, predicted menopausal burden. Many women were never diagnosed, so relying on a chart diagnosis misses the women most at risk.”

“We have evidence that the menopause transition is associated with measurable brain changes,” said Vaughn. “But no specific brain signature of menopausal brain fog has been established. To study it rigorously, we first need to define what the term means and measure it consistently.”

Limitations of this review include the small number of studies and the small sample sizes. Most of the measures were also self-reported, which leaves room for error.

Also, the FDA has not approved these types of medications for menopausal brain fog, meaning using them for these purposes would be off-label.

“ADHD medications are not without risks, and I think it is too early to use them routinely for menopausal ‘brain fog,’” said Vaughn.

ADHD medications can carry risks, including cardiovascular side effects. Stimulants such as lisdexamfetamine also have the potential for misuse and dependence. These medications should only be used with medical supervision.

“I am a strong proponent of treating the underlying disorder rather than treating isolated symptoms. ADHD and menopausal cognitive symptoms may look alike, but that does not mean they share the same underlying mechanism—or require the same treatment,” noted Vaughn.

Further research, including additional long-term prospective studies, is needed before clinical guidance can be changed

“We hope this opens up the conversations with patients and providers. We recommend clinicians to screen patients for ADHD and, for those who screen positive, refer them to our psychiatric colleagues who have the knowledge and resources to perform a diagnostic test so that those women who have gone undiagnosed with ADHD can get the treatment they need and deserve,” said Tat.

#664. Scientists discover a surprising double benefit of exercise for Parkinson’s

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People with Parkinson's disease who are more physically active may experience slower declines in memory, thinking, and movement, according to a new study published October 7, 2026, in Neurology, the official medical journal of the American Academy of Neurology.

The findings suggest that staying active could offer two important advantages for people living with Parkinson's. However, researchers emphasize that the study identified a connection between physical activity and slower symptom progression. It does not establish that exercise directly causes the disease to progress more slowly.

"Parkinson's disease is a brain disorder that causes movement and cognitive problems that gradually worsen leading to disability and a loss of independence," said study author Dong-Woo Ryu, MD, PhD, of the University of Cincinnati in Ohio. "Previous research has shown associations between physical activity and movement symptoms. Our study found physical activity may have dual benefits, with associations with slower declines in movement and cognition."

Tracking Exercise and Parkinson's Symptoms Over Seven Years

The research involved 294 adults with mild to moderate Parkinson's disease. Participants were 66 years old on average, and researchers monitored their health for up to seven years.

To understand their activity habits, participants completed questionnaires estimating how many hours each week they spent being physically active. The questions covered activities performed at work, around the home, and during hobbies or recreation.

Researchers classified activities by their intensity. Moderate activities included brisk walking, doubles tennis, leisurely swimming, and household tasks such as cleaning windows, vacuuming, or mowing the lawn. More demanding activities included running, cycling at higher speeds, participating in team sports, playing singles tennis, shoveling, and carrying heavy objects.

The team examined physical activity in three ways. First, they calculated the total time participants spent doing moderate or vigorous activities. Second, they estimated overall activity volume by combining duration with intensity, giving greater weight to strenuous exercise. Third, they separately measured the amount of time spent doing vigorous activities.

Based on total weekly activity time, participants were placed into three categories. Those in the least active group reported up to four hours per week, the moderately active group reported four to eight hours, and the most active group reported more than eight hours.

Each year, participants also completed assessments designed to track changes in thinking and memory, along with tests evaluating the severity of their Parkinson's symptoms.

More Activity Linked to Slower Memory Decline

One of the most striking differences emerged in cognitive performance. Researchers used a 30-point test of thinking and memory, with lower scores reflecting poorer cognitive function.

Among participants in the least active group, scores fell by an estimated average of 0.478 points each year. The moderately active group experienced a much smaller annual decline of 0.090 points.

For the most active participants, the estimated average yearly decrease was only 0.007 points, indicating that their cognitive scores remained nearly unchanged over time.

The researchers also examined changes in Parkinson's movement symptoms using an assessment scored from zero to 132. Higher numbers represented more severe symptoms, and participants began the study with an average score of 23.

In the least active group, symptom scores increased by an estimated 0.143 points annually, indicating a slight worsening. By contrast, scores decreased by 0.225 points per year in the moderately active group and by 0.783 points in the most active group.

Even after accounting for participants' age, sex, and how long they had been living with Parkinson's, the researchers found that the most active group experienced slower cognitive decline and less worsening of movement symptoms than the least active group.

The connection was not limited to one measure of exercise. Higher total activity time, greater overall activity volume, and more time spent exercising vigorously were each associated with slower deterioration in both thinking abilities and movement symptoms.

Why Consistent Physical Activity May Matter

The results suggest that remaining physically active over time could be especially important for people with Parkinson's disease. Researchers noted that even relatively small differences in the rate of decline might become more significant when they accumulate over several years.

"The possible benefits of physical activity for people with Parkinson's disease may depend on how long they stay active and whether they continue exercising over time," said Ryu. "While our study found small differences between activity levels, these differences may become more meaningful over time. Since participants generally had good thinking and memory skills at the start of the study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are needed."

The researchers also identified an important limitation. Physical activity levels were based on participants' own reports rather than direct measurements, meaning some estimates may have been inaccurate.

Although the findings strengthen the evidence connecting physical activity with better long-term outcomes in Parkinson's disease, additional research will be needed to determine whether exercise itself can slow the progression of symptoms.

Funding for the study came from a grant provided by the Gardner Family Foundation and the Precision Medicine in Neurodegenerative Diseases Fund.

Will sustainability become a core principle or an afterthought?

David Zamora is the chairman of the Space Data Association. He contributed this article to Space.com's Expert Voices: Op-Ed & Insights.

The commercialization of space is well underway, and as a result, Earth’s orbit is becoming ever more congested. The current number of active satellites in orbit stands at around 16,000, according to the European Space Agency( ESA), and this number is expected to jump close to 60,000 by 2035, Novaspace projects. While it’s not always given the recognition it deserves, space has fast become a critical infrastructure layer, underpinning nearly all systems that enable modern life as we know it. Therefore, it’s critical that a safe and clean operating landscape is maintained.

Yet this is becoming increasingly challenging, particularly given that, alongside the rapid increase in space traffic, there are also more than 1.2 million pieces of debris over 1 centimeter (0.4 inches) cm in Earth’s orbit, each of which could damage satellites and spacecraft and potentially cause disastrous consequences. With the number of active satellites and debris objects in space continuing to increase, the space environment is under growing pressure.

Space was once the sole domain of nation states, but this is no longer the case. As technology has advanced, launch costs have decreased, and satellites have become more efficient, smaller and cheaper to build. These changes have democratized space, resulting in a massive increase in space traffic, not least constellations in low Earth orbit (LEO) that comprise thousands of satellites.

Plans are underway to further expand these constellations, and new and even bigger constellations are also expected to launch in the coming years. Added to that, we’re also likely to see the first space-based data centers within the next few years, which may well mark the start of the wholesale shift of data centers from Earth to space, which will further add to the congestion already being felt.

To ensure that space continues to meet our current and future needs, this continued expansion must happen in a sustainable way. Maintaining a safe and clean landscape is more important than ever before, and mitigating the impact of space debris is critical to that effort.

Mitigating the impact of debris involves a multipronged approach. Whether intentional or unintentional, the creation of new space debris by future space missions must be minimized as much as possible. This applies during launch, operation, and end of life phases, and satellites and other spacecraft need to be designed with this in mind. Additionally, satellites that are no longer active should not remain in orbit for extended periods, because this only increases the chance of collision events which would create new debris. The standard rule has been 25 years, but there are recommendations now that this should be lowered to five years.

Also critical is the prevention of in-orbit collisions between active satellites and other objects. This is only achievable through effective Space Situational Awareness (SSA) and Space Traffic Coordination (STC) systems. SSA maps out and characterizes objects in space (both satellites and debris), determining their location, trajectory and velocity, while STC actively manages the space traffic identified through SSA to prevent collisions. When a conjunction is probable, SSA/STC service providers alert the operators involved, who then decide whether to undertake a collision-avoidance maneuver.

Extending the operational life of satellites is another important aspect of debris mitigation because it reduces the need for so many new launches, thereby reducing the likelihood of more debris being created. Innovations such as electric propulsion systems that allow for greater maneuverability and extended mission durations are part of the solution, as is in-orbit servicing, maintenance and refuelling.

While these strategies focus on preventing new debris from being created, we also need effective methods for removing existing pieces of debris. This is a rapidly growing sector attracting much investment. Debris-removal technology being developed ranges from physical capture and tethered deorbit to destruction with ground and space-based laser ablation and plasma systems, as well as the emerging concept of mining debris to extract metals and gases for in-orbit processing to feed into the circular space economy.

The UN views space safety and space sustainability as urgent priorities. Its treaties and agreements focus on preserving the peaceful, safe, and sustainable use of outer space, preventing collisions, managing space debris, and ensuring responsible operations. Governments, space agencies and international organizations are also strengthening their focus on space sustainability.

ESA has launched its Zero Debris strategy, which is aimed at achieving a debris-neutral orbital environment by 2030. As part of this approach, ESA has updated its debris mitigation requirements and standards that will govern how its missions are designed, built, flown and disposed of. It also sets requirements for any company or institution that works with ESA on its missions.

In the U.K., the UK Space Agency is also taking a proactive role in space safety and sustainability through its Active Debris Removal (ADR) program and through the development of space sustainability standards in partnership with industry. It is also in the process of developing a space sustainability strategy to ensure a coordinated approach to sustainable practices in space.

On the other side of the pond, NASA’s Space Sustainability Strategy identifies five key challenges that are felt nationally but also within the wider space community. These include the absence of a single framework for space sustainability that has been accepted by the space community and the fact that space sustainability may be in tension with other mission interests. The strategy defines six goals that are intended to address each of the challenges alongside corresponding objectives to guide NASA’s achievements of the goals.

The U.S. is also building on the National Orbital Debris Mitigation Plan, which was released in 2022, with the Orbital Sustainability (ORBITS) Act. The Act, which has been introduced to the Senate but not yet been enacted into law, seeks to establish a program for the active remediation of orbital debris as well as requiring the development of uniform orbital debris standard practices. The Office of Space Commerce has also developed TraCSS (Traffic Coordination System for Space) to provide basic SSA data and services to civil and private space operators in support of spaceflight safety, although the system is currently in beta mode.

The International Standard Organisation (ISO) is also working on a standard for STC (Space Traffic Coordination) so that SSA activities can be standardized. Titled Space Systems — Space Traffic Coordination (STC), the standard is moving toward final publication, and its core focus is on establishing baseline protocols, data exchange formats and operational requirements for space safety, on-orbit conjunction assessment and collision avoidance.

While these strategies are certainly moving the industry in the right direction, there is a real need to act quickly. Success will rely not only on speed, but also on sustained and collaborative effort, as well as engagement from all space users. The decisions made and actions undertaken over the next decade will shape the space environment for generations to come.

As to whether sustainability becomes a core principle rather than an afterthought, that remains to be seen. There is, however, reason to be optimistic.

#4245. Nearly 20% of Switzerland’s glacier ice has vanished in just five years

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Switzerland's glaciers lost more than 5% of their total ice volume in 2026, marking another devastating year for the country's rapidly shrinking glaciers. Although the melting did not quite match the record losses of 2022, glaciers thinned by an average of 2.5 to 4 meters. In some areas, the lower sections of glaciers lost as much as 10 meters of ice.

Several of Switzerland's major glaciers, including the Rhône, Aletsch, and Clariden glaciers, experienced their most severe melting on record in 2026. The losses were even more dramatic for some smaller glaciers, which disappeared entirely. Among them were Bella Tola in the canton of Valais and Griessfirn in the canton of Glarus, both of which have now vanished permanently.

The scale of the decline is particularly striking over the past five years. During that relatively short period, Switzerland has lost nearly 20% of its total glacier ice volume, illustrating just how quickly the country's mountain landscapes are being transformed.

These findings come from the annual report of the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT), which draws on long-term glacier observations collected by the Glacier Monitoring Network, known as GLAMOS. The monitoring program is based at ETH Zurich and the universities of Fribourg and Zurich in Switzerland.

Extreme Summer Heat and Low Snowfall Accelerate Ice Loss

A combination of unusually little snowfall and prolonged summer heat contributed to the extensive melting. The winter of 2025 to 2026 ranked among Switzerland's ten least snowy winters since records began, leaving glaciers with less snow to protect them from warmer temperatures.

Conditions became even more unfavorable between May and September 2026, when persistent heat and dry weather accelerated ice loss, even at the highest elevations.

During the summer, the elevation at which temperatures reached freezing climbed above 4,000 meters on 76 days. That was more than twice the usual number of days and established a new Swiss record. By September, snow had disappeared even at elevations of 3,500 meters, exposing glacier ice that would normally remain protected beneath a layer of snow.

Melting Glaciers Provide Water, but the Supply Is Shrinking

The enormous amount of melting also temporarily helped ease Switzerland's summer water shortages. Between July and September, the country's glaciers released approximately 2.2 trillion liters of water, an amount exceeding four times the drinking water consumed by Swiss households in an entire year.

However, this additional water comes from a resource that is steadily disappearing. As Switzerland's glaciers continue to shrink irreversibly, the amount of meltwater they can provide will also decline, reducing a source of water that currently helps offset shortages during the warmer months.
