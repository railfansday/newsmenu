#4344. Mass Effect 5 was aiming to offer a "level of autonomy and choice" that could vault it into orbit with Larian and CD Projekt's top RPGs, former dev says post-cancellation

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

"I'm sad you won't ever get to see what we were cooking"

Following EA's decision to shut down BioWare, the game that would've been the fifth entry in the Mass Effect series is being dumped out of an airlock, never to become a finished article that'll reach players hands as its creators envisioned. The larger tragedy is, of course, the job losses and the closure of a studio with a legacy befitting RPG royalty, but it's still sad that next Mass Effect will never come out, and some of the folks who worked on it have begun offering some insight into what the team were hoping to accomplish.

"I was one of the lucky ones who stayed with BioWare to start on the next Mass Effect, where I believe we were making something special: a level of autonomy and choice that no other game has been able to match," cinematic supervisor Tony de Waal wrote in a LinkedIn post revealing one of the 50 or so remaining BioWare staff let go as part of the closure. "We saw what Larian and CD Projekt were bringing to the table with their latest offerings, and started down a path to bring Mass Effect to the next level while remaining true to the Trilogy.

"I'm sad you won't ever get to see what we were cooking, and sad for the small, talented group of devs who were all punching above their weight to make something special. Please consider those who stayed with BioWare until the very end. They are truly some of the most talented people I've had the pleasure of working with throughout my career."

Writers Catherynne M. Valente and Derek Künsken didn't go into as much depth in their own posts on Twitter, but similarly echoed that they were excited about what the studio were sketching out and building with this Mass Effect game.

While it's worth maintaining the perspective that Mass Effect 5 was still early in development rather than minutes from being out of the door, it's galling that BioWare will never even get a chance to see if their plans could make for an RPG capable of pushing the genre forwards as the likes of Baldur's Gate 3, Cyberpunk 2077, and The Witcher 3 have over the past decade. Similarly to some of their long-established RPG-making cousins, the likes of Bethesda, BioWare's path hasn't been a straight line to massive success in recent years.

2017's Mass Effect: Andromeda wasn't as critically acclaimed and near-universally beloved as the trilogy that preceded it. Anthem failed to stick the live-service landing despite some promising ideas. Then, there was Dragon Age: The Veilguard, which had a well-documentedly messy development cycle that saw it pulled and pushed between multiplayer and singleplayer to suit the whims of EA management. The game it eventually turned into is one Nic (RPS in peace) summed up in his review as "a reluctant RPG, but a compelling, heartfelt action adventure". Sadly, it didn't sell the oodles of copies a game must to be deemed a success under EA these days, and thus seemed to instantly become the final black mark against BioWare that saw their corporate overlords start to fatally close in the walls.

Could BioWare have gotten their RPG master mojo back in full if they'd achieved their aims with Mass Effect 5? We'll never know. games studios under big publishers these days are rarely afforded such chances these days, no matter how obvious it might seem to those of us not just concerned with achieving short-term financial gains to offset being loaded up with debt in a Saudi-lead takeover by ditching everything that isn't turning over EA FC-level amounts of cash.

#4804. Some people really are werewolves—at least, in their minds

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 9:02 AM EDT

Dr. Nick Medford’s patient had plenty of reasons to be terrified of a simple blood test.

His body had shrunk down to a fraction of its size, or so he believed, and the physicians looming over him were enormous. Then there was the needle about to pierce his vein: to the patient’s eye, it was larger than his entire arm.

It might sound like something out of Alice in Wonderland. But this patient’s story belonged to a different genre entirely. Horror, namely. As far as the patient was concerned, there was no point in taking his blood. It was no longer human. Neither was he.

Medford’s patient was suffering from clinical lycanthropy, defined as the delusional belief that one is turning into a werewolf. And unlike the flesh-tearing beasts of folklore, who terrorize the unsuspecting by the light of the full moon, patients like Medford’s are often the ones haunted, trapped inside a body-horror nightmare.

According to Medford, his patient knew something was wrong, but was as confused about his condition as anyone. “Not only did he think he was a werewolf, but he would also talk about things like wanting to realign the cosmos and wanting to kill God,” says Medford, a neuropsychiatrist with the South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust.

“And then you would say, ‘Do you believe in God? Are you religious?’ And he’d say, ‘No.’ His ideas didn’t really make sense even to him, which was a part of his distress.”

So, what causes this rare condition? And why does it take the form of one of Halloween’s classic monsters?

If you’ve never heard of clinical lycanthropy before, there’s a good reason: Hollywood’s werewolves easily outnumber the real-life cases of this condition. Only about 25 instances have been reported in the medical literature.

Because there are so few cases, Medford says it is difficult to identify a common cause or predictable disease course. However, he says that typically it is seen in patients already experiencing mental illness.

“In around 40 percent of published cases, it occurs in the context of schizophrenia,” Medford says. “There are also reports of it occurring in the context of bipolar disorder or psychotic depression. So, in the context of severe mental illness, basically.”

Medford’s own patient had been diagnosed with Huntington’s disease, an inherited, fatal brain disease that causes psychiatric symptoms and uncontrolled body movements. The case, which Medford documented in a 2023 report, was the only published instance of clinical lycanthropy associated with Huntington’s. Although Medford says it is uncertain what then led his patient to believe he was a werewolf, he says the physical changes associated with Huntington’s may have fed this idea.

“There may be some change in the person’s body or in their perception or their experience of their body,” Medford says. “That might be, if you like, the seed from which this idea can develop.”

This seems possible in the case of Medford’s patient, who exhibited involuntary writhing movements and other strange physical sensations.

“He did not seem to be aware that he was having these abnormal movements, but he was aware of some weird sensations,” he says. “He described freezing sensations and electric-shock-like sensations. He had some sense that something strange was happening in his body.”

But physical changes alone don’t explain why the patient imagined he was turning into a werewolf. Which raises the question: Why do these delusions so often focus on lupine metamorphoses?

They don’t always, Medford notes. There’s a broader term—therianthropy—for the delusion that one has turned into any animal. But most of these cases involve canines, he acknowledges.

“Most of them are wolves or dogs or something of that type,” he says. “And then there’s a whole range of other strange things. There’s a report of someone who thought they’d turned into a bee.” That would make for an underwhelming horror movie, to be sure.

So, is it a coincidence that many of these patients report delusions ripped straight from a creature feature?

Probably not. In their case report, Medford and his co-author, neuroscientist Natasha Sigala, note that “delusions are influenced by culture,” and few cultural images of human/animal shapeshifting are as deeply embedded as the werewolf.

“The werewolf folklore and werewolf mythology goes right back to antiquity,” Medford says. “And it’s kind of interesting that the werewolf or something like it seems to pop up in a lot of different cultures and different times and places.”

Why is the werewolf legend so potent and enduring? Medford suspects it may tap into something primal.

“The werewolf symbolizes something about the human condition,” he says. “We live in civilized societies, and we adhere to social norms, but there’s a sort of animal part of us that perhaps chafes against that.”

That internal conflict may explain some of the patient’s other fixations. He described urges to strangle his wife and to defecate in a church. Medford notes that Huntington’s may also have impaired his sense of disgust, which can happen as the disease progresses.

Yet the patient also fought against his own impulses. At times, he lunged at staff, only to pull back and apologize.

“You could see the conflict in that moment,” Medford says. In that sense, the patient wasn’t so far from the werewolves of fiction. Like a movie werewolf beholding his mirror image in horror, he looked at his reflection and saw himself changing in ways he couldn’t control.

For Medford’s patient, that dreaded blood test ultimately was part of a turning point in his treatment. The bloodwork allowed Medford to keep the patient on clozapine, a potent antipsychotic. As Medford increased the dose, the patient’s delusional beliefs dwindled, and although the underlying Huntington’s disease remained, the werewolf soon was gone.

Medford hopes patients like his will be treated with compassion rather than sensationalism. “These cases, they’re rare, but when they do happen, they tend to arise in the context of serious psychiatric or neurological disturbance,” Medford says. “They are people meriting sympathy and treatment, rather than romancing about it.”

#1712. Honours even at the Allianz Arena, but worries over Saka

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arsenal and Bayern Munich shared the spoils of a ding-dong Champions League encounter that left Mikel Arteta guffawing at the quality of the two sides.

Part chess match, part basketball, it was an absorbing contest at the Allianz Arena. There’s plenty to sink our teeth into, so let’s crack on.

Aside from the absent Christos Tzolis, William Saliba and Piero Hincapie, Arteta had close to a full squad to choose from. Fresh from their lively cameos at Nottingham Forest, Jurrien Timber and Noni Madueke came into the starting lineup, while Bruno Guimaraes retained his midfield place ahead of Myles Lewis-Skelly.

Bayern were somewhat depleted. We knew Jamal Musiala was injured and Michael Olise suspended, but Luis Diaz only being fit enough for the bench felt like an added bonus. Joshua Kimmich was also absent following the death of his father. Our sympathies to him.

As someone who doesn’t watch a lot of German football, I was struck by how few players I recognised in their lineup. That probably gave me, and perhaps a few others, a false sense of security. Our record in Bavaria should have put paid to that. A solitary 2-0 win in 2013 is the only thing to crow about, and even then we went out on away goals.

Arsenal started briskly, Raya twice sending long diagonal balls towards the right flank in the opening seven minutes. It was a tactic he would use throughout the evening, the aim being to gain territory, compete in the air and pounce on the loose ball.

An early foray into the Bayern box came to nothing, and Odegaard missed a chance to thread a pass behind the defence before Saka offered a glimpse of what he might bring to the occasion. He muscled Alphonso Davies off the ball and charged down the right, looking sharp and up for the challenge.

That made it all the more galling to see him go down clutching his thigh in obvious pain. He immediately signalled that he needed to come off, leaving Arteta to reshuffle his attack. More on the injury later. For now, Eze, whose afternoon at the City Ground had ended after an underwhelming 45 minutes, came on down the left, with Madueke switching to the right.

We continued to enjoy more of the ball, while Havertz’s early booking for a late challenge on Josip Stanisic underlined how keenly contested everything was. So did the sight of Ezri Konsa following Harry Kane almost to the Bayern penalty area. Arteta had predicted a man-to-man encounter, and neither side were going to let him down.

Gabriel made an important challenge to stop Davies getting a shot away before Arsenal fashioned their first real opening. Odegaard curled in a corner, Neuer flapped and Calafiori fired well over from the back post.

Bayern had left three players upfield and wasted no time exploiting the space. Kane grabbed a ball and launched a quarterback-style pass towards Davies, with Gnabry eventually testing Raya with a low shot. The whole thing happened in seconds, giving Arteta ample reason to remind his players that our corners are ideally supposed to threaten the opposition’s goal, not ours.

The team responded by taking the lead with a move that was beautiful in its simplicity. After Arsenal recycled possession in midfield, Odegaard shaped to send Havertz through, paused, then looked up again to spot Eze sprinting behind Konrad Laimer down the left.

The pass was perfectly weighted. Eze let it bounce a couple of times and, by the time he struck across Neuer with his left foot, he was almost level with the edge of the six-yard box. A lovely finish, followed by the familiar experience of starting a celebration only to be told to put it back in its box.

The flag had gone up, but replays showed Stanisic on the near side just playing him on. VAR confirmed the goal, a welcome moment for a player who had taken plenty of criticism at the weekend. It was also our fifth goal from a substitute this season. (0-1)

On the way the goal played out, Odegaard told TNT Sports:

“It was a good timing in the end, and a great finish. I’m really happy for him to get his goal.

“The way he performed as well was a top performance from him. He’s a top player, and I think everyone knows his qualities, and today you could really see that out there.”

Bruno had a half-chance to double the lead from a corner, and I took encouragement from Ismael Saibari resorting to a wayward effort from distance. It felt as though we had a decent handle on Bayern, which was obviously the cue for that momentary sense of calm to be nipped in the bud.

Konsa had marshalled Kane well, but a throw-in on Bayern’s right left him unsure whether to follow his compatriot into the centre circle. You could see him pointing towards the striker, aware of the danger, but nobody else picked him up.

Kane received the ball in acres of space and, crucially, had time to measure his pass. It was a beauty, finding Davies as he darted inside Timber and through on Raya. The Canadian finished first time. That was all it took. Europe’s best teams don’t need you to switch off for very long. (1-1)

Arsenal resumed their probing, Bayern remained dangerous on the break, and the half ended with a flurry of corners that neither side could turn into another goal.

There was no let-up after the interval. Eze won a corner, Rice sent it towards the back post, and Bruno’s downward header was somehow kept out by Neuer, who pushed the bouncing ball up onto the bar before it was cleared. Replays showed it was right on the line when the 40-year-old intervened. Some stop by the veteran.

At the other end, Raya produced a flying save to repel Upamecano’s header after the Frenchman had barraged through the middle of the pitch like it was a Champs-Élysées shopfront. Eze then wriggled into the box and forced Neuer into another stop before Davies had a shot blocked.

It was breathless stuff, although probably a little more stretched than Arteta would have liked. Bayern were beginning to build momentum when they were awarded a penalty by a referee who, to his credit, was impressive throughout.

There wasn’t much to argue about. A cutback found Kane 12 yards out and Timber, throwing himself across the shot, inadvertently blocked it with his hand.

Who had our old nemesis scoring a penalty on the bingo card? Yes, all of you. Kane took it well, to be fair, shuffling through his run-up to send Raya the wrong way before planting the ball in the top left-hand corner. Just his ninth successful spot-kick against us. FFS. (2-1)

I feared the worst at that point. For the next five minutes, Bayern swarmed all over us, forcing mistakes and penning us back around our own box. Laimer went close, although we did briefly threaten an equaliser when an Odegaard free-kick caused a scramble that ended with Gabriel appealing for an imaginary handball. Worth asking, I suppose.

Both managers made double changes with 20 minutes remaining, and those substitutions helped swing the contest back our way. Bayern brought on Luis Diaz and Lennart Karl for Saibari and Gnabry, but a shot from the Colombian, comfortably saved by Raya, was about as threatening as the hosts got from there.

Arteta replaced Havertz and Bruno with Viktor Gyokeres and Myles Lewis-Skelly, who both made their presence felt. We settled, began keeping the ball again and patiently searched for a way through. Eventually, another moment of attacking quality brought the equaliser.

Timber, Eze, MLS and Konsa were all involved as Arsenal worked possession from left to right. Rice laid the ball off to Madueke and surged into the box, where he collected a deft touch from Gyokeres before hammering low across Neuer.

It was a fine move with a finish to match. Rice’s first goal since March, too. When he strikes a ball like that, you do wonder whether we could arrange for him to be in the penalty area a little more often, even if I appreciate he’s quite busy everywhere else. (2-2)

“When you play against Bayern, a team that go man-to-man over the whole pitch and follow you everywhere, it’s such a tough game for 95 minutes, and you have to apply certain principles and concepts, and you’re constantly thinking about where you can move into the space.”

Arteta sent on Mikel Merino in search of a winner in the closing five minutes, but the tempo finally dropped. Diaz picking up an injury and having to go off just 20 minutes after his introduction didn’t help the flow.

There was still time for Timber to unleash a rasping effort that Neuer tipped over. The resulting corner came to nothing, but after a contest like that, a draw felt fair.

Arteta’s immediate post-game comments were telling:

“Wow, what a game, that’s the level. That’s one of the highest levels that we played. The level of the opposition, what the game required, and our level as well. Two teams that want to press each other constantly, two teams that want to attack, that want to play. I think it was a fascinating game.”

In his post-game press conference, he added:

“We’ll take the draw, but it doesn’t mean we’re really happy about it because we prepared a team to win the game – you could see that from right at the beginning, with the mindset, the purpose the adventure team had to be very dominant and cause Bayern problems.”

Vincent Kompany, who had recovered sufficiently from a virus to take his place in the Bayern dugout, said:

“We faced a strong opponent; we felt it, we saw it. I think we had our moments. A few big chances that I didn’t think we’d get. A bit of a shame, because in the last ten to 15 minutes we suffered. It took a bit of our momentum. We still had the feeling we could win the game. But this is a strong opponent, and the game was never over. In that moment, the equaliser was a shame.”

Hopefully, the performance, if not the result, briefly quietens some of the tiresome discussion about Arsenal’s style of play. As Ben White once said, “Obviously, we’re quite good at football.”

It does help when the other team wants to play some too. In the Premier League, we spend plenty of time trying to prise apart a low block, with the opposition apparently hoping to win through the sheer structural integrity of their back five. Last night, both sides were prepared to press, attack and take risks. The spectacle benefited accordingly.

The obvious downside was Saka’s injury. He looked thoroughly unhappy as he limped off, and Arteta acknowledged the medical team’s concern.

“They are a bit concerned,” confirmed the Spaniard. “A player that has to come off with a really sharp pain is never good news.”

Saka was still limping on his way to the team bus, which does little to ease fears of a spell on the sidelines. We’ll have to wait for a proper assessment before knowing how long.

My brother cheerfully messaged to suggest a grade one quad tear could mean three months out. I’ll take that with a pinch of salt, seeing as his medical qualifications amount to an English literature degree from the University of Nottingham. Clearly, I missed the chapter of Wuthering Heights dealing with muscular rehabilitation.

A sad note to end on, but a memorable game. With seven points from nine, Arsenal remain in the automatic qualification spots after three games. Wins over Slavia Prague and Borussia Dortmund in November should help us consolidate that.

Right, let’s leave it there. Blogs should be back tomorrow, hopefully with an Arsecast and a pre-Everton Patreon podcast with Lewis. For now, have a great Thursday.

Mbappé was the top-rated player on the night with 8.42. The second half, though, was shaped by Vinícius Júnior’s exit and the man who replaced him.

Bernardo Silva opened the scoring just before half-time, in the 43rd minute. It was his first goal since joining the club. Madrid led 1-0 at the break. Güler doubled the lead from Mbappé’s pass after the restart. It was a second successive match with a goal for Güler.

Diomande came on for Vinícius in the 70th minute. Some Bernabéu fans whistled the Brazilian as he left the pitch, OKDIARIO reported. The substitute then laid on Mbappé’s goal. Tidiam Gomis pulled one back for Leipzig in the 85th minute, assisted by Christoph Baumgartner. Diomande restored the three-goal margin two minutes later from a Brahim Díaz pass.

He left Leipzig in the summer in a deal that could be worth up to €140 million. His rating of 7.55 came from 20 minutes on the pitch.

Mourinho said the change showed the strength of his bench rather than unhappiness with Vinícius. He added that Diomande or Endrick could change the pace of the game.

The numbers backed Madrid’s control. They had 65% of the ball and 18 shots to Leipzig’s 11. Their expected goals reached 3.13 against 1.51. Thibaut Courtois still made five saves for a rating of 7.89. Leipzig have now lost two in a row, after a 1-6 defeat to Bayern München.

Vinícius cannot play at the Spotify Camp Nou on Sunday 25 October. He is serving his suspension after being sent off against Villarreal. LaLiga has scheduled the matchday-ten Clásico for 21:00.

Madrid head to Barcelona on a run of four straight wins in all competitions. That sequence includes victories over Villarreal, Roma and Sevilla. Their last defeat was 1-2 against Atlético Madrid.

#4353. EA is shutting down RPG veteran Bioware "to align talent and investments where we see the greatest opportunities for players and long-term growth"

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Venerable RPG studio Bioware announced on it is shutting down after more than 30 years of making RPGs. The studio is responsible for making the original Baldur's Gate, Neverwinter Nights, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Dragon Age, Mass Effect, and Anthem.

As part of the announcement Bioware also say that "development on the next Mass Effect project is ending."

The post gives no reasoning for the shut down, nor does it mention its parent company EA who made the decision. In an open letter from EA's Chief Operating Officer David Tinson and Chief Studios Officer Cam Weber explained the closure, saying "we’re making deliberate choices to align talent and investments where we see the greatest opportunities for players and long-term growth."

They also cancelled a project in The Sims series code-named project Lotus, prioritising instead another called Project Evergreen.

The letter also revealed that there will be continued investment in EA's sports games, Apex Legends, Battlefield, and the next Star Wars Jedi game. Apparently, Dice and Respawn are collaborating on a new game which is likely a new Star Wars: Battlefront game.

Bloomberg reports that across EA there will be 400 layoffs, 50 of them taking place at Bioware.

Just last week, I ran a piece from Mark Darrah, a Bioware alum who had been with the studio since its Baldur's Gate days, only leaving after shipping Anthem in 2021. He explained how dangerous it was for a studio to make the scale of games Bioware was making with Mass Effect Andromeda, Anthem, and Dragon Age: Veilguard. The cost of the projects, the length of time they took to produce, and the risk they run of not selling enough companies puts the studio in "a really dangerous place". He said of his old studio that "BioWare sat right at the point where it doesn't quite work." For instance, shortly after Dragon Age: Veilguard's launch, EA CEO Andrew Wilson said the RPG had significantly underperformed, reaching only 1.5m players, less than the 3m the company had planned for.

It's also hard not to view the news in light of EA's buyout in August this year. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, equity firm Silver Lake, and Jared Kushner's Affinity partners completed their $55 billion leveraged buyout of EA, a move that both took the publisher private and added a vast amount of debt to the company's books. A year before the deal went through, unionised workers warned this buyout would mean layoffs. And, while there was an initial round in June, ahead of the deal going through, it appears this larger round is EA's first attempt to address the pressing debt they're now saddled with.

While there's no mention in EA's letter of further layoffs to come, they don't rule it out either.
