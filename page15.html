#5623. Judge who apologized to accused would-be Trump assassin lights up DOJ in Hunter Biden FBI case

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fox News correspondent David Spunt reports on two former FBI officials being charged by the DOJ with lying in the investigation into Hunter Biden's taxes.

U.S. Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui, who previously apologized to a man accused of attempting to assassinate President Donald Trump, rebuked the Justice Department on Tuesday over its attempt to bring new obstruction charges against two former FBI officials without first securing judicial approval.

Former FBI officials Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides were arrested last week after the Department of Justice accused the duo of misrepresenting their involvement in investigations concerning former President Joe Biden’s son, Hunter Biden, in statements to Congress.

Hours before prosecutors were scheduled to defend their original allegations at a preliminary hearing, where the government must establish probable cause, the DOJ filed a superseding criminal complaint seeking to add new charges against the former officials. The new complaint sought to add felony obstruction allegations against the former officials while softening some of the language prosecutors had used to describe their involvement in the Hunter Biden investigations.

Faruqui sharply criticized the DOJ for filing an unsigned and unsworn criminal complaint in the middle of the night without first obtaining judicial approval.

WHO IS JUDGE ZIA FARUQUI? WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT THE FEDERAL MAGISTRATE WHO APOLOGIZED TO COLE ALLEN

Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui speaks before a debate between incarcerated students and James Madison University students on the topic of abolishing life without parole at Prettyman Federal Courthouse in Washington, D.C., on Nov. 22, 2024. (Craig Hudson for The Washington Post via Getty Images)

"I think they should have filed nothing," Faruqui said, according to reports from inside the courtroom. "They never had complaints filed on their record that are unsigned, waiting for a judge to make a probable cause finding. I think that it is inappropriate and harms the defendant. I'm frustrated that I got it in the middle of the night, but I'm much more concerned that it's filed on the public record."

"Why today on this one case have you changed the entire history of how you charge cases?" Faruqui asked the DOJ. "What is it? It appears that – it makes it look like the United States is afraid of me trying to find probable cause."

John Borchert, a prosecutor from the U.S. Attorney's Office in Washington, D.C., said it was the first time he had filed new charges against someone without going through judicial review.

Faruqui rejected the superseding complaint because prosecutors had not secured the required judicial authorization, allowing the preliminary hearing to proceed on the original allegations that Floris and Benavides lied to Congress. Floris’ lawyers argued that the changes in language between the two documents calls into question the factual basis of the DOJ’s case.

Hunter Biden and former FBI official Nikki Floris. (AP Photo/Susan Walsh; AP Photo/Eric Thayer)

In the superseding complaint, prosecutors described Benavides and Floris as having been "involved in" the Foreign Influence Task Force's work concerning Hunter Biden, a softer characterization than the original complaint's suggestion that they had been at the "center" of those activities. Floris, who was previously said to have been involved in "scores of conversations" related to Biden’s son, is now only accused of participating in "conversations."

In what may have been a reference to the recently unsealed charges against former FBI Director James Comey, Faruqui said that moving on a superseding complaint without a probable cause finding "would be like charging someone by information... but in a complaint context."

"If you want a superseding complaint, like every other case that’s happened in this district, we will have a judge make a probable cause finding," the judge said. "Not send me something in the middle of the night and expect that it gets done."

Faruqui has previously faced criticism from conservatives over his support for diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives, criticism of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and positions on pretrial detention. He also drew backlash after apologizing to Cole Allen, the man accused of attempting to assassinate President Donald Trump at the 2026 White House Correspondents' Association Dinner, and criticizing the government's use of five-point restraints against him.

"To me, it’s extremely disturbing that he was put in five-point restraints, a person with no criminal history," the judge said about Allen at a hearing earlier this year. "It’s troubling. I never heard of one Jan. 6 defendant who was put in five-point restraints or in a safe cell. If the only way to keep him safe is the most punitive thing, that’s a problem."

The DOJ has also been questioned for charging via complaint rather than through a grand jury, a procedural shortcut usually reserved for imminent threats to public safety.

Critics have questioned the timing of the charges, with midterm elections being just days away when news of the two former officials’ arrests broke.

Floris’ lawyers have similarly argued that the timing and content of the charges are meant to engineer publicity.

"What is going on at the D.C. U.S. Attorney's Office? In the middle of the night, the government filed an unsigned and unsworn complaint attempting to fix the issues with their original charge against Ms. Floris and adding a new charge just as meritless as the first one," said Floris' legal team in a statement provided to CBS News.

"In our combined decades of DOJ service, we have never seen or even heard of something like this being filed," they went on. "That's because the public lodging of criminal charges must be authorized by the grand jury, a judge, or the defendant herself. Here, the government has taken that power for itself in a desperate attempt to delay our client's right to challenge the charges in court and litigate this case in the media, rather than the courtroom. We have asked to address this outrageous government conduct against a decorated public servant at today's court hearing."

Legal teams for Floris and Benavides, as well as the DOJ, did not respond to requests for comment when reached by Fox News Digital on Tuesday.

#4782. Robinhood Misses Q3 Earnings as Crypto Revenue Rises and Prediction Market Bets Grow

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Robinhood Misses Q3 Earnings as Crypto Revenue Rises and Prediction Market Bets Grow

Robinhood, the trading app where more than 28 million people keep money to buy stocks and crypto and now bet on sports and elections, reported third-quarter revenue of $1.42 billion billion on Tuesday, missing the roughly $1.43 billion Wall Street expected

Earnings came in at $0.51 per share, missing the $0.55 analysts projected and down from $0.61 a year ago. Earnings per share is profit divided by the number of shares outstanding, and it is the figure investors use to call a quarter a beat or a miss.

Robinhood is the app that brought commission-free trading to a generation of first-time investors, which makes its quarterly report one of the clearest readings of what ordinary people are doing with their money. When customers trade more, deposit more or pile into new products, the shift shows up in these numbers.

Net income was $476 million, down 14% from $556 million a year earlier and 17% from $573 million in the second quarter. The quarter included a $67 million unrealized loss, meaning a drop in value on paper, on Robinhood Ventures Fund I, which cost $0.07 per share. Adjusted earnings per share, which exclude certain items, came to $0.60, down 3% from a year ago.

Event contracts, the yes-or-no bets that pay out $1 if a game, an election or an economic report goes a certain way, brought in $173 million, up from $156 million in the second quarter, when they overtook crypto revenue for the first time. Customers traded a record 16.4 billion of them, more than seven times a year earlier.

“I believe we’re at the very beginning of a prediction market supercycle,” CEO Vlad Tenev said in December last year, when Robinhood added NFL player contracts and parlay-style combos that pay only if every outcome hits.

Crypto trading revenue rose to $120 million from $100 million in the second quarter, but remained down 55% from the $268 million Robinhood earned in the third quarter of 2025. Customers traded $50 billion in crypto during the quarter, down 38% from a year earlier, with $21 billion on the Robinhood app and $29 billion through Bitstamp, the crypto exchange it acquired

Robinhood Chain went live on July 1 as a layer-2 network, meaning a blockchain that runs on top of Ethereum to make transactions cheaper and faster. Its first weeks were driven by meme coins, joke-themed tokens, rather than the tokenized stocks it was built for, which are digital versions of company shares that trade on a blockchain.

By the end of August the chain had earned about $3.13 million in fees over 30 days, more than Ethereum and Base, which came in at $2.47 million and $2.89 million.

Hours before the report, Robinhood announced a deal with Ripple that brings RLUSD, a stablecoin (a crypto token designed to stay worth one dollar), to Robinhood Chain and Bitstamp, with an in-app rollout planned later this year. RLUSD has a market value above $2.5 billion, and Ripple will also supply Robinhood’s treasury software and lending for Bitstamp traders.

Robinhood shares closed Tuesday at about $94.61 and slipped in after-hours trading- The stock is down roughly 18% this year and about 37% below its 52-week high of $150.47.

Robinhood narrowed its 2026 outlook for adjusted operating expenses and stock-based pay to $2.675 billion to $2.75 billion, trimming the top of the $2.675 billion to $2.775 billion range it set in July.

#4671. Treasury's Bessent sees crypto as America's next tool for keeping the U.S. dollar on top

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said digital assets could help strengthen the U.S. dollar's global dominance as technology becomes an increasingly important source of American economic influence.

Speaking Tuesday at Ripple's Swell conference with CEO Brad Garlinghouse, Bessent argued that the power behind the world's reserve currencies during history derived from military strength, but it’s increasingly shifting toward technological leadership.

"We will keep our military umbrella, but I think more and more it's becoming a technology umbrella," Bessent said.

He pointed to the growing divide between U.S. and Chinese technology ecosystems, where countries and companies face choices over whose artificial intelligence systems and semiconductor technology to adopt. Bessent said he sees digital assets as part of that competition, with the financial infrastructure countries may choose influencing the U.S. dollar’s reach.

"I think that digital assets are going to be no different, and we are going to win in the digital asset stack, and that's gonna continue to fortify the [U.S.] dollar," he said.

His remarks offered a broader rationale for the administration of President Donald Trump’s push to bring crypto businesses onshore, framing digital assets not just as a financial market but as part of the infrastructure that could sustain U.S. economic influence. The administration has already secured passage of the GENIUS Act, which established a federal regulatory framework for stablecoins, cryptocurrencies designed to maintain a fixed value against fiat currencies like the U.S. dollar.

Bessent argued that stablecoins could reinforce the dollar's position by expanding access to U.S. currency in countries where local currencies face inflation or sharp swings in value.

More than 90% of stablecoins are backed by U.S. dollars, he said, and their issuers often hold U.S. government securities as reserves. As adoption grows, demand for those assets could rise, creating another source of financing for the federal government.

"I think it's gonna be an important source of funding for the Treasury," Bessent said.

Bessent pushed back against the idea that stablecoins would merely replace dollars people already hold, arguing that they could reach new users in emerging markets who want to protect their savings from currency devaluation.

"I think we are opening up new markets all around the world," he said.

However, the White House’s broader push to regulate crypto markets suffered a setback after Congress failed to pass the Clarity Act, which would have established a federal framework for the industry.

Bessent called the failure a disappointment but said the Treasury Department could work with regulatory agencies like the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) to advance oversight through agency action.

"At Treasury, we have a lot of convening powers," he said, describing the SEC and CFTC as “great partners” in forming a regulatory framework that could serve as a model for other countries.

The agencies' regulatory efforts could provide more clarity for crypto firms, though rules adopted by regulators would lack the permanence of legislation.

Bessent also addressed the industry's concerns about debanking, a term used to describe financial institutions cutting off services to crypto businesses, sometimes allegedly under regulatory pressure.

He said the Clarity Act would have helped prevent a return to such practices but argued that regulatory safeguards could still offer protection.

"I think we can set up regulatory best practices, guardrails that will prevent that from happening again," Bessent said.

Jennifer Lawrence's candor was a huge part of her appeal as she rose to fame, and the Oscar-winning mother of two remains our favorite source of unfiltered commentary on any topic.

Along with the Oscar-caliber acting skills she displayed almost upon arrival in Hollywood 20 years ago, the now 36-year-old quickly became a reliable source of fabulously unfiltered commentary, her takes on pretty much everything—be it fame, appearance, bodily functions or Larry David—never failing to shock and delight.

If the trend does hold and Netanyahu is ousted, this will please many people in Washington who want to see substantial changes in Israeli policy. But here’s something they should keep in mind. On the issues that journalists, analysts, and some members of Congress care most about—the Gaza Strip, the West Bank, and a two-state solution—Eisenkot’s change bloc does not differ all that much from the current coalition. On most of these issues, Netanyahu has been firmly within Israel’s mainstream, just like his opponents.

Much of Washington is afflicted with Bibi Derangement Syndrome. They have a hard time discerning between what is actually the fault of the prime minister and facts about Israeli politics that observers happen to not like. This syndrome underlines the inability of both its passionate supporters and its equally passionate critics to treat Israel as a normal country whose leaders confront internal political constraints and incentives. I don’t mean to let Netanyahu off the hook. He has rubbed a fair number of people the wrong way and played partisan politics in Washington. Former U.S. Secretary of State James Baker banned him from the State Department when Netanyahu said U.S. policy in the region was based on “lies and distortions.” President Bill Clinton, after meeting Netanyahu for the first time in 1996, asked: “Who the fuck does he think he is? Who’s the fucking superpower here?”

President Barack Obama and Netanyahu clearly could not stand to be in the same room. Jared Kushner relays in his book about the first Trump administration that his father-in-law always expected Netanyahu to undercut him. And President Joe Biden, who thought he knew Israel better than everyone, clearly found dealing with the Israeli leader to be trying. And what Americans who dislike him say about Netanyahu is mild in comparison to his Israeli opponents. And yet, personal tics aside, the prime minister clearly reflects a broader band of the Israeli electorate than many in the policy world want to believe.

On Sept. 26, Eisenkot and other leaders of the change bloc—Naftali Bennett, Yair Lapid, Avigdor Lieberman, and Yair Golan—released a document detailing their priorities should they form a government. In it, they announced a government of repair or reconstruction that would prioritize the establishment of a state commission of inquiry into the failures surrounding the Hamas attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, promised to pass term limits for the prime minister, draft a constitution, and oppose Haredi exemption from military service.

There was nothing in the document about the Gaza Strip; nothing about the E-1 settlement, which the current government has begun the process of building decades after its approval and which it is feared would make it even harder for Palestinians to travel between the northern and southern West Bank. And, crucially, there was nothing about a two-state solution. One might assume these topics were left out so that the platform could appeal to the widest possible group of Israelis—and that there will be some flexibility once a new government is in place. But after spending almost two weeks in Israel, talking to political figures, analysts, and just everyday Israelis, I am not so sure that’s the case.

On a warm Sunday evening, about 30 Israelis and I gathered in the living room of a modest home in central Israel to listen to two members of Beyachad, the joint list of Bennett’s “Bennett 2026” party and Yesh Atid under Lapid’s leadership. Both candidates emphasized the need for political and social reform in an Israel they see as battered and abused by almost two decades of governments suborned to Netanyahu’s parochial and personal interests at the expense of the rest of society. They were just as critical on foreign policy, blasting Netanyahu for Israel’s low international standing and not making the country’s case more forcefully and effectively in important national capitals and international fora like the United Nations. It was at this moment when the conversation took a turn many in Washington would not expect: Both candidates assailed the government for bungling Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran—making the case that for all of the effort Israel had expended across these battle zones, all three remained a threat. This was not a call for diplomatic solutions to the problems of Iran and its proxies, but a more effective military strategy. This position was hardly controversial among people who are wary of American and European demands for agreements that do little more than allow Israel’s enemies to live and fight another day.

They also stressed their opposition to a Palestinian state. They offered that after Oct. 7, the Palestinians could expect nothing more from a new Israeli government than autonomy in Area A (of the West Bank)—an artifact of the Oslo era in which the Palestinian Authority manages the affairs of the population. When one member of the small audience asked them about the situation in the West Bank, using the term “West Bank,” Bennett’s representative made sure he emphasized “Judea and Samaria,” the preferred terminology among the settlers for the West Bank that has become normalized among many Israelis. And when asked about their alliance with Yair Golan, whose Democrats party is considered left of center, both candidates reiterated their opposition to Palestinian statehood. Most Israelis agree with this position, according to polls.

The concern about Golan among at least one person attending the Beyachad event demonstrates just how opposed Israelis are to a Palestinian state. Golan’s position is hardly dovish. He opposes settlement expansion because he fears it would lead inevitably to annexation and a binational state. Instead, Golan believes that over the long term, a two-state solution is best for Israeli security, but because of Oct. 7, Golan supports “civil separation” and an indefinite security presence in the West Bank until a responsible Palestinian leadership can take over. I dare someone to define “long term” and “responsible” in this context. And even if you could get to this semantic promised land, Golan, like almost all Israeli politicians, believes Israel must hold on to the Jordan Valley, further truncating a Palestinian state.

It’s not that nothing would change under a new Israeli government led by the change bloc. Israel would work quickly to establish law and order in the West Bank, where settler violence against Palestinians has surged lately. Of the multiple pressures facing Israel, it is the easiest issue to deal with, but it is not easy. There will be a lot of politics around a crackdown, especially since this issue of settler violence is more complicated than the press, analysts, and members of Congress portray. Ben-Gvir could be out of power, but he is not going away, especially since he commands a loyal following among Israel’s middle and lower classes. He is among a strain on the Israeli right who do not care about sanctions or Israel’s standing in the West.

It is possible that Israel will have a new prime minister soon. And while Israel’s tone would likely be less pugnacious under a Prime Minister Eisenkot or Bennett, all the issues that separate the United States and Israel will remain. It is hard to see how a new government papers over the differences. It would be better to respectfully disagree and let the U.S.-Israel relationship change. A little bit of space would be good for everyone.

While GM announced it plans to invest $1.25 billion in the factory, including over $2 billion in the Lansing area over the next several years, it didn’t explicitly confirm what models will receive the hybrid powertrain or disclose any details about the system.
