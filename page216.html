Right-hander Brandon Woodruff, the longest-tenured player on the Milwaukee Brewers, has made the club's World Series roster. Woodruff has not pitched in an MLB game since July 4 due to what was initially considered a severe shoulder injury, though he showed enough improvement in August to begin a throwing program. Game 1 of the Fall Classic between the Brewers and Tampa Bay Rays is set for Friday night in Milwaukee.

"We know who the kid is. We know his -- that it's not going to be too much for him. This is what this kid's all about. He's been around here for ten years or whatever, and we've understood it. We feel like there's a window where he can help us," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said on Friday.

"That was the whole decision. This isn't a, 'wow, we love Woody, he's great for the organization' -- because he already said to us, I don't want to be a sympathy pick. I want to be a pick because I can help the team. Then you go through all the things and say, can he help us? The answer is yes, he can definitely help us."

Now 33, Woodruff made nine effective starts prior to the shoulder injury this season. He missed the entire 2024 season after having surgery on his shoulder, returned to make 12 starts in 2025, and then was on the Opening Day roster this year. It was initially feared that Woodruff would require another surgery in July. That ultimately was not the case.

Murphy acknowledged the roster rules would play a role in carrying Woodruff on the World Series roster. Injured players can be replaced on the postseason roster if they suffer a new injury. By rule, the Brewers would not be able to replace Woodruff if he suffers a setback and re-injures his shoulder. They're gambling that his shoulder will hold up.

"I didn't want this to be a charity pick," Woodruff said on Friday. "That was important to me. I wanted to feel like I could -- I wanted to be on the roster because I felt like I could help win. So I did not want this to be part of the decision."

Woodruff said he met with Murphy and president of baseball operations Matt Arnold on Thursday before the final decision was made.

"It didn't really hit me," he said of the call from Murphy to tell him he'd made the roster. "I was kind of like thank you for the opportunity and, basically, let's go win this thing. That was pretty much the extent of the conversation. It was like they've got work to do, I've got a job to do to mentally prepare, and I just thanked them. That was pretty much it, yeah."

During his nine starts this season, Woodruff pitched to a 2.98 ERA with 47 strikeouts and only nine unintentional walks in 45 ⅓ innings. At times, his fastball clocked as high as 96.0 mph. There were also times when he sat in the upper 80s. The movement traits on his fastball remain terrific, allowing it to play at reduced velocity.

It is unknown how the Brewers intend to use Woodruff, though it seems likely he won't be much more than a one- or two-inning option given the injury and long layoff. Truth be told, he may only see the mound in a blowout. Regardless, the Brewers consider him healthy enough to contribute in some capacity. Woodruff, who has spent his entire MLB career with the Brewers after debuting in 2017, replaced right-hander Chad Patrick on the roster. Patrick was on the NLDS and NLCS rosters, but did not pitch.

Game 1 of the World Series will be played Friday night at American Family Field in Milwaukee. Both the Brewers and Rays are seeking their first-ever championship. National League Cy Young favorite Jacob Misiorowski will start for the Brewers, while the Rays counter with Nick Martinez -- a somewhat unexpected choice.

#4047. Nintendo's Shigeru Miyamoto says even if gen-AI "were to eventually handle everything, there will always be a role left" for human designers

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Legendary Nintendo developer Shigeru Miyamoto - who led development on classic games such as Super Mario 64 and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time - has argued that the rise of generative AI tools won't necessarily replace human talent.

Next month, Shigeru Miyamoto will become the first ever video game developer to be awarded the Order of Culture by the Emperor of Japan, a hugely prestigious honour. But in a recent press conference dedicated to the award, he also shared some thoughts about gen-AI and its use in game production.

"Our generation lived through a period of rapid computer development - a time when tools were constantly evolving, moving from typewriters to word processors,” he said while speaking to the press, in quotes translated by VGC. Miyamoto argued that a designer's true capabilities "finally came to the fore" as technology advanced and was capable of handling more of what might be considered "labor", and a similar transition may take place with generative AI tech.

"While the act of creation is inherently satisfying, there's a danger: one might find satisfaction in the labor itself and mistakenly believe that a high-quality piece of work has been created," he added. If generative-AI software replaces more tasks involved in game production, human game devs might have "an opportunity to reflect on what's really important."

"Looking back at past designs, there are some that were created merely by copying and pasting. Yet, such work rarely stands the test of time; it lacks the individual’s particular design intention," Miyamoto continued. "Because the process relies on an individual's intent, with the computer serving as an assistant for the labor, I believe that even as technology continues to evolve, and even if AI were to eventually handle everything, there will always be a role left for us to play."

Masahiro Sakurai was a bit less optimistic about a potentially AI-first future than his peer. The Smash Bros. director recently said gen-AI tech might lead to a surge of unoriginal and unprofitable games, though it won't be able to replicate the creativity of humans.

Nintendo itself hasn't outright ruled out the possibility of using gen-AI tools in the future, but former Nintendo of America president Doug Bowser assured folks that the company's games would retain a "human touch" last year. "We still believe that what makes our games special is our developers, their artistic capabilities, their insight into how people play," Bowser said at the time.

Nintendo is somewhat at odds with several of its contemporaries when it comes to AI adoption, however. Square Enix CEO Takashi Kiryu previously said the company would be "aggressive in applying AI." Sega, Capcom, Level-5, Epic Games and a number of other high-profile game studios have admitted to using gen-AI in some form, as well. 📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Please allow the first sentence here to warn you that this interview discusses a portion of Control Resonant that takes place near the end of the game and is arguably the most unexpected and surprising sequence. I encourage you to experience it without spoilers. So if you haven't beaten the game yet, please do, and then come back to this page.However, if you have beaten the game, or curiosity has driven you to learn what game has been redacted from the headline and why (it's the 1995 Super Nintendo RPG, EarthBound, by the way), then please read on.

In the late-game of Control Resonant – basically right before you tackle the final confrontation – the game suddenly shifts timeframe and genre to become a 16-bit RPG. The nostalgic lens turns the game into something that feels like 1995's EarthBound, complete with turn-based battles, in order to explain the trauma that Jesse and Dylan went through at a young age after they came across an object of power: the slide projector. The whole sequence is funny, heartbreaking, weird, and importantly effective at making you feel you are seeing a part of their past. It was particularly effective on me as I have fond memories of playing EarthBound and games like it when I was young. To see and experience Dylan and Jesse's childhood this way felt accurate, as absurd as that sounds.

I reached out to Remedy to specifically discuss that portion of the game. You can find the full e-mail interview below with the team members who contributed to its creation.

The big RPG backstory sequence for Jesse and Dylan – why approach the storytelling that way?

Lead level designer, Anne-Marie Grönroos: The basis for the whole sequence is that the Hedron attacks Dylan's mind, breaks his reality apart, and he has to rebuild himself. We wanted the reality-breaking part to affect the game itself and have the player feel some of the confusion Dylan is feeling by breaking the fourth wall.

Who pitched that sequence, how did they pitch it, and was it difficult to sell to the larger team?

Grönroos: At Remedy, ideas rarely come from a single person or team, and we rarely do formal pitches, but rather discuss and workshop ideas cross-craft until they start taking the shape of something we like. The initial idea from the writer was for this to be a text-based adventure, to return to a very early form of a video game to show that the Patterning is devolving him, but an Alan Wake 2 DLC had already done that, and we wanted to find something else.

Independently, some months ago a level designer had suggested a sidequest with JRPG pixel graphics, but that had gone nowhere. I remembered that and suggested doing an old Final Fantasy-style JRPG here, which also felt more fitting since that’s the type of game Dylan and Jesse would have played in their childhood, and not a text-based adventure. People got excited about that, and after some concerns about feasibility, we went forth with it. It helped that the level designer who had originally wanted to do the JRPG side quest is a big fan of these types of games and has made them before. I wouldn’t have dropped something this crazy on someone who wasn’t passionate about it and ready to figure out how to do it with our engine that’s not built for anything like this.

Is EarthBound (Mother in Japan) the primary inspiration for that sequence? Are there big fans of those games at Remedy?

Senior level designer in charge of the JRPG quest, Arhi Makkonen:The whole Quest was born from that childhood feeling I had when playing old JRPGs – Final Fantasy, Breath of Fire, Chrono Trigger, Secret of Mana, Bahamut Lagoon, and yes, Mother. The most important thing for me was capturing that feeling of a time when games felt like magic. I think many people can relate to that nostalgia, and now Jesse and Dylan share that feeling in the game.Visually, Mother was a major inspiration of course. Juuso Anttila is the concept artist who did all the splendid pixel art for Rebuild quest. Mother's style fits the mysterious Americana small town vibe perfectly. We also couldn't pass up the pun about a story revolving around "Not-Mother" that was made in the style of Mother.

Was it difficult to implement the sequence into the game? Did Remedy's Northlight Engine need significant work to account for the sequence?

Makkonen: The Northlight engine was designed to work perfectly for third-person action games. Well, Rebuild Quest is pretty much the opposite of that. Northlight is a specialized tool rather than a general-purpose one, so we had to come up with some rather creative solutions to create a 2D JRPG.

First of all, we’re faking the 2D look. In reality, the entire JRPG Ordinary exists within a 3D world that the camera simply views from directly overhead. It occupies a tiny patch in a remote corner of the game world—a space where 3D Dylan would barely have room to stand. The entire game-within-a-game is written in Lua; our incredibly versatile game designer, Tommi Saalasti, created the underlying system, upon which I was then able to build the story.

Since we didn't really have the tools to create a game like this, I had to hand-code every single detail. Dylan takes one step up, Dylan turns left (where Jesse hopefully is at that moment), text box 1, text box 2. The Lua script for the Rebuild quest alone probably contains as many lines of code as all the other quests in the game combined. And then there were things like animations were restricted to a maximum of two frames, and some UI elements were created using static particle effects.

So yes, we faced all sorts of challenges and compromises, but at the heart of it all was a wonderful story – simply told in a somewhat unconventional way. We trusted that the narrative would make up for some of the imperfections.

Remedy has a reputation for big unexpected and often crazy singular moments. What are the conversations like around delivering those? In pre-production, are there conversations like, "What is the big moment for this one?" Or do they happen pretty naturally?

Grönroos: They both happen naturally and we also have conversations about what the big moments should be. Sometimes we like a section so much that we decide to put even more resources and importance to it, even if it wasn’t supposed to be all that important from the get-go. The Into the Sinkhole quest, for instance, is a quest that wasn’t planned to be a “tentpole moment” from the start, but we kept putting more effort into it when it was shaping up to be something very special.

But most setpieces in the game were planned to be such from the early stages of pre-production. In Resonant, we wanted to have multiple standout moments instead of a single sequence like the Ashtray Maze overshadowing everything else, and I’m happy to see that players have different favorites.

And a different approach from Control is that several of these memorable moments (Sinkhole, Underpass Fault, the JRPG section) are more emotional and subdued instead of bombastic action (although we also have big action setpieces), which was an interesting challenge itself.

Was there a fear that the visuals would limit the emotional impact of the sequence?

Grönroos: Yes, but we decided to trust the writing. Several other games have managed to deliver a similar emotional impact despite pixel graphics (and sometimes because of them, since the level [of] abstraction can make characters more relatable and up to the player’s interpretation), so we weren’t too concerned.

Was there a fear that action game fans would be frustrated by the RPG sequence?

Grönroos: Yes, this was the main concern about the sequence. It was a very polarizing sequence in the playtests: people either loved or hated it. We did discuss shortening it significantly, but in the end decided to keep it as it is but added some fast travel options and make some parts skippable. There were more fans than haters, and we didn’t want to sacrifice something we really enjoyed.

How concerned were you about the actual RPG mechanics of that sequence?

Makkonen: Since Rebuild doesn't feature a vast number of battles, we didn't need to build particularly deep RPG or progression systems. The combat served as an additional storytelling tool – illustrating how bullies torment Dylan, showing his first fight (against rats, naturally), and depicting his eventual abduction by the FBC.

Refining the combat to be simple, yet offer the player meaningful choices required a few iterations. In addition to standard attacks, the children can occasionally use special abilities: Dylan delivers a heavy blow to a single enemy, while Jesse fires at everyone. Enemies deal enough damage that defending becomes necessary when multiple foes target the same character.

The lightbulbs in The Underpass mission goes in similarly unexpected, animated directions. How did that mission come about? What was the pitch like for that one?

Grönroos: That one started with the idea of doing something comically mundane, switching lightbulbs, and adding a paranatural twist to it. The cartoon man wasn’t in the initial version, but instead we were going to do live action videos and have Dylan get stuck in that video as the end twist. They were supposed to be old-fashioned “work safety” videos explaining how to change lightbulbs, and as the quest went on, it became more and more obvious that the maintenance guy in the videos was too aware of what Dylan was doing. This all came out from a one-hour workshop.

There wasn’t really a pitch, but rather a back-and-forth like “could we do a quest about Dylan changing lightbulbs in a ritualistic way? We could have these sentient work safety videos explain how to do it and “Dylan could get trapped inside the video.” Much later, when the live action turned out to be a no-go, we pivoted to the current cartoon instead, and that was even funnier than our initial idea.

Inside the Art Basel Paris bubble on Monday and Tuesday, things appeared to go ahead as if nothing bad would happen. Since it moved back to the Grand Palais in 2024, this fair has been the most desirable art-buying destination for collectors—combining the allure of A-plus hardcore masterpieces, à la the flagship fair in Switzerland, with the party-happy circuit of restaurants and hotel lobbies found adjacent to the group’s fair in Miami Beach. Even in the shadow of the demonstrations, Art Basel Paris was exactly that. There didn’t appear to be a single chink in the armor. The museums around town were packed to the point where you couldn’t move.

The morning after the demonstrations, tourists dutifully lined up around the Louvre. Even after bypassing the queue, I had to wait to enter the galleries, and of course to see the Mona Lisa. It was easier to get to the museum’s small show of work by Elizabeth Peyton, made while she was in residence at the Louvre, including portraits of Jennifer Lawrence and Chopin. I had never seen François Pinault’s Bourse de Commerce this crowded before, especially in a room with Irving Penn photographs installed chockablock up the walls. After arriving at the Fondation Louis Vuitton for a show of works once in the holdings of French collector Gustave Fayet, I dutifully stood sentry as visitors took their pictures of the van Gogh self-portrait before getting a blissful minute with The Large Plane Trees, on loan from the Cleveland Museum.

The galleries saw no downturn in visitors. According to sources, the show of Lee Krasner works at Gagosian, presented with the Pollock Krasner Foundation and Olney Gleason, was among the Paris gallery’s best-attended openings ever. Gallerygoers thronged through the Marais Monday afternoon, making their way to see the astounding collection of Cy Twombly works at Karsten Greve. None were for sale—but if overheard chatter is to be believed, that wasn’t stopping a big-pocketed sovereign wealth fund from attempting to make a purchase. David Zwirner also opened a large survey of new work by Oscar Murillo there, while not one but two satellite fairs opened in the neighborhood: Post-Fair, an offshoot of the Santa Monica expo that’s held during Frieze Los Angeles, and Paris Internationale, the longstanding young gallery fair in town.

The spree of meals and parties continued unabated—most prominently a dinner celebrating Alice Diop’s “Geste de Consolation,” an exhibition commissioned by Miu Miu as part of the Art Basel Official Program. The show incorporates a new video by Diop and a wildly inventive installation that will host a series of performances throughout the week. Dinner, as any good Paris dinner should be, was held at Brasserie Lipp, long the fashion world’s preferred old-world bistro, right across from Café de Flore.

Mrs. Prada, who personally oversees all of the art commissions for Miu Miu and Prada—and who of course runs one of the world’s great private art concerns, the Prada Foundation—held court at a table in the back of Brasserie Lipp, sitting with Serpentine director Hans Ulrich Obrist, Neue Nationalgalerie director Klaus Biesenbach, LUMA founder Maja Hoffmann, curator Nancy Spector, artists Francesco Vezzoli and Arthur Jafa and Meriem Bennani; and Art Basel contingent represented by Global CEO Noah Horowitz, director of fairs Vincenzo de Bellis, and Art Basel Paris director Karim Crippa.

On Tuesday, Art Basel let a few select viewers into the fair a day early—part of a special event in its second year called Avant Première. The premise: Each gallery disburses a few passes to its primo clients. The crowd did seem especially hand-selected. I saw Bernard Arnault, LVMH’s chairman and CEO, inside the Nahmad Projects booth, checking out works by Jean Dubuffet. He was one of the few collectors present who could have easily snapped up the fair’s most expensive work: a Modigliani at the Pace Di Donna Schrader booth priced at $100 million—though as of this writing the work had not yet been sold.

Arnault’s daughter, Delphine Arnault, was on hand as well. James Murdoch, who is the majority stakeholder in the fair’s parent company MCH Group, was chatting with Iwan Wirth. I walked in behind George Economou, the shipping magnate with a wildly important private museum in Athens, and once inside, I saw the local collector Patricia Marshall walk past the Paula Cooper Gallery booth as American collector Glenn Fuhrman walked by the exit.

Were they buying the work? Evidently, someone was. Hauser & Wirth sold two different paintings, a Gerhard Richter and a Georg Baselitz, for around $10 million each on Tuesday. David Zwirner sold more than $36 million in work on Tuesday and Wednesday, including a Richter painting for $8.5 million. Gagosian sold a 1987 de Kooning for eight figures. White Cube sold two different Julie Mehretu works for $7.85 million each.

And galleries were happy to provide examples of works by the artist with the most visible work in Paris: the only artwork projected on an entire stretch of the riverbank. Spruth Magers, which first started showing Holzer in 1986, brought a rare Holzer collaborative work with the graffiti artist Lady Pink that declares on canvas, “IF YOU ARE CONSIDERED USELESS NO ONE WILL FEED YOU ANYMORE.” Hauser & Wirth sold Providing, a 2024 work in 24k gold, antique gold, champagne gold, moon gold, platinum and red gold leaf, for $400,000.

Holzer was not actually at this unprecedented brief edition of Art Basel Paris. While Avant Première unfolded Tuesday, she was on the banks of the Seine, taking in the projections, musing about her French artist heroes.

“Monet was doubting whether his work was good enough even after he was successful, and wondered whether it measured up to the times,” she said. “He was agonized. Good man. This is when he was in water lily land. He was nailing it, and he was agonized. Which is why it was that good, because he wasn’t going out for a drinky-poo.”

I watched French text from Monet’s writings scroll up the Orsay.

“Not such a shabby facade,” Holzer said. “And look…”

One of the ferries going down the Seine came into view, in front of the museum. Holzer pointed.

“When the tourist boat goes by, the phones come out. So that’s kind of reassuring, right?” she said.

ÉCLAIRÉ, 2026 Light projection at Musée d’Orsay.

The projections are only temporary. But like her other ephemeral text-light-on-building installations, they will exist in images, and remain in the minds of those who see them. Maybe this work will remain tethered to this period in French history, as students protest for better education in a city that still reveres art and culture.

Even with the fair cut short by the protests, Holzer, as you might be pleasantly surprised to hear, is optimistic.

“There’s chronic persistent idealism in a lot of this content. Maybe that’ll be contagious,” she said. “That’s a dream, anyway.”

For at least a year now, newspapers have been increasingly filled with urgent and anxious reports about the quickening pace of artificial intelligence development, warning ominously that the technology will eventually defy human control and take over the world.

It is a threat worth taking seriously, and no mere hype. But with all the attention devoted to AI’s growing power, an even more imminent danger has become clear: With their prodigious fortunes, a small cast of men who lead the world’s most powerful AI-related companies have come to wield extraordinary and perhaps already-unstoppable power over U.S. government and society—and, thereby, unprecedented influence over world affairs.

The richest among them, Elon Musk, is the most spectacular example. He has exhibited no modesty about his ambitions as he has used his Midas-like wealth and tentacular corporations to become an irresistible force in contemporary affairs. The U.S. government now openly depends on Musk, who became a trillionaire in June, for its space program, for global communications, and for holding the country’s own in competition with China in frontier industries such as electric vehicles and batteries. But that is far from all.

Early in U.S. President Donald Trump’s second term, the White House accorded a leading role to Musk in shaping—and ostensibly streamlining—the country’s bureaucracy. It gave him not only unprecedented say over large-scale firings and the shuttering of long-standing government entities but also access to the personal data of millions of citizens.

A parallel and equally shocking example of never-before-seen personal influence over government came to light in recent days with the news that Ukraine’s access to the military-grade version of Musk’s internet services company, Starlink, had been interrupted or seriously degraded. Clarity about how this happened or who was responsible has been hard to come by, but the Wall Street Journal reported that Musk ordered the slowdown, badly hindering Ukraine’s offensive and defensive abilities in its ongoing war with Russia. Reuters, moreover, reported that the decision came from the White House. (Trump has denied involvement.)

No other company, or even country, has a system nearly as capable as Starlink, meaning that even the Pentagon, with a budget larger than the military spending of the next 10 defense powers combined, is reliant on Musk for these sorts of capabilities. And the lack of transparency about the chain of command—or the involvement or lack thereof of the U.S. government—in ordering changes that temporarily crippled another country’s defenses is ominous.

Musk, meanwhile, has begun to stealthily play an influential role in what are likely to be two of the most important public policy debates of the coming decades. The first is immigration. Although far from alone in this, Musk has advocated for sharply limiting migration to the West from nonwhite areas of the world. He has helped make post-apartheid South Africa a kind of poster child for this, pushing Trump to ease entry to the United States for members of South Africa’s white minority, whom Washington now pretends are the victims of gross persecution.

Musk’s other big, stealthy public policy play involves the future of Social Security and taxation. Many projections suggest that at current payout rates, the United States’ Social Security system will soon run out of funds, requiring some combination of postponed retirement for millions, greatly reduced benefits, or increased taxation. This is where the ultrarich would step in, creating further government dependence on them.

Musk recently announced that he would give one share of his SpaceX company stock to every U.S. child, a gesture whose value is estimated to be worth as much as $11 billion. Analysts have pointed out that, far from altruism, this would save Musk billions in tax. Other AI-related giants such as Anthropic and Dell, increasingly an AI services provider, have also begun to offer stock or cash donation programs.

Although Musk has not said as much, this also seems likely to be part of a bid by the ultra-wealthy to secure a powerful and potentially lucrative position for themselves in the looming struggle over the U.S. retirement system, via a kind of creeping privatization—a long-standing objective in conservative politics. By donating shares or providing child allowances, these individuals and their companies can profit not only from write-offs, but also perhaps through the management of financial services and fostering government reliance on their mega-corporations, much as the U.S. military depends on Musk’s Starlink.

A historical consensus has already begun to form around the idea that the increasing concentration of wealth, along with the rise of historically unprecedented fortunes stemming from AI and the broader internet and computer economy, is driving a decisive shift in the politics and economy of the United States, along with its democracy. On the country’s present path, the risk this presents is what political scientists and economists call state capture: a blurring of the lines between private and public interests, in which the former gradually predominates.

If the diagnosis is correct, notions about what to do about this have been much slower to coalesce, and that is no accident. Just this week, news emerged that in response to a proposed new tax on the ultrarich in California, Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg was considering leaving the state, where his company is based, and then hastily arranged changes in his corporate compensation. Despite being one of the world’s richest men, Zuckerberg has long received a mere $1 in annual pay from Meta, with the bulk of his fortune coming in the form of company shares. With California considering taxing assets like these instead of income alone, Zuckerberg got his board to create a new class of nonvoting shares that he could sell to pay any new state tax bill without diluting his controlling interest in the company. Other AI-related business leaders in California, where the industry is largely centered, have contributed to a campaign against the new tax measure, Proposition 40, which residents will vote on in November.

Jeff Bezos, the founder of Amazon, has found other ways to wield his influence. Like Zuckerberg and other mega-billionaires, Bezos has contributed to some of Trump’s pet Washington construction projects. Bezos also owns the Washington Post. Once considered a close rival to the New York Times in terms of the breadth and aggressiveness of its government accountability journalism, the Post has undergone a transformation under Bezos, shedding staff and running far softer features than before.

As this has happened, the newspaper’s editorial pages have become openly pro-business, including the AI industry, and thereby much less concerned with speaking truth to power. Bezos himself announced the change in February 2025: “We are going to be writing every day in support and defense of two pillars: personal liberties and free markets. We’ll cover other topics too of course, but viewpoints opposing those pillars will be left to be published by others.”

It is hard to see Bezos’s decisions as separate from his interest in currying favor with the White House, given his company’s huge business dealings with the U.S. government and exposure to state regulation.

It is Musk, though, who has been the least inhibited about flexing his influence over government. In the run-up to next month’s midterm elections, in which Democrats are widely expected to take control of the House of Representatives, and possibly win the Senate, Musk recently issued a threat to anyone in Congress with ideas of investigating him. “Those who pick fights with me should understand that I will find out who is really pulling the strings and come for them,” he wrote on X.

This is a brazen statement, but Musk may feel he has reason enough to believe in his impunity. The man who has become the largest campaign financier in U.S. politics, to the tune of hundreds of millions of dollars in campaign donations to Republican candidates, likely sees the struggle for influence over the government as a long game in which his wealth and that of an allied class of new AI billionaires can be decisive. The question—and it is a fateful one—is whether the country’s civil society, and along with it, its democracy, can hold its own.
