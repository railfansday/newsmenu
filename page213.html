#3068. Rust? What rust? Brewers open World Series refreshed and ready thanks to all that winning

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

MILWAUKEE -- The Milwaukee Brewers won Game 1 of the World Series, 3-2, Friday night at American Family Field. It was their first win since Thursday, Oct. 15. They hadn't lost since then either, though; they had been off.

Any time a team has a long layoff in the postseason, we end up hearing the same tired song and dance. "Rust vs. Rest" is the succinct headline. The basic argument is that with baseball being a daily sport in the regular season, teams sitting around for multiple days between series -- or between the end of the regular season and the divisional series -- are at some sort of disadvantage because they'll come out rusty.

One example obviously doesn't disprove a theory -- though we have several more and we'll get to those -- but the Brewers are doing their best to kill that narrative.

In the first inning of World Series Game 1 on Friday, Jackson Chourio led off with a single. Christian Yelich tripled him home and then scored on a William Contreras single. In the span of four batters, the team that spent seven full days without playing a game just scored two runs. Nick Martinez, on the mound for the Rays, wasn't great in the second half, but he was an All-Star this season and was coming off an outing in which he allowed only one run in more than five innings against the Guardians in the ALCS.

Was Contreras rusty? After that RBI single in the first, he threw out two runners trying to steal and nailed both of his ABS challenges. It sure seems like a rusty catcher wouldn't be so excellent behind the plate at judging balls and strikes.

"Contreras was unbelievable," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said after the game. "The two caught stealings, the ABS, the hits, calling the game.

" ... Now, we did not play great. You can look back and we made a baserunning mistake, we made an execution mistake. We made a defensive mistake. We weren't perfect. But after being off for some time, I'm pleased we came out in the first inning ready to go."

The wait between sweeping the Dodgers in the NLDS and Game 1 of the World Series wasn't the Brewers' only break. They have now played just nine games in 26 days. They've won eight of them. It sort of blows up that "rusty" argument, doesn't it?

As all teams with long layoffs do, the Brewers had plenty of practices. These days, that's not just swings off batting practice pitchers; Trajekt technology uses holograms of real pitchers and recreates everything about their pitches, including spin rate, velocity, movement, the works. The tech has gotten so good that players have started bypassing minor-league rehab assignments after injury absences and instead just getting back up to game speed using the Trajekt machines.

"We played baseball, that's how we prepared," Murphy said on Thursday before their team workout. "We went out there and played baseball every day, live at-bats. You can't recreate real, you know what I mean? It's a long layoff, and if we want to use it as an excuse, then let people use it as an excuse, but I refuse to do that."

I'm glad he said something about excuses, because some of the narratives we've seen used with teams after layoffs in the past are tantamount to excuse-making. When high-seeded teams lose a game or even a series, people love to cling to excuses instead of just accepting that baseball in small samples lends itself to variance.

Sure, we've heard former players making claims like your body doesn't adjust well to not playing games for multiple days after having played nearly every day for six months, and maybe they do feel that, but the numbers tell us otherwise.

The 2022 and 2023 Braves seemed to be the poster child for this under the current playoff format, which debuted in 2022. How many times did we hear about how unfair it was that the top two seeds were forced to sit around and get rusty while the lower seeds get to play meaningful baseball, giving them a supposed edge?

That very argument ignored the top-seeded Astros in 2022. They got a bye and then swept the Mariners in the ALDS, waited a few more days off and then swept the Yankees in the ALCS. They had another long layoff before the World Series and then beat the Phillies six games. They went 11-2 in all, the best record in this iteration of the playoffs. If the Brewers sweep here, they'll beat it.

In the current playoff format, teams with at least five days of rest before Game 1 have gone 14-10 in Games 1. You might think 10 losses is too many, but that record is a 162-game pace of 95 wins, and these are obviously all games against playoff teams. The rested teams are good, not rusty.

Let's get back to the Brewers. They earned the spotlight just as they've been doing all playoffs and, really, nearly the entire season. They earned the rest during their bye by having the best record in baseball with 103 wins. They earned their days off before the NLCS by getting rid of the Padres in four games instead of five. They earned more than a week of rest before the World Series by taking out the two-time defending champion Dodgers in a four-game sweep.

We already discussed the offense and Contreras behind the plate, but the Brewers again played great defense overall. There was one misplay in center in the fourth inning, but it was on a hard line drive right at Garrett Mitchell and those are always difficult to judge. The scorekeeper ruled it a double anyway, not an error.

Jacob Misiorowski, who has now thrown more than 40 innings over his previous career-high workload, saw his velocity tick up over his season average. He hadn't pitched since Oct. 11 and got deeper into the game than his previous two playoff starts.

The Brewers are a team that shortens games with their bullpen, and while they have depth, they'd prefer to ride their three back-inning beasts in Abner Uribe, Aaron Ashby and Trevor Megill. With all that rest, that trio has been able to impact nearly every game. Those three combined for four innings of work to close down Game 1 and they'll likely all be available for a similar workload in Game 2. In fact, even if they combine for four innings again in Game 2, there's a day off before Game 3, so it wouldn't be surprising to see this exact formula for each of the first three games. Hell, they could do it in Game 4, too.

Murphy himself said the rest was good for his squad.

"We needed a little bit of a break," he said on Thursday. "It was an emotional series in L.A. Cross-country trip, all that. We had a few days where it was a little lighter, but then we've picked it up and gotten a lot of live ABs, but nothing simulates the game the way it is."

The rest is what is setting the Brewers up for what could well be a historic run. Again, they've only played nine games in the last 26 days, but that is because they played such great baseball that they earned their rest. Winning means rest, and rest means winning.

In order for the Rays to win the World Series, they'll now have to beat the Brewers four times in the next five or six games. To find the Brewers' last four losses, we'd have to go back to Sept. 13. They've gone 18-3 since then, despite having all those days off.

They didn't forget how to play baseball. They may just have forgotten how to lose.

#5490. US Tech Leaders Are Urgently Calling for Rules on AI — China Already Has Them

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Recent warnings from American AI leaders have created the impression that the world is only beginning to confront the dangers of advanced artificial intelligence.

Dario Amodei, chief executive of tech company Anthropic, has called for governments to intervene to slow the development of increasingly powerful AI models.

His proposal begins with independent evaluators embedded inside AI companies, moves on to regulation and progresses to government-backed coordination among democratic states.

Ultimately, it seeks global agreements with China on testing of AI systems, dangerous applications of the technology and the pace of AI development.

Yet there is a China-shaped hole in this narrative. The Asian superpower is portrayed as the difficult final participant in a governance system that the US and its allies must first create.

In fact, China has already established an extensive system of AI rules, assessment, filing, technical standards and government oversight. Here’s how China’s AI governance system has developed in stages alongside the technology itself.

Beijing’s 2022 rules on algorithmic recommendation services apply to technologies that select, rank, filter or recommend online content to users.

They compel companies that use algorithms to examine how they work, to protect the personal information of users, to give users certain controls, and to introduce safeguards for workers, consumers, children and older people.

China’s deep synthesis provisions, effective from January 2023, extended regulation to synthetic (or AI-generated) text, images, audio and video. AI companies must authenticate users, protect data, keep relevant records, assess their systems and identify synthetic material that could be mistaken for authentic content.

The interim measures for generative artificial intelligence services were introduced in August 2023. These added rules for public-facing generative AI, particularly those that can influence public opinion or persuade uses in other ways.

The measures apply to the legality and quality of training data, users’ personal information, intellectual property and discrimination based on protected characteristics. The rules also cover transparency by AI companies and the accuracy and reliability of products.

Under the rules, companies must address problems found in their services. These services must undergo a security assessment to examine their risks and compliance with Chinese legislation. The companies must also file information about their algorithms with Chinese authorities.

These mechanisms are not identical to the independent evaluators that would be embedded in AI companies, as has been proposed by Amodei. But they perform some of the same functions.

Beijing’s latest set of measures is called the AI safety governance framework 3.0, published in September 2026. It sets out a broad approach to identifying, grading and responding to risks throughout the lifecycle of AI systems.

Building on earlier versions, the framework covers AI model and data risks, vulnerabilities in open source models (those which can be freely modified by users), AI’s effects on employment and society and increasingly autonomous systems.

It includes a dedicated mechanism for managing risks associated with rapidly developing AI agents (systems that carry out tasks in response to instructions by a user).

The framework addresses some of the same dangers now generating alarm in Silicon Valley, including highly autonomous systems, the misuse of chemical, biological and nuclear technologies, and the possibility of humans losing control of advanced AI. It proposes continual safety evaluations, risk based safeguards, humans being in charge, and emergency intervention mechanisms.

An independent analysis by the US Carnegie Endowment for International Peace thinktank has described the framework as China’s attempt to “to advance frontier safety without the need for heavy handed regulatory interventions”.

None of this means that Chinese AI rules should be transplanted elsewhere. Its institutions, regulatory traditions and technological priorities are different.

Many functions now presented as urgent aspirations in the US, including evaluation, accountability for AI companies, risk classification for AI systems, regulatory filing, technical standards and government oversight are already embedded in the governance architecture of the world’s other AI superpower.

The US is free to conclude that a market-led system best serves its economic and strategic interests. The Trump administration has favoured a “minimally burdensome” national approach to AI and has sought to limit more demanding state-level regulation.

But a market-led model cannot mean privatising the rewards of AI while exporting its risks. US companies may capture the commercial gains, while governments elsewhere are left to investigate security incidents, protect public systems and absorb the wider costs when those technologies fail. The OpenAI agent that gained unauthorised access to Australia’s state healthcare service shows how AI risks are being exported.

If Washington rejects extensive controls before systems are released, the US needs a credible legal regime for assessing who is liable for damage caused by those AI models. Otherwise, countries dependent on foreign AI systems will face increasingly difficult choices about whose technology to trust, and on what terms.

Silicon Valley’s governing ethos has long been to “move fast and break things”. That was always a questionable principle for technologies woven deeply into public life. It is untenable for companies whose own leaders now warn that their products could cause catastrophic harm.

Beijing has already surrounded its AI sector with public obligations, regulatory oversight and an evolving framework of risk assessment. A global debate that treats such governance as a future aspiration should at least recognise that one country is already ahead of the curve.

By Madeline Carr, Professor of Global Politics and Cybersecurity, UCL

#608. This 150-million-year-old bird had dinosaur teeth and a strange secret

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A remarkably preserved 150-million-year-old bird fossil discovered in China is challenging long-standing ideas about how birds evolved. Rather than developing increasingly sophisticated flight abilities, some of the earliest birds appear to have taken a very different evolutionary path, including losing the ability to fly altogether.

The newly identified species, Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, lived during the Late Jurassic period (150-148 million years ago). Described in a study published in Nature, the crow-sized creature provides evidence that early bird evolution was much more complex than scientists once believed. Instead of evolving primarily to become better flyers, ancient birds were adapting to a variety of environments and ways of life, including those that favored living on the ground.

Researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and the Fujian Institute of Geological Sciences uncovered the fossil in the recently recognized Zhenghe Fauna of Fujian Province in southeastern China. According to their findings, Yuezhuornis represents the earliest known example of a bird lineage losing its ability to fly.

An Ancient Bird With Dinosaur Teeth and Claws

One of the most remarkable features of Yuezhuornis is its unusual combination of bird and dinosaur characteristics. Although it possessed skeletal features associated with birds, it also retained anatomical traits that would have been familiar among small predatory dinosaurs.

Its jaws contained closely spaced, serrated teeth resembling those of deinonychosaurs, a group of meat-eating dinosaurs that included agile predators related to birds. Such teeth are not normally associated with early birds.

Even more striking was its second toe, which could bend backward unusually far and carried a curved, sickle-shaped claw. This specialized feature is strongly associated with deinonychosaurs, including dromaeosaurids and troodontids, two groups of predatory dinosaurs.

"When we examined Yuezhuornis, we were struck by its extraordinary mix of features," said Dr. Min Wang, corresponding author of the study. "It possesses bird-like pelvic and hindlimb structures yet retains the specialized predatory foot and teeth of a small meat-eating dinosaur. It shows us that early bird evolution was far more complicated than we previously realized."

Where Does This Strange Jurassic Bird Fit in Evolution?

To understand how Yuezhuornis was related to other prehistoric birds and dinosaurs, scientists carried out detailed analyses of its evolutionary relationships. They used two approaches, maximum parsimony and Bayesian tip-dating, to compare anatomical characteristics and reconstruct the species' position on the evolutionary tree.

Maximum parsimony identifies evolutionary relationships that require the fewest changes in characteristics, while Bayesian tip-dating also incorporates statistical estimates of evolutionary history and information about when fossils lived.

Both methods produced consistent results, placing Yuezhuornis within Avialae, the evolutionary group that includes modern birds and their closest extinct relatives.

"The results consistently placed Yuezhuornis firmly within Avialae (the group containing modern birds and their closest extinct relatives), just crownward of Archaeopteryx and as a sister taxon to Zhengheornis," said Dr. Zhonghe Zhou, a co-author of the study.

This placement is particularly important because Archaeopteryx, which also lived roughly 150 million years ago, is among the most famous early bird relatives. The findings indicate that Yuezhuornis belonged to an early branch of bird evolution rather than simply being a small predatory dinosaur with bird-like characteristics.

Evidence That Some Early Birds Lost the Ability to Fly

The researchers also examined how Yuezhuornis might have lived. Using measurements of its skeletal proportions and statistical comparisons with other animals, they investigated whether its anatomy was better suited for flying or moving along the ground.

The results showed that its limb proportions closely resembled those of modern ground-dwelling birds and certain other prehistoric dinosaurs closely related to birds. This similarity suggests that Yuezhuornis spent its life on the ground and was likely unable to fly.

The findings point to an unexpected development in the history of birds. Rather than simply progressing toward stronger wings and more efficient flight, some early bird lineages appear to have moved in the opposite direction, adapting to lifestyles in which flying was no longer necessary.

This challenges the traditional assumption that the earliest stages of bird evolution were driven primarily by the demands of flight. Instead, birds were already exploring different ecological roles and developing a surprisingly wide range of body forms.

A 150-Million-Year-Old Discovery Rewrites Bird Evolution

The discovery of Yuezhuornis suggests that the evolution of birds was never a simple, one-directional process. Even during the Late Jurassic, when the earliest bird lineages were emerging, some species were developing adaptations that favored life on land rather than in the air.

Its unusual combination of bird-like anatomy, dinosaur-style predatory features, and apparent flightlessness provides a revealing glimpse into that evolutionary experimentation. The fossil shows that early birds were more anatomically and ecologically diverse than previously recognized.

It also underscores the scientific importance of the Zhenghe Fauna, a fossil assemblage that is helping researchers investigate a poorly understood period in bird evolution. As additional discoveries emerge from this region of China, scientists may gain an even clearer picture of the surprising evolutionary paths taken by the earliest birds.

#683. Glioblastoma breakthrough reveals how brain cancer cells become more aggressive

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Glioblastoma is among the most lethal forms of brain cancer, and scientists have uncovered a surprising factor that may help its cells become more invasive. The fluid surrounding the outer edges of these tumors is much thicker than the fluid near their centers, forcing cancer cells to encounter increasing resistance as they move into healthy brain tissue. Rather than simply slowing their movement, however, this environment appears to help the cells adapt in ways that make invasion easier.

To investigate this unexpected behavior, researchers created an innovative microfluidic chip that recreates the physical conditions encountered by glioblastoma cells. They found that spending extended periods in thick fluid caused the cells to become smaller, more flexible, and better able to squeeze through narrow openings. The discovery suggests that fluid viscosity does more than obstruct cancer cells. It can actively influence their behavior and potentially make them more aggressive.

How Thick Fluid Influences Brain Cancer Invasion

Cancer research has traditionally concentrated on chemical signals and the stiffness of the tissues surrounding tumors. Far less attention has been given to the viscosity of the fluid through which cancer cells travel.

In glioblastoma, this difference is particularly striking. Fluid at the tumor's advancing edge is approximately eight times more viscous than fluid in its dead tissue at the center. As cancer cells move outward, they must push through an increasingly resistant environment.

Studying this process has been difficult because conventional closed microfluidic devices cannot accurately reproduce these conditions. Their enclosed channels can limit access to oxygen and nutrients, while friction against the walls introduces additional forces that influence cell behavior. These limitations also make it challenging to observe how cancer cells respond to prolonged exposure to viscous fluid.

Researchers therefore needed a way to isolate the effects of fluid viscosity from other mechanical influences and determine whether extended exposure produces lasting changes in glioblastoma cells.

A New Device Reveals How Cancer Cells Adapt

Scientists from Chongqing General Hospital and Chongqing University in China developed a specialized open microfluidic platform to address these challenges. Their findings were published in Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

The device consists of a two-layer open microfluidic membrane equipped with a removable cap and an array of tiny pillars. This arrangement allows researchers to control precisely when cancer cells begin migrating while observing how their nuclei change shape as they move through confined spaces.

Unlike traditional enclosed systems, the platform also supports continuous cell culture for up to one month. This makes it possible to examine how prolonged mechanical stress changes the movement and invasive behavior of glioblastoma cells.

The researchers tested two human glioblastoma cell lines, U-251 and LN-229. Both were grown for a month in a thick fluid designed to reproduce conditions at the tumor's invasive outer edge (7.1 cP).

When the cells were subsequently examined using the chip, those that had adapted to the viscous environment traveled faster and covered greater distances than untreated control cells. This result was especially surprising because thicker fluid would ordinarily be expected to impede movement.

Brain Cancer Cells Become Smaller and More Flexible

Microscopic observations revealed how the adapted cells overcame this resistance. After prolonged exposure to the viscous fluid, they had become smaller and easier to deform, allowing them to maneuver through the narrow gaps between the device's microscopic pillars.

As the cells squeezed through these restricted passages, their nuclei underwent visible deformation. Researchers also observed an accumulation of YAP, a protein involved in sensing mechanical forces, inside the nuclei. This is a recognized sign that cells have activated pathways involved in responding to physical stress.

Although both glioblastoma cell lines developed similar changes in shape and movement, their underlying molecular responses were unexpectedly different.

U-251 cells underwent a form of mesenchymal-like reprogramming, activating genes associated with cancer invasion, including CD44, FN1, and MMP9. LN-229 cells, by contrast, became more flexible and mobile without showing substantial lasting changes in gene expression.

Further analysis using Western blotting showed that protein changes remained detectable even after cells were transferred back into fluid with normal viscosity. This indicated that at least some of the adaptations were persistent rather than simply short-lived responses to their surroundings.

Scientists Discover a Strange Kind of Cellular Memory

One of the most unexpected findings was that exposure to thick fluid appeared to leave a lasting imprint on glioblastoma cells.

The researchers had anticipated that viscosity would primarily function as a physical obstacle. Instead, their experiments showed that prolonged exposure could alter how cells behaved even after the original mechanical conditions were removed.

This phenomenon points to a form of mechanical memory, in which cells retain changes produced by earlier physical stress.

The open design of the new chip was essential for identifying this effect. By separating resistance from the fluid itself from the confinement imposed by channel walls, the researchers could investigate viscosity without the additional mechanical interference that complicates conventional experiments.

Observing changes in nuclear shape and migration patterns after weeks of exposure provided evidence that glioblastoma cells can adapt to their mechanical environment in lasting ways.

The contrasting molecular responses of the two cell lines were particularly revealing. Although both appeared to undergo similar physical transformations, only one showed extensive changes in its gene activity. This suggests that glioblastoma cells may acquire comparable invasive characteristics through different underlying biological processes.

A Potential New Approach to Studying Brain Cancer Treatments

Beyond uncovering an overlooked influence on glioblastoma invasion, the researchers believe their device could provide a valuable tool for investigating potential cancer therapies.

The open microfluidic platform fits into standard multi-well plates, allowing it to be incorporated into existing laboratory procedures for cell culture and imaging. Its accessible design also enables researchers to stain cells directly and observe them over long periods without the clogging problems that can occur in closed systems.

These features could make it useful for testing drugs that interfere with the cellular pathways responsible for detecting and responding to mechanical forces.

For glioblastoma, the results raise the possibility that highly viscous environments may favor cancer cells that are better adapted to invasion. Treatments targeting YAP signaling or the remodeling of the cytoskeleton, the internal framework that helps cells maintain and change their shape, could therefore be investigated under conditions that more closely resemble those inside tumors.

The technology may also prove useful for studying other cancers in which fluid viscosity varies across different regions. By examining how tumor cells respond to these mechanical conditions, researchers could gain a better understanding of why some cancers are particularly invasive and potentially identify patients whose tumors depend on mechanical adaptation to spread.

Although further research is needed to determine how these laboratory findings translate to tumors in patients, the discovery highlights an important and previously underexplored aspect of cancer biology: the physical environment surrounding tumor cells may actively shape their ability to invade healthy tissue.
