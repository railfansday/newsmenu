Few players have had a career as decorated as Neymar’s, and the MSN trio he formed with Lionel Messi and Luis Suarez at Barcelona marked an era in world soccer.

The three forwards dominated defenses across Europe and led the club to a historic treble in 2015, winning LaLiga, the Copa del Rey and the Champions League. Even so, Neymar feels his best version did not come during that stage of his career.

The Brazilian made that clear while speaking with Argentine streamer Davoo Xeneize in a video on the creator’s YouTube channel, where he named Paris Saint-Germain as the club where he reached his prime.

“For me, the prime was at Paris Saint-Germain, when I played there. People remember Barcelona because of the MSN (Messi, Suarez, Neymar), but as a player I think PSG was my prime because I had strength, speed and intelligence there, I already knew the whole pitch, and for me that was my best version,” he said.

The answer stands out given Neymar’s four seasons in Catalonia between 2013 and 2017, before his move to PSG that summer. In Paris he won four Ligue 1 titles, and his reasoning has more to do with his maturity as a player, with the physical and tactical knowledge he had gained, than with trophies.

The statistics help explain Neymar’s view. With Barcelona, he scored 105 goals in 186 official matches, with 2014-15 standing out as his best campaign, when he netted 39 times in 51 games. In Paris, he scored 118 goals in 173 matches, a better goals-per-game rate than in Spain, and became the club’s fourth all-time top scorer.

The trophy cabinets tell a different story. Neymar won ten major trophies at Barcelona, including two LaLiga titles, three Copa del Rey trophies and the Champions League, all of them alongside Messi. At PSG he collected 13 domestic trophies, led by five Ligue 1 crowns, but never won a European title.

Whether his prime came in Catalonia or in Paris, Neymar’s career leaves little doubt about his talent, and the debate over which era was his best is likely to continue among fans. Now back at Santos, the club where it all began, the Brazilian can at least say he has chosen his own answer.

#3668. This New Garmin Feature Is Draining Battery Life, but There's a Quick Fix

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Garmin users are taking to the company's support forums with a common grievance: The newly introduced "OK Garmin" hands-free voice feature appears to be eating through battery life. The feature arrived through Garmin’s September software updates, bringing hands-free voice activation to the Fenix 8, Forerunner 570 and 970, Venu 4, and several other models. Here's how to make sure Garmin's new updates aren't slashing your watch's battery life.

Last month's updates for select watches also brought all-day fall detection, a set of new Quick Workouts and Garmin Run Coach plans, as well as that "OK Garmin" hands-free voice command feature. It's exactly what it sounds like: Instead of pressing a button, users can simply say “OK Garmin” to activate voice commands. However, it looks like "OK Garmin" in particular is what causes some watch's projected runtimes to drastically drop, allegedly by roughly 35% to 50% in some cases.

Garmin does acknowledge that enabling these settings shortens battery life, though it doesn't say by how much. The tradeoff makes sense, since the watch has to listen constantly for its wake phrase. Even so, giving up several days of runtime is a steep price for a feature that many people may seldom use. Luckily, the fix is simple: Turning off "OK Garmin" should restore your battery life estimates right away.

If your watch has been draining faster since the update, go to Settings > System > Voice and disable "OK Garmin." If needed, you can also switch off the Phone Assistant wake word from the same area. The good news is that turning this feature off won't even take voice commands away—you can still trigger them manually using the watch's physical controls.

Keep in mind that these numbers are battery estimates rather than independently tested, real-world runtimes. Some of the battery complaints that surfaced after the update may also stem from unrelated issues. Still, trading away as much as half your battery life just to avoid pressing a button is a difficult bargain to defend.

Pawlikowski has picked his moment well. On April 26, 1945, at the conference in San Francisco that created the United Nations, U.S. Secretary of State Edward Stettinius lamented “how close our whole civilization has come to utter ruin.” That conference played a key part in building a new global settlement that aimed to ensure, through guaranteed rights and freedoms, through mutual trade and prosperity, that such a catastrophe could never happen again.

But today, that postwar order is coming under ever heavier bombardment. Predictions of its destruction are commonplace. It’s a good time to look through the lens of the 1940s, and contemplate just why that order was created in the first place.

Back then, Hollywood was filming in the ruins for real. Between 1947 and 1949, four great directors each went to Germany and shot an American spin on what the postwar Germans called the Trümmerfilm, or “rubble movie.”

The 1940s was perhaps Hollywood’s apotheosis. The war fostered new depth and seriousness; television had not yet weakened cinema’s dominion. This was the era of Humphrey Bogart, Ava Gardner, and film noir; of movies like Casablanca, Double Indemnity, and Notorious. The four rubble films themselves are hardly classics, but between them, they captured how a newly super-powerful United States was trying to understand how the world should be rebuilt. They dramatized the dilemmas and nightmares Americans faced as they decided whether to entangle themselves with Europe in the long term. Today, they remind us that the creation of the settlement we’re watching die was driven not only by idealism, but by guilt, hard-faced realism, and fear.

In Jacques Tourneur’s Berlin Express, shot in summer 1947, the wreckage of Frankfurt—the capital of the U.S. zone—stands for the dangers of failing to build a new, peaceful world. Agents of the revanchist Nazi underground kidnap a revered advocate of international unity to prevent him attending a conference on German reunification, hiding him in a derelict brewery.

This dramatized the dread felt by many, not least in Hollywood, that desperate Germans could all too easily turn to fascism once again. Among the attendees at that 1945 San Francisco conference was Orson Welles, who reported in his column in the New York Post that in the hotel lobbies and committee rooms, “the phony fear of Communism is smoke-screening the real menace of renascent Fascism.” Following this logic, Berlin Express strains to cast the Soviets as just one of the four friendly wartime allies.

But all this is undercut by an overarching, noir-ish sense of uncertainty. As a worldly Frenchwoman tells the film’s naive U.S. liberal, Europeans have learned the hard way to maintain “suspicion of everyone and everything.” Even Hollywood couldn’t quite gloss over the impossibility of the dream of a one-world utopia.

That same summer, another Hollywood director, Billy Wilder, was in Berlin, shooting a film that was much less optimistic about the United States’ chances of transforming Europe. Wilder was born to Jewish parents in Galicia, then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire; his career began in Berlin before he had to flee in 1933, first to France, then the United States. This was not his first return to Germany. In 1945, he had served as head of the film unit of the U.S. Information Control Division and had been involved with making Die Todesmühlen (Death Mills), a grueling 20-minute montage of footage from the liberated death camps, designed to force Germans to look their starvation-ravaged victims in the eye. Among the Holocaust’s victims were Wilder’s mother, stepfather, and grandmother.

The movie Wilder shot in 1947, A Foreign Affair, uses the city’s broken buildings to confront Americans with the moral ruin that Nazism, bombing, and invasion had wrought on the German population—and the degrading effect that this was having on U.S. soldiers. Characteristically, this is played as a screwball comedy, sending up the difficulty of “denazifying” people who are scrabbling in the rubble to survive.

Millard Mitchell (center) and Marlene Dietrich in A Foreign Affair (1948).Paramount Pictures/Sunset Boulevard/Getty Image

The film follows a pious Iowa congresswoman who arrives intent on tackling the moral turpitude of fräulein-chasing GIs. (In this, she is reflecting an outdated domestic U.S. attitude: Soldiers had so widely ignored rules forbidding fraternization with civilians that the ban had been scrapped.) The congresswoman presents an army captain with a birthday cake baked by his beloved back home—which he promptly barters on the black market for a mattress for his mistress, Erika von Schlütow, a nightclub singer and former consort of a Nazi general, who now scrapes along in a derelict building.

Erika, played by Wilder’s old Berlin friend and fellow Hollywood exile Marlene Dietrich, is the picture of European worldliness. As she explains, “We’ve all become animals with exactly one instinct left—self-preservation.” She invites the congresswoman to imagine what it was like “to be a woman in this town when the Russians first swept in.”

Wilder has no solution to any of this; he was just trying to coax Americans to be honest about the depth of the problem they were trying to solve. Unsurprisingly, government officials soon pressured his studio to pull the film from U.S. cinemas. It was not seen in Germany until 1977.

Like Wilder, Fred Zinnemann grew up Jewish in the Austro-Hungarian Empire and began his movie career in Berlin before moving to Hollywood, only to lose close family to the Holocaust. While his old colleague was shooting in Berlin, Zinnemann was at work in the ruins of Ingolstadt, Munich, Nuremberg, and Würzburg. He too wanted Americans to face what had happened in Europe, but his approach was both more emotional and more practical, explicitly intended to raise awareness of one of the war’s most painful consequences.

The Search is based on reports of the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Association (UNRRA) about the teams trying to reunite lost and displaced children with any surviving family, and on interviews the director conducted with some of the children in UNRRA’s care. A few of them appear as characters in the film, blankly recounting stories of murder and enslavement. Zinnemann consciously left out the worst of what he had heard, reasoning that U.S. audiences would not be able to stomach it.

Movie poster for The Search (1948).LMPC/Getty Images

His film uses Germany’s ruins to evoke the destruction of the most basic human bonds—trust, motherhood, medical care, kindness. It follows a 9-year-old Czech boy, Karel Malik, who has survived Auschwitz but can barely speak. He has forgotten his name and his family. When he and his friend are put in an ambulance for medical treatment, past experience tells them they are going to be gassed, so they break out and flee into the rubble. Trying to swim across a river to escape the uniformed officials giving chase, Karel’s friend drowns. All this is intercut with Mrs. Malik’s refusal to give up searching for her son, even as each is told the other is dead. Finally, thanks to the compassion of an English UNRRA official and of a U.S. soldier (played by Montgomery Clift), mother and son are reunited, years after their cultured family life was torn apart.

Zinnemann’s film is pointedly international in its focus—the displaced children speak Polish, Czech, French, German; the adults trying to help them are from various Allied nations. It well expresses the horror that informed the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in December 1948, a few months after the film’s release, and the 1950 European Convention on Human Rights.

Yet in all three of these films, there is something missing. As the critic J.E. Smyth has noted, Zinnemann had chosen to make his young protagonist Czech to draw attention to the newly free Republic of Czechoslovakia as a symbol of “future European regeneration.” But by the time it premiered in March 1948, Prague had fallen to a Stalinist coup. The new government banned The Search; the young actor who played Karel was forbidden to travel to the United States to receive his special juvenile Oscar. For all their sincere objections to tyranny, none of these films criticized Stalin’s Soviet Union—not even Wilder’s, even though the director had done so in 1939’s Ninotchka—despite its advance across Eastern Europe, and its brutalizing of both German civilians and displaced persons. As should have been obvious, the fear of Stalinism wasn’t “phony” after all.

Within months of the Czech coup, the Soviets had blockaded Berlin, determined to starve the city into submission and force the western Allies out. Instead, they launched a vast rescue operation, flying in supplies for 2 million West Berliners for over a year. This prompted the final U.S. rubble film, George Seaton’s The Big Lift—which captures what had happened to the one-eye-shut idealism of 1947. When Seaton started shooting in July 1949, the North Atlantic Treaty was a few weeks old; by the time he wrapped, China had fallen to communism and the Soviets had the atom bomb.

Montgomery Clift (left) and Paul Douglas in The Big Lift (1950). 20th Century-Fox/Getty Images

Like The Search, The Big Lift stars Clift as a warm-hearted U.S. officer—a pilot assigned to the airlift. But this time, his kindness leaves him open to being duped by a desperate German woman hiding a Nazi past. This makes the film an instance of an ideological turn toward anti-totalitarianism: hostile both to the Nazi past and the present Soviet threat.

This was distinct from the vicious hard-right anti-communism that was spreading at the same time and had spurred the House Un-American Activities Committee to summon Hollywood leftists to Washington, D.C., to be show-trialled. The Big Lift specifically sets its face against the United States’ own aggressive authoritarianism. Clift’s fellow officer, a Polish American called Hank Kowalski, is shown struggling to overcome his drive to revenge himself for the torture he endured as a prisoner of war, and his tendency to tyrannize his German girlfriend. She forces him first to make the case for democracy, and then to live up to it, citing the U.S. Constitution at him, and even calling him a “storm trooper.” She also calls out U.S. antisemitism.

Hollywood’s capitulation to the Red Scare is sometimes exaggerated: This was far from the only film that struck a liberal, pro-democratic, anti-totalitarian stance. And while Sen. Joseph McCarthy had launched his mendacious reign of terror by the time The Big Lift came out, this strain of liberal anti-totalitarianism long outlasted him.

But today, Fatherland is being released in a very different United States, where that ideology has been in retreat for years, terribly discredited by the war in Iraq, while the heirs to HUAC and McCarthy are in their pomp. The lesson from those old 1940s rubble films is that it’s not only the postwar order that lies in ruins. The robust liberal United States that once built that settlement is in dire need of reconstruction too.

#5501. New EV Battery Gives Gasmobiles The Deep Freeze

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The cost of EV batteries is spiraling downwards while the cost of gas and diesel is spiraling up. That’s a picture perfect scenario for accelerating the transition to EVs. The missing link is a level of battery performance that can meet, if not beat, a comparable internal combustion engine, and the Finnish innovator Donut Lab has just taken a -30 degree Celsius step in that direction.

If the name Donut Lab rings a bell, run right out and buy yourself a cigar. CleanTechnica is among the many (many, many) EV enthusiasts following the Donut Lab story, which is a rather unusual one.

The company unveiled its new solid-state EV battery at the Consumer Electronics Show in January, describing it as “the world’s first solid-state battery that is ready for use in OEM vehicle manufacturing,” while also pledging to share the results of third-party testing.

“With a full charge in five minutes and 400 Wh/kg of energy density, it seemed too good to be true. However, for any skeptics out there, Donut Lab is posting a new video series along with supporting material that explains the results of an independent assessment at the VTT Technical Research Centre of Finland,” CleanTechnica reported in January.

That’s right, VTT has been validating Donut Labs’s claims every step of the way, and the startup has been sharing those reports, in excruciating detail. In a bit of clap-back at skeptics who are challenging its claims, Donut Lab directs the curious to the VTT reports on its website at I Donut Believe.

Donut Labs has kept VTT hopping since the big unveil. In March, VTT issued a report that validated the new battery’s performance at 100°C — in other words, the boiling point. That led some Donut-watchers, including CleanTechnica’s Christopher Arcus, to conclude that the battery is not based on lithium, as some others have surmised.

“Speculation that the Donut Lab battery is not real are diminishing as independent lab data shows 100° Celsius performance. Speculation that the battery may be lithium run[s] into difficulty explaining how a battery can work at 100° Celsius,” Arcus observed.

“In the independent tests, the thin membrane surrounding the pouch breached. If the contents were a standard lithium-ion with liquid electrolyte, there would have been significant outgassing and the organic electrolyte would have caught fire,” he further noted.

In the latest news, earlier this week Donut Lab posted a new VTT report validating that the new battery retains 99% capacity at temperatures down to -30 degrees Celsius (-22 degrees Fahrenheit), a claim the company made at the CES unveiling. The cold weather validation is meaningful because it pulls the rug out from under EV critics, who are fond of claiming that cold weather significantly limits battery performance and range.

Cold weather can impact EV battery range, but then again, cold weather also puts a crimp on the fuel efficiency of gas-powered engines, too. Whether gas or electric, most drivers are in the habit of planning trips to make sure they don’t get stranded by the side of the road in cold weather or, for that matter, any weather, which lets a little steam out of the anti-EV argument.

Still, there is always room for improvement. EVs can be engineered with thermal management systems that protect against performance loss in extreme temperatures, but Donut Lab makes the case for doing away with external systems and building resilience into the battery itself.

The VTT test regimen involved a module consisting of 14 battery cells, placed in a temperature chamber at -30°C and left there for five hours. After the module was completely frozen through, tests showed that it retained 97% of its ampere-hour capacity, compared to a previous measurement at +25°C (about +77°F).

“As the report demonstrates, following battery capacity discharges performed during deep-freeze conditions, the module charges and discharges completely normally, with no adverse effects whatsoever resulting from the cold,” Donut Labs summarized in a press statement. The company further emphasized that the testing process did not include any heating, cooling, or insulation systems.

“We intentionally eliminated all the potential advantages a battery might normally have,” Donut Lab CTO Ville Piippo elaborated. “Despite this, the battery remained unaffected by the conditions. A typical lithium-ion battery would struggle to provide power, even for a brief moment, in such cold temperatures.”

Piippo also emphasized that the new battery eschews add-ons for temperature control. “This validates our promise of 99% capacity, which customers can expect from a fully insulated battery module without any heaters or tricks,” Piipp added for good measure.

Want more? Check out the YouTube video or download the VTT report.

Because Donut Labs is taking everyone on a step-by-step validation journey, there are still some steps left to climb before a whole-of-system report is conclusive. And, the big mystery wrapped around an enigma remains: what, exactly, is in this new battery?

That’s a good question. One likely candidate is — or was — the NMC (nickel-manganese-cobalt) formula, but that idea has sunk like a lead balloon.

Meanwhile, over here in the US both Ford and General Motors have teased that the Next Big Thing in EV batteries is the LMR (lithium manganese-rich formula). The idea is to compete with lower-costing LFP (lithium-iron-phosphate) batteries on cost, while out-performing LFP on energy density.

Last month, GM let word slip that its existing EV battery factory in Spring Hill, Tennessee, will begin producing LMR batteries for the electric mobility market.

Ford has been somewhat less forthcoming on the LMR topic of late, which is understandable because the company’s long-awaited Fathom electric pickup truck is poised for volume production next year. At a relatively affordable price point of just under $30,000 the truck will probably showcase LFP batteries.

At least, that was the case last year. “To really deliver truly affordable electric vehicles to our customers, we made a decision years ago to fully invest in a new facility with world-class LFP production technology installed in the U.S. for the first time — wholly owned and operated by Ford,”explained the company’s VP of Technology Platform Programs and EV Systems in an explainer posted in June of 2025.

#4064. As devastating Xbox "reset" continues, Dishonored studio Arkane Lyon reportedly only has until November to find a new buyer before it's closed

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After getting caught up in Xbox CEO Asha Sharma's devastating layoffs and cuts earlier this year, Arkane Lyon - the beloved immersive sim studio behind the likes of Dishonored and Deathloop - reportedly only has until November to find a buyer. If the search fails, it will be shut down.

Arkane Lyon was one of a number of first-party studios reportedly facing closure ahead of Xbox's massive "reset" in July. However, when the layoffs were officially announced - bringing confirmation that 3,200 Xbox employees will eventually lose their jobs as part of the sweeping restructuring - Microsoft revealed Psychonauts developer Double Fine, State of Decay studio Undead Labs, and South of Midnight developer Compulsion Games would go independent following negotiations.

Hellblade studio Ninja Theory is now likely to close after two acquisition deals fell through, only leaving the fate of Arkane Lyon - a studio Microsoft acquired in 2021 when it purchased ZeniMax - uncertain. At the time, Xbox said it was "beginning required consultation with its Works Council to review potential strategic options" regarding Arkane, but little has been heard since.

Now, a report by French publication Le Monde (via Google Translate) has shed additional light on the studio's fate. Speaking to the publication, Printemps Écologique and the Video Game Workers’ Union (STJV) said Xbox management had informed Arkane Lyon it was facing closure on 16th July. As Le Monde notes, however, Microsoft's plan was complicated by French law, which requires a company with more than 1,000 employees to spend three months seeking a new buyer for any operational arm it plans to close. Only then, if no buyer is found, can it be shut down.

According to Le Monde, the search - alongside a consultation process and negotiations regarding an employment protection plan - began this summer, and the deadline to find a buyer for the studio is imminent, ending on 13th November. If the closure occurs, 217 workers could lose their jobs.

However, a union representative told the publication redundancy negotiations, which seemingly include efforts to get Arkane workers redeployed to other Microsoft studios, are going "badly", and that unions are trying to buy more time in the hope of saving the studio and its jobs. Reportedly, "several forms of takeover are being considered."

In response to the report, Microsoft did not confirm or deny Arkane's proposed closure, but told Le Monde, "We continue to explore various options for Arkane as part of the ongoing French consultation process". It also said it was "keeping the Social and Economic Committee and our employees informed." Internal documents seen by the publication say "the implementation of the plan [for the total and definitive cessation of the company's operations] is scheduled to begin at the end of 2026 and is expected to continue throughout the first quarter of 2027."

Amid all this turmoil, it's unclear what might happen to Arkane Lyon's current project, Marvel's Blade. In July, Xbox insisted "none of [its] first-party publicly announced games or projects" would be cancelled as part of Sharma's cuts, suggesting Blade - which hasn't been seen since its announcement in 2023 - was safe. Earlier this year, the game was rumoured to be dead, but a follow-up report from The Verge claimed that while it was "over-budget", it was now targeting a release in 2027 after an internal delay.

Earlier this month, Sharma insisted Xbox had "started to return to growth" following the company's sweeping cuts, but reports suggest three more rounds of layoffs are still to come, possibly starting before October is through.

Field Notes from an Extinction: A Novel, Eoghan Walls, Seven Stories Press, 272 pp., $19.95, March 2026

Derry-born poet and creative writing lecturer Eoghan Walls evokes such monster-making narratives in his second novel, Field Notes from an Extinction. Set in 1847 during the Great Famine, the story follows English ornithologist Ignatius Green on a quest to study the world’s last colony of great auks—or garefowl—on Tor Mor, an uninhabited rock off the coast of the Irish island of Inishtrahull. The novel unfolds through Green’s field notebook, in which he depicts Irish people as freakish, grotesque, and animal-like.

Walls has crafted an immersive, unsettling meditation on morality and monstrification, or the act of denying the humanity of individuals or groups. In contrasting Green’s empathy for the auks with his lack of any similar sentiment for the colonized Irish, the novel illuminates how ostensibly objective scientific language and analytical frameworks can narrow the parameters of who is considered worthy of compassion.

The novel’s central plot begins when the residents of Inishtrahull upend Green’s plans to study the auks. Instead of delivering Green’s provisions to Tor Mor as agreed, the villagers requisition some for themselves and deposit in their place a feral child, the offspring of an allegedly murderous, cannibalistic Irishwoman. The irate Green must tend to the child, negotiate an encounter with her fearsome mother, fight poachers, and stretch rapidly dwindling resources—all while trying to study the auks.

When it comes to the Great Famine, Green understands that Ireland is suffering “a sickness of the body & the crops.” However, he doesn’t acknowledge that the British government’s refusal to control grain exports and implement effective relief measures exacerbates death tolls. By its end in 1852, the famine would kill roughly 1 million people; nearly 3 million would emigrate. But Green frames the scarcity of food mockingly and through his own comfort: He has “coal enough for weeks, as it appears the Irish cannot eat coal.”

Actors Jean Aubrey and Diane Aubrey look at the skeleton of Charles Byrne, also known as the Irish Giant, at the Hunterian Museum in London on April 1, 1959. Evening Standard/Hulton Archive via Getty Images

Green casts himself as a victim of robbery, an inadvertent accessory to human trafficking, and the survivor of attempted murder-by-abandonment. The rowboat sold to him for transportation by his provisions coordinator, Liam McGonigle, is a chicken-poop-encrusted “vessel of death” that leaks, “albeit slowly enough you can bail with a cup that has been attached to the seat by a length of twine for precisely this purpose.”

To Green, the islanders’ crime against the auks is even worse. In his notes, he accuses McGonigle and four accomplices who hunt and kill auks for the stuffed bird trade of “a deliberate act of extinction.” In reality, the last known sighting of a living great auk was off the coast of Newfoundland in 1852. (One of the last great auks in the British Isles was captured in Ireland in 1834. Fed an inappropriate diet, it eventually succumbed to a choking death by potato.)

Green rows to Inishtrahull to return the child to McGonigle, who refuses to take her back. Desperate to observe the imminent auk-hatching, Green returns reluctantly to Tor Mor with the child (a “dark day for science & the garefowl”), whose name, he learns later, is Bridget. It’s the first of many moral dilemmas he faces as he weighs scientific discovery against personal convenience, Bridget’s wants and needs, and even potential starvation.

Bridget’s experience is filtered through the lens of the crochety Green, who routinely describes her as he would an animal: “flaccid as shot game”; “naked as a piglet”; with “skin [like] roast pork.” Since she speaks no English and Green no Gaelic, he “[m]ight as well have chatted to the garefowl.” As he tries to clean her, brandishing taxidermy scissors to clip away her matted, lice-filled hair, he observes the “fuzz on her neck & shoulders” and contemplates whether she “remains within the realms of normality for a human child of hirsute lineage.”

Northern Ireland’s most outlandish plan comes to fruition in Jan Carson’s new novel.

Once cleaned and dressed, Green observes that if Bridget were given ringlets and scented in “liberal buckets of rosewater …  she could almost pass as English”—but that, “[s]haved as she is, the effect is more simian.” Indeed, Green approaches Bridget and the Irish like an anthropologist, drawing on a discipline pervaded with sexist, racist, and ethnocentric stereotypes in both method and terminology. He describes some of McGonigle’s relatives and various islanders as “scrawny, muttering in their guttural tongue”; and as “skinny monkey youths” resembling “sea-otters in their hirsuteness, weak shoulders & sloped brows.”

Such stereotypes date back at least as far the 12th century, when Ireland was invaded by descendants of England’s Norman conquerors. In the Middle Ages, chroniclers described Ireland as a land of barbarity, bristling with werewolves and human-ox hybrids. Ireland became England’s first Atlantic colony during the 16th century, when the English Crown confiscated Irish land and granted it to planters and military adventurers. By dehumanizing Irish people and denigrating their customs, English writers framed the conquest of Ireland as acceptable, even necessary.

An illustration featuring Irish Chief Secretary Arthur Balfour addressing a dummy representing the potato famine published in Moonshine in London on Oct. 4, 1890. Paul Popper/Popperfoto via Getty Images

Green’s animal metaphors—particularly those involving monkeys—thus activate a longstanding European shorthand. Medieval Christian theologians warned that people were merely conditionally human, their souls slipping back into those of beasts if they ignored the spiritual contract with God. Apes were the last animal God made before fashioning Adam, and it was with apes (broadly construed) that the least “civilized” people supposedly overlapped.

Narratives of “enfreakment,” meanwhile, framed bodily differences as spectacular errors of nature. In the 19th century, impresarios exhibited “bearded ladies” and “wild” individuals as acceptable viewing for family entertainment and scholarly study. At the height of the British Empire, when white Englishness was the ultimate aspiration, showmen highlighted how individuals departed from those norms. By pathologizing difference, they erected a permission structure for touching and gaping at people—especially women and girls—like Sarah Baartman, a Khoikhoi woman trafficked from South Africa, and Krao, an atypically hairy Laotian child whose captor advertised her as Darwin’s “missing link.”

White Englishmen also framed figures such as Baartman and Krao as transgressing gender norms—just as Green does when he records Bridget’s “moustache of puckered scabs” and describes her tall, strong mother, Aisling O’Leary, as a “slut” and a “grinning giantess,” noting that he “thought her a man” because of “the sheer bulk of her.”

While Green dehumanizes Bridget, Aisling, and other Irish characters, he anthropomorphizes the auks, even naming the colony’s denizens after monarchs and Shakespearean characters. Obsessed with their well-being, he calls the death of Canute, a bird named after a legendary 11th-century English king, a “sorrow” and a “scientific & human loss.”

After Canute’s death, Green dissects and begins to preserve the bird, but when he turns his back on the body, which is potentially infused with arsenic from the embalming soap, Bridget grabs the heart and begins to eat it. Panicking at the potential loss of his specimen—a “scientific tragedy”—Green persuades Bridget to spit out the heart. He even fears for Bridget’s safety, signaling a turning point in his feelings toward the child. The impact is limited, however: Once Bridget’s basic needs are met, Green continues to choose observing birds over fulfilling her desires.

A month after Bridget’s arrival, the pair leave Tor Mor again for Inishtrahull, where the situation has grown dire. Green observes the sick, the dead, and the dying on every road and in nearly every dwelling. He arrives at the bleak realization that the reason the Irish chose to kill and sell the auks—whose international market value rose in part because of the extinction it hastened—was a matter of survival. Killing the last great auks (those aforementioned “deliberate act[s] of extinction”) meant affording grain, which could be the difference between life and death by starvation.

An illustration of Krao Farini at Castan’s Panoptikum in Berlin in 1890.Bildagentur-online/Universal Images Group via Getty Images

In interviews, Walls has observed how the far right in Ireland describes immigrants with the same language that the English used to dehumanize Irish people in the 19th century. During a time of widespread xenophobia, Field Notes reminds readers that millions of Europeans—Irish, Italian, and more—were once refugees, their humanity dismissed by privileged elites.

How does a nation remember collective trauma? Was the Irish takeaway from the tragedy that saw nearly half the country perish or emigrate “never again”—or “never again to anyone”? As a European country and a former British colony, Ireland has a unique position. The country often stands in solidarity with other oppressed peoples, including with the South African anti-apartheid movement and, more recently, with Palestinians in Gaza. That sensibility is at odds with increasingly vocal and violent racist and anti-immigrant sentiment in the country today.

Between 1999 and 2001, Walls lived in Rwanda and witnessed famine in the aftermath of genocide. He saw firsthand human compassion amid immense suffering and economic inequality, but also the moral dilemmas facing outsiders who observed those struggles. Field Notes illustrates how such empathy and understanding grow through extended contact—and how intersecting catastrophes can lead individuals to abandon their humanity and preserve certain beings at the expense of others.

#4739. NHTSA’s Changing the Definition of a ‘Truck,’ Possibly Encouraging More Overseas Production

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Starting in 2030, the U.S. will have way more cars than trucks (technically). It’s the other way around now.

We’ve all known that the way the government classifies trucks and cars has been a bit of a moving target, so NHTSA is finally forcing trucks to actually do truck things.

The changes—which would reclassify today’s 70/30 truck-to-car ratio into a 30/70 situation—were made as part of the government’s loosening of MPG rules.

But the different rules for cars and trucks could mean that it will make sense to build a vehicle overseas and import it, given different rules for the domestic and imported fleets.

Some questions are difficult to answer. Is a hot dog a sandwich? Do crabs think fish can fly? Is a light truck a passenger car?

Whatever your responses are to the first two, the government made it clear that its legal answer to that last one will soon change. The announcement came in the Safer Affordable Fuel-Efficient (SAFE) Vehicles Rule III for Model Years 2022 to 2031 Passenger Cars and Light Trucks, which was published in the Federal Register at the end of September and goes into effect on November 30. While the change was made in the context of the Trump administration’s loosening of fuel-economy regulations, it might also force automakers to move some production outside the U.S., depending on the political winds.

The latest SAFE Rule updates fuel-economy standards for model years 2022 through 2031. Under the Biden administration, the standard had been set to a U.S. fleet average of around 50.4 mpg, but the new rules lower that to 34.9 mpg. Some of this change comes from new rules about what defines a car and what makes something a truck.

Since federal fuel-economy rules were established in the 1970s, the government has divided noncommercial road vehicles into two categories of light-duty vehicles, passenger cars (legally: passenger automobiles) and light trucks (non-passenger automobiles). How the government technically defines these vehicles has been a cat-and-mouse game between regulators and engineers. The SAFE Rule says that the back-and-forth led “manufacturers to reshape the market in unanticipated ways—such as by almost eliminating the production of station wagons (passenger cars that generally have more robust cargo capacity, adding mass and reducing fuel economy) in favor of vehicles like minivans and crossover utility vehicles (considered light trucks, and subject to less stringent standards).”

Today, the rule says, “The classification system that has long divided the fleet between passenger cars (intended to move passengers) and light trucks (intended to move cargo or operate off highway) no longer lives up to its anticipated use.” It states that while 68 percent of the light-duty fleet technically meets the regulatory definition of a light truck, the majority of them “cannot realistically operate off-highway and have little value moving cargo. Instead, most of these vehicles are designed and intended primarily to move passengers but have additional features solely to meet regulatory definitions —resulting in little added functionality, reduced fuel-economy performance, added cost, and a homogenous design lacking in creativity.”

Under the updated regulatory definitions, which will go into effect for model year 2030 vehicles, NHTSA said it expects trucks to be trucks and cars to, well, not be trucks. Light-duty non-passenger vehicles will “exhibit true non-passenger capabilities that display relevant off-highway vehicle attributes such as approach angle and running clearance or include design features that provide higher payload and towing abilities for transporting cargo,” the rule states. The agency expects the current fleet mix of 70 percent light trucks and 30 percent passenger cars would be classified as 70 percent cars and 30 percent trucks under the new rules.

A vehicle’s category matters from the production side, which is where unintended side effects start to crop up. As noted by Automotive News, a “truck” today that has lower fuel-economy numbers than a car won’t have to change to meet the new, lowered fuel-economy standards imposed by the Trump administration. But if a future administration revives Biden’s 50-mpg target or otherwise raises the standards, these vehicles will need a lot of engineering effort to be compliant because they’re now considered “cars.”

One alternative that automakers might choose in that situation is to import more vehicles through recontenting. That’s because while domestic passenger vehicles need to be produced to meet a CAFE standard minimum, imported passenger vehicles do not. A company’s overall average target remains the same, but since the rules allow automakers to move credits between imported and domestic fleets, there could be benefits to building outside the U.S.

Tom Stricker, senior policy adviser at Van Ness Feldman, told Automotive News that if an automaker were to offshore part of the part supply chain or work for a model in order to get it reclassified as an import vehicle, “the net environmental impact and fuel consumption impact has not changed one bit; all you’ve done is potentially, in this scenario, offshored certain production.”

#4765. Is Nathan Fielder the Peak of Modern Masculinity?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

You Can See Everything co-director Lance Oppenheim calls him a “very unconventionally hyper-masculine man.” We get what he’s talking about.

IF YOU’RE EVEN somewhat interested in movies, you’ve heard all about You Can See Everything (including from us at Men’s Health). In the week and change since the movie was officially released, the film—which combines unprecedented access to former Theranos CEO and convicted fraudster Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans with a meta-narrative threaded by co-directors Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim—has been discussed ad nauseam, sparking discourses about not only the facts realities of Holmes’s case and situation, but about the very nature of documentary films, language, and the truth.

And while the headlines of You Can See Everything are largely about the access to Holmes as a public figure and things she did or didn’t say, the reality is that it’s more of a Nathan Fielder movie than an Elizabeth Holmes movie. Fielder, who broke out with the docu-spoof series Nathan For You on Comedy Central before moving on to more surreal takes on similar subjects with HBO’s The Rehearsal and Paramount+ and Showtime’s The Curse, is at the center of the story all along, looking for something, anything, in Holmes and Evans as he talks to them. He’s searching less for the truth of the case—that’s already been investigated by the courts—and more for the truth of who Elizabeth is and why she’s behaved the way she has.

For a film of this structure to work, the person we’re along with for the ride needs to be someone we both understand and don’t mind spending that kind of time with. While You Can See Everything ultimately leaves us questioning everything we’ve just seen, Fielder, to his credit, is someone who makes the three-hour runtime an easy watch. He’s dry, yes—that’s his on-screen persona. But he’s also vulnerable and often humanistic. When he faces down Holmes, for example, in a scene where he shows her Abbott and Costello’s famous “Who’s On First?” routine for the first time, he reacts in a way most viewers could probably see themselves reacting.

Oppenheim did an interview with GQ, and he suggested that Fielder’s nature in the film makes him something of an emblem for modern men. “I feel like he's at his most masculine in You Can See Everything,” he said. “I'd always tell him, ‘You look so handsome in this.’ And I was just like, ‘I love being this close on your face. I'm so tight on your face right now and you look amazing.’ And he'd be like, ‘Dude, you could see my blackheads and my zits and stuff.’ I'd be like, ‘You look great. I actually think it makes you look better.’”

His quote alludes to the way Fielder carries himself in the movie: as something of an audience surrogate (at least during the first part of the film), and someone the audience can see themselves in. This marks a shift from Fielder’s previous on-screen personas, when he was a bit more of an on-screen joker or troll, egging his subjects a bit more into saying or doing something that will make them look silly. Here, he’s basically a blank slate for the audience to graft onto.

It’s interesting to read the quotes from Oppenheim, who last month also directed Primetime, a scripted A24 film starring Robert Pattinson. Pattinson, of course, is a traditionally handsome movie star, someone who’s played heartthrob roles like Edward Cullen in Twilight or literal superheroes like Bruce Wayne in The Batman. There’s, of course, a vision of masculinity in that. But when you look at someone like Fielder who is a normal guy—there’s even more of a mirror image being reflected to those of us watching in the theater or at home.

You Can See Everything understands the power in this unassuming parallel. Outside of Fielder, Evans is the only other male figure substantially present in the film, and he does partake in some more traditional expressions of masculine energy: He goes to the gym, he shoots guns in a range once he moves to Texas, and he even partakes in a shower scene of full-frontal male nudity as Fielder looks on. But it’s that parallel that Oppenheim implies is important—Fielder isn’t ever nervous or uncomfortable. “He's a very unconventionally hyper-masculine man,” Oppenheim said. “That's a compliment, I guess.”

The movie works because of the access we’re getting, and the way Fielder and Oppenheim set things up and draw us in and spit us out at the end. But it also works because Fielder so clearly knows who he is and understands who he is—blackheads be damned. And when the result is You Can See Everything, does anything else really matter?
