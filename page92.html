The father of the god game weighs in on AI use in the gaming industry

Recently, I travelled to Guildford, England, to interview developer Peter Molyneux, the legendary game developer behind Populous, Theme Park, Dungeon Keeper, Black & White and Fable, among others.

And, as part of that interview, which covered everything from Molyneux's brand-new god game, Masters of Albion, which has just got a huge game-changing update, through the moment he conceptualized Black & White's famous titan creatures, and onto the bombshell news that a Black & White remaster could be incoming, I also asked the father of the god game genre about AI use in the video game industry.

Molyneux is a gaming industry veteran, having been active in the gaming industry for over 40 years, so naturally his take on the rise of AI in game development was of interest. This is what he said when I asked if the gaming industry should embrace AI.

"Right. So, it's such a hot topic. Yes. It's such a hot potato. You've got to be very, very careful what you say, because, you know, I think the latest survey said that 12% of people would never buy any game under any circumstances if it had any AI at all.

"The first question we've got to ask is actually why does 12% of the people have this incredibly polarized view of AI? And the answer, I'm going to blame ChatGPT, I'm going to blame Gemini. They did this criminally ridiculous thing. They released those models that produced those repulsive images of 10-fingered hands and incredibly inappropriate materials which anybody could generate, and that locked into people's minds AI equals AI slop.

"So we've got consumers [who] are annoyed. We've got this technology which ain't going to stop. You know, if you think it's going to stop, it isn't. And then we've got Steam, which is almost everything in the industry at the moment. Steam, as a developer, you have to tick the box. Does this game contain AI? And if you tick it, you know instantly 12% [of gamers are] gone, and probably a further 18% would probably look at it and go, 'Oh, not for me.' So we are pretending. This is the truth, Rob. We as the industry are pretending we're not using AI, but the reality is, it's almost impossible to code now without using AI. Almost impossible. I mean, the efforts you have to go to extract AI from line completion stuff and from generative stuff is very challenging. So everyone, every title that's come out in the last year has been using that, but we're still able to not tick the AI box. AI is going to be, when it's sorted out and not producing AI slop, it is the most… This is very controversial. It is the most, the biggest advance in creativity for smaller studios, for medium studios and bigger studios. The biggest step forward in creativity."

At this point, I asked Molyneux how AI was such a big step forward for studios, as, at least from my point of view, that has often been something not clarified well over the past few years of AI discourse. What are the everyday game development examples of how AI is being such a transformative tool for game studios of all sizes? This is what Molyneux said.

"I'll talk in general terms. I won't talk about ourselves. Let's say you and I thought about a game, you know, let's set it in an old Carpathian mountain village. And it starts in a graveyard, and there's this young girl wandering around the graveyard looking lost. Now, traditionally, what you would do is you'd have someone… like there was a genius concept artist at Lionhead called Mike McArthur, and I'd sit down with Mike McArthur [and] I'd say, yeah, there's this girl, and she's wandering. How old is the girl? Mike might say. I don't know, maybe she's about 12. Okay, and is it an old church, a new church? You know, we'd have this conversation.

"[So] he would go away. Two weeks later, he would come back. Then, do you mean this girl? This child? Oh no, I don't really like the girl. She looks too pretty. She should be, you know, starving, and he goes away for another week, comes back. Yeah. [Now] you can do all that concepting work in five minutes [with AI].

"If you take that same premise. Imagine that you were a zombie; you were just emerging from there. That's the character you play. Okay, yes. You play an undead. And you move in an undead form. To do that prototype, you know, to get you to come out of the ground, to get you to locomote, to get you to navigate around the world effectively would take months and months and months of work. But now you can go to AI and say, right, I want to have a character. I want him to be buried. I want him to be a fully rigged character. Produce the code. To bring him out to the ground and move him, the code will be produced. This is all done in a day, not months.

"You can tweak the code. You can change the code with this conversation. Now, all of that is the preparation work that you're doing for the [development] team to engage with [on] the actual title. I don't think AI is powerful enough yet to trust it to produce the assets for the final quality of the game. But Rob, it will be. Yeah, it will be in less time than we can possibly imagine. Within a year, I predict that there will be AI so intimately bound to middleware like Unity and Unreal that it will be very hard for us to extract it. So at the moment we've got this ridiculous state where other industries, like the advertising industry, are embracing AI; it's the savior. Whereas the games industry is trying to push it away."

What struck a chord with me during this interview was Molyneux's belief that, within a year, AI will be so fundamentally built into key game development middleware like Unreal Engine and Unity that it will be almost impossible to not use AI in the creation of a new game. That just rang so true to me as soon as he had said it. It felt inevitable. Very few studios have their own proprietary tools/engines, and even if they did, if AI can indeed make game development easier, why wouldn't they adopt it into their own tech? I'm neither massively pro AI nor anti-AI in game development; it's clearly how the tech is used, and to what extent, that is key, but I'm not sure ignoring it entirely solves anything.

However, I think there is a clear difference between using AI-powered development tools and middleware to help create quality games powered by real human passion and performance, and churning out a non-stop slurry of AI slop loaded with AI assets and poorly implemented AI-coded systems in order to make a quick buck. We're clearly living through very turbulent times in the gaming industry right now, and the rise of AI in game development is just one facet of that, but it feels like there's plenty of change yet to come, and that will likely be both for the better and worse.

General Motors may have introduced several new engines for its upcoming trucks, including a new V8 and a massive diesel mill for heavy-duty models, but the American automaker appears to have more ideas in the works. A recently published patent points to another approach to improving the way internal-combustion engines operate.

First spotted by CarMoses, the patent was filed last year and published last week. Titled “Extended Expansion Engine With Variable Compression Ratio for Longitudinal Mounting Applications,” it describes a mechanical arrangement designed to change piston movement and vary the engine’s compression ratio.

In a conventional engine, pistons move up and down inside cylinders, with connecting rods transferring that motion to the crankshaft. GM’s proposed design, which is an inline-six according to the sketches, adds a more elaborate arrangement of gears, links, and control components to change the relationship between these moving parts.

With this setup, the engine could play with how much it squeezes the air-fuel mix before lighting it up. Compression ratio is a big deal – it affects everything from efficiency to power, and even how the engine handles a heavy load.

Then there’s the extended-expansion trick. After the bang pushes the piston down, those hot gases still have some juice left. Letting them expand a bit more means you can squeeze out extra work before they head out the tailpipe.

On paper, this could mean better fuel economy – at least when the stars align. Of course, tossing in more moving parts means more complexity, higher costs, and extra friction. GM will have to prove the gains are worth the headaches.

The patent specifically addresses longitudinal engine mounting, a layout commonly used in rear-wheel-drive vehicles and many trucks and SUVs. That makes the concept relevant to some of GM’s existing vehicle architectures, although the company has not identified a particular model for the technology.

If developed further, the design could offer another way to improve gasoline-engine efficiency without changing the basic fuel source. Still, GM has not provided production plans, performance figures, or fuel-economy estimates for the proposed arrangement.

Like most car patents, this is just a peek at what could be, not a guarantee you’ll see it at your local dealer. Most of these ideas never make it past the drawing board.

Former Morgan Stanley and Weiss executive Jordi Visser says AI agents are boosting demand for crypto as he expands his holdings beyond Bitcoin, Ether and Solana.

Cryptocurrencies could deliver a better payoff from the artificial intelligence boom than AI stocks themselves, according to Visser Labs founder Jordi Visser.

Explosive gains in AI stocks are becoming harder to repeat, Visser tells Cointelegraph’s Trade Secrets. At the same time, AI agents are creating demand for crypto payments and tokenized assets.

“Without AI, I don’t think crypto would be going through this bull market,” he says.

Hyperscaler capital spending and free cash flow (left), and forward valuations compared with the S&P 500 (right). Source: JPMorgan Asset Management

As a result of his thesis, he has expanded his own holdings beyond Bitcoin, Ether and Solana into a broader basket of tokens.

Visser’s three-decade career includes stints as managing director at Morgan Stanley and chief investment officer at Weiss Multi-Strategy Advisers. He now heads 22V’s AI Macro Nexus Research.

The Wall Street veteran’s broader bet is that AI agents will allow people to instruct software to execute trades and other financial transactions on their behalf.

“AI agents are effectively the same as the App Store or the smartphone for crypto,” he says.

In that scenario, users would direct their agents while the software handles execution. The growing use of those agents will drive more activity in tokenized assets and crypto payments, extending the investment opportunity beyond the companies supplying computing power.

But Visser’s crypto outlook still carries exposure to the AI trade he has grown more cautious about. He identifies a severe AI downturn that drags down equity markets as one threat to his bullish case.

“I don’t think that would end the bull market, but I definitely think it would retest the lows that we just saw,” he says.

Visser says he began reducing his exposure to memory chipmaker Micron in mid-May after concluding that hedge funds and retail investors had crowded into the AI trade.

“One of the reasons that Bitcoin and Ethereum were having such a hard time is because the focus was completely on AI,” he says.

By mid-May, Bitcoin was down 10.85% for the year and Ether about 25.86%. Source: TradingView

AI offered investors potentially greener pastures backed by rising business revenue. Microsoft’s quarterly report on April 29 claimed that its AI business was generating revenue at an annualized rate of $37 billion, more than double a year earlier. Meanwhile, Micron reported in March that its fiscal second-quarter revenue had nearly tripled to $23.86 billion.

But AI investors now face a less forgiving market, according to Visser. The 100-stock AI portfolio he tracks is up about 40% this year, but has become more volatile than Bitcoin on a rolling 126-day measure.

For example, Oracle spent $55.7 billion on capital expenditures in fiscal 2026 against $32 billion in operating cash flow, and raised $43 billion in debt financing.

“I didn’t say AI was over. AI fireworks show is over,” Visser says.

He still sees several years of growth ahead in computing demand from AI agents, but says investors should temper their expectations for stock returns.

Until August, Visser’s crypto holdings consisted solely of Bitcoin, Ether and Solana. While Bitcoin remains his largest position, he has since started to acquire a broader range of tokens.

Dogecoin is a clear signal he watches for returning retail enthusiasm, which he considers essential to sustaining a broader rally.

“They’re the energy of the space and they’re the ones that are going to fuel this as other people get involved,” he says.

To track that broader market, Visser maintains an equally weighted index of 42 tokens and four publicly traded stocks. He says it has outperformed Bitcoin since June and plans to hold all its constituents.

Privacy is a theme he has already invested in.

“I’m not betting on one company, but I believe in privacy,” Visser says.

#166. UK Politician Jess Asato Got Deepfaked. Now She’s Taking xAI to Court

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Few people understand the current deepfake epidemic better than Jess Asato. The British Labour MP has spent years campaigning against the use of technology to abuse women and girls. Then, after calling out Elon Musk during the notorious Grok Deepfake Scandal of 2025, Asato found herself caught in the very crisis she had been trying to prevent.

Disturbing and degrading deepfake images of the MP circulated widely. One appeared to depict a sexual assault. When Asato reported them to X, the platform repeatedly dismissed her complaints. So she decided to take the fight somewhere else: the courts. She is now pursuing legal action against Musk’s xAI, arguing that it unlawfully used her image without her consent. The case could help establish a precedent for holding AI companies accountable when their products cause harm.

For Asato, this is personal, but it is hardly new territory. Before entering Parliament, she spent years working with charities supporting survivors of domestic abuse and vulnerable children. That experience has shaped her political career, informing her push for stronger protections for women and children online. Now, she is confronting more troubling questions: What happens when the technology enabling abuse becomes ubiquitous, the platforms hosting it refuse to intervene, and the people targeted are left to fight back on their own?

At stake for Asato is more than the misuse of a single image. It is whether consent still means anything when AI can manufacture almost anything, and whether the companies building these tools can be made to answer for what they do. Asato is betting that the law can catch up with the technology. And she is prepared to take on Musk to find out. (xAI did not immediately respond to WIRED’s request for comment.)

GSK is seeing its return to oncology start to pay off, STAT says. In the past week, a cancer drug picked up an approval for another tumor type, the company unveiled the first data from a global study of a promising next-generation chemotherapy, and a medicine the firm licensed got the limelight in a presidential session at a major oncology conference. GSK is also awaiting another expected approval in the coming weeks for a new lung cancer drug. That said, GSK is still a small player among big drugmakers. Its oncology sales last year were about $2.67 billion, a 43% jump from the year prior, but less than one-tenth of the sales of Merck’s Keytruda alone.

A House lawmaker is investigating whether the three largest pharmacy benefit managers targeted independent pharmacies with audits in retaliation for supporting a bill that would require the companies to adjust payments to the retailers, STAT tells us. James Comer (R-Ky.), who heads the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, late last week sent letters to CVS Caremark, OptumRx, and Express Scripts, claiming that independent pharmacies “experienced an unusual surge in burdensome audits” after many openly backed the legislation he introduced last year.
