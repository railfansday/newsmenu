#4442. 2027 Audi Q9 and SQ9: We Drive Audi’s Big-League SUVs

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Audi breaks into the full-size luxury-SUV club with the Q9 and SQ9.

It was only a matter of time until Audi put on its big-boy pants and fielded a legitimate full-size luxury SUV. It’s been going toe to toe with Mercedes-Benz and BMW in nearly every other segment for decades, and it has high hopes that its new 2027 Q9 and SQ9 SUVs will take a bite out of the full-size class, a profit-rich environment.

Unsurprisingly, this series is played largely in North America, where Audi says it expects 65 percent of the Q9s to find a home. In addition to the Mercedes-Benz GLS and BMW X7, the Q9 duo will, to a lesser extent, line up against the Cadillac Escalade and Lincoln Navigator. While it may seem a stretch to include those body-on-frame stalwarts, consider that the Q9's 123.5-inch wheelbase is 2.6 inches longer than the Caddy's, although its 208.9- to 209.0-inch overall length is 2.9 to 3.0 inches shorter. But its overall length outstretches both the BMW X7 and Mercedes GLS by several inches. (A full comparison of this German trio can be found here.)

During our first drive in Saint George, Utah, Audi reps cheerfully extolled the Q9’s size and expressed their hopes that it will resonate with U.S. consumers. Still, the designers put at least some energy into humanizing its considerable profile, an effort somewhat contradicted by the massive grille that’s illuminated and aggressively patterned.

The self-professed space race continues inside, where buyers find a choice of a standard second row-bench or captain’s chairs, the largest sunroof in an Audi vehicle to date (16 square feet, with optional illumination!), and “oversized” cupholders—relative to what, we’re not sure.

While we didn’t have a chance to sample the standard second-row bench seat, we can confirm the middle captain’s chair setup is bound for car-service glory. The power-adjustable seats offer acres of headroom and legroom, a nice blend of padding and support, and even feature removable inner armrests to facilitate easier access to the third row. As a $450 option in a vehicle that starts at just under $90K, we figure it’s a box most buyers will check. Should you opt for the bench seat, however, know that it folds in a 35/30/35 fashion to help manage cargo and passengers.

The third-row seating will not share in the second row’s glory. Though Audi is quick to point out there’s more headroom back there than in the Benz or BMW, the seat base and backrest are rather flat. It will, however, make fitting a child car seat with the standard LATCH points a snap, once you get past the awkward forward-folding second-row seats. But foot- and legroom for fully formed humans is compromised at best and nearly eliminated when the A-listers in the second row get greedy and start scooting their power-adjustable chairs back.

Even the biggest SUV still needs a driver, and we’re happy to report life in the front compartment is carefree and comfortable. The seats offer an ideal blend of padding and support that left us fresh after a three-hour stint behind the wheel of the Q9. The sportier SQ9’s front seats are a bit more aggressive in terms of bolstering, but they don’t abandon comfort either. We’d gladly grab the SQ9 for a nine-hour Detroit-to-NYC run, confident that we’d exit the vehicle fresh enough for a dinner engagement. Complaints will be few from the front passengers.

The Q9's 429-hp twin-turbo 2.9-liter V-6 and the SQ9’s 591-hp twin-turbo 4.0-liter V-8, both in combo with an eight-speed automatic transmission, are also found in the redesigned (if somewhat smaller three-row) 2027 Q7/SQ7. Thankfully, both are up to the task of motivating this massive SUV at a rapid pace.

The Q9 V-6’s 429 ponies may be 162 shy of the SQ9’s V-8 output, you’d never be wont for more grunt if you didn’t know that beefy V-8 was out there. Audi claims a 4.9-second run to 60 mph for the V-6, but we’ll bet lunch that our test drivers will shave a tenth or two from that number.

As for the SQ9’s palpable performance improvement on the Q9, well, let’s just say Maker’s Mark is a fine drink if you’ve never had a sip of Weller 12. Audi is upfront about this being a large vehicle aimed at North America, and having a meaty V-8 under the hood is key to that mission. The SQ9’s 60-mph estimate shrinks to 3.8 seconds flat, and again, we think that’s conservative. Mat the accelerator, and the SQ9 moves like it has something to prove.

Visually, the SQ9 signifies its performance focus with a honeycomb grille, quad exhaust pipes, wider rear wheel arches, and larger brake rotors sized 16.5 inches in front and 15.0 inches in the rear. The Q9’s rotors span 15.7 and 13.8 inches, respectively.

But the SQ9’s biggest secret lies beneath the floor: While the Q9 uses the newest version of Audi’s long-running Quattro all-wheel-drive system with an updated locking center differential, the SQ9 gets an electronically controlled locking rear differential and all-wheel-drive clutch. Corporate siblings Porsche and Lamborghini have done this before, but it’s a first for Audi, and the benefits of are multifold.

First, the system permits varying levels of lock and unlock based on driving parameters and drive mode selection, and it works with the brake-based torque vectoring to provide higher levels of slip angle, allowing drivers to enjoy a bit more rotation regardless of the road surface. Second, Audi says it allows for the fitment of staggered front and rear tire sizes, a situation that could cause binding with the traditional system. Essentially, it does all the desired Quattro things but allows a wider range of operating variables and favors a rear-biased power balance. The more aggressive redirection on throttle-down corner exits is palpable.

Rear-wheel steering is standard on the SQ9 and available on the Q9, and we wouldn’t leave the dealership without it. It varies the amount of rear-wheel angle up to five degrees, significantly reducing the turning radius. At higher speeds, the rears turn in phase with the fronts, but it’s not obvious to the passengers; it’s just another refinement that contributes to the overall tranquility of highway travel. Like satellite radio and backup cameras, rear-wheel steering falls into the “once you have it . . .” category of modern automotive features for large vehicles.

The ride in both Q9s is remarkably quiet and smooth, the steering precise but overly light in our opinion—Audi engineers tell us that is intentional to help make a big, heavy vehicle feel lighter and smaller. Braking hard reveals a firm pedal, but the conversation is a bit light on details. The same can be said for the steering, although the progressive setup avoids harsh changes in response; the center valley feels light to the touch, but response moving off-center is linear, if synthetic. Still, a holistic, stout, and solid vibe permeates the entire driving experience in both vehicles.

Audi equips the Q9 and SQ9 with adaptive cruise control with hands-free driving, a proprietary system it classifies as Level 2+. This is the first Audi model to get this version; it allows hands-free driving up to 85 mph and can automatically perform lane changes. Tested on Utah’s Interstate 15, it performed as billed but behaved a bit erratically, switching off and back on randomly in areas that otherwise appeared well marked and free of impending traffic. We’re eager to sample it again as soon as we get a Q9 in our office for testing. As it exists now, the Super Cruise team at GM can rest easy.

A 16-speaker Bang & Olufsen audio system is standard, but the cars we drove had the available 22-speaker version incorporating a sound actuator—a big-money word for “bass shaker”—in each of the front seats, in addition to a genuine subwoofer. The system includes the Fraunhofer Symphoria processing for what Audi calls 4D sound reproduction. (Fraunhofer gave us the MP3 audio compression codec back in the digital music dark ages, so it’s legit.) The system sounds balanced and nuanced, and kudos for resisting the urge to crank up the sound actuators to high-school parking-lot levels. Conspicuously absent is Dolby Atmos sound processing; Audi tells us it will arrive “later,” a firmware issue rumored to be responsible for the delay.

The 2027 Q9 and SQ9 also serve as the host body for Audi’s long-gestating adaptive Digital Matrix LED headlights, a system that strives to put lots of forward illumination where you want it and keep it from where you don’t—primarily, the eyes of oncoming drivers and the rearview mirrors of vehicles in front of you.

Audi has been fussing with the technology for over a decade in Europe, but the United States Department of Transportation only recently finally revised its standards to allow such technology. (Read our in-depth tech analysis here.)

We tried out the system in the dark, late-night environs of Utah’s Snow Canyon State Park. While the sheer volume and clarity of the illumination on a deserted road is impressive, the system really proves its worth when an oncoming driver enters your field of view. Vehicle after vehicle, the headlights precisely and fluidly created a dark shadow of sorts around the approaching vehicle’s windshield while maintaining full illumination outside of the selected area. It performed the same trick when following a vehicle, accurately positioning a dark rectangle around the lead vehicle’s rear window and exterior rearview mirrors. An additional flourish: The Q9s can also project a turn signal on the pavement on the side of the vehicle.

The Q9 carries a base price of $89,095, with optional upgrades, including a towing package that allow it tug up to 7700 pounds, pushing that figure well beyond $100,000. The SQ9, meanwhile, opens at $119,395 and climbs from there.

As a first effort into the large-luxury-SUV segment, the Q9 and SQ9 hit all the right marks, combining healthy power outputs; a smooth, quiet ride; and some worthy gee-whiz technology. We just wonder if that’s enough to get noticed in this crowded segment.

#5893. Travel’s New Creator Economy: Why Brands Need Smarter Systems

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Travel brands are pouring more money into creators, but spend alone won’t build an edge. The advantage will come from learning faster, measuring better, and turning what works into a repeatable system.

This sponsored content was created in collaboration with a Skift partner.

Creators and social media now influence over $7 trillion in global travel spending, according to figures shared by Skift Head of Research Seth Borko at the inaugural Skift Creator Summit, which took place in New York City on September 22, 2026.

That level of influence shows how far creator marketing has moved from the margins of the travel industry, but the challenge facing brands now is turning that influence into a repeatable business function.

Sanjay Bhatia, director and head of industry, travel, auto & experiences at Meta, and Laura Troy, senior director, creative and content, U.S. and Canada at Marriott International, joined Skift Studio Content Director Alison McCarthy on stage to discuss how travel brands can move beyond one-off creator campaigns and build a more scalable, measurable system around creator marketing.

Bhatia identified three characteristics that distinguish established creator programs from more fragmented approaches: organizational ownership, a defined budget, and creative planning that begins alongside the media strategy.

“Creator strategy has to be a priority internally, with clear accountability,” he said. “There needs to be someone on the organization chart who owns the program and is responsible for it.”

According to Troy, there was “no rule book” and “no playbook” for social media when she joined Marriott’s luxury division in 2007. That early experience shaped a test-and-learn approach, but Troy said experimentation still needs a clear definition of success.

“You could have a beautiful piece of creator content, but if you weren’t aligned on the goals and what success looked like, it could fall flat,” she said.

Paid media has consequently become central to Marriott’s approach to creator content. Troy said creators need to understand not only the organic story they’re being asked to tell but also how the asset will function once media investment sits behind it.

That integration also changes the internal structure of creator marketing. Work that may once have primarily involved social or PR now requires creative, brand, media, and performance teams to agree on objectives and measurement.

Bhatia described organizational constraints as one of the biggest barriers he sees across brands. A shared definition of success can help bring those groups together. He said creator marketing was growing at four times the rate of the rest of the media. Meta’s presentation also cited forecasts putting paid creator amplification at $13 billion, up 48% year over year, while 92% of marketers said creator content outperforms their own.

Bhatia broke the creator process into four stages: discover, connect, scale, and optimize.

Discovery increasingly means looking beyond follower counts to identify creators who match a particular audience or traveler intent. For example, Meta’s Creator Marketing Hub makes more than seven million creators across Instagram and Facebook discoverable, with recommendations informed by Meta’s machine learning and first-party performance data.

The second step is creating the permissions and rights structure that allows brands to use creator content beyond an isolated organic post. Scale then becomes a matter of identifying content that’s already resonating and backing it with media.

#5391. Ford’s new sub-$30K electric truck can power your home for a lot less

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ford wants to make powering your home with an EV a lot cheaper. Its upcoming sub-$30,000 Fathom electric truck will feature a new bidirectional charging system that ditches the need for an expensive external inverter.

Arriving in 2027, the Fathom will be the first EV from a US automaker priced below $30,000 that can deliver more than 10 kW of AC power to a home without a separate wall-mounted inverter.

And Ford says it’s cutting the cost of the other equipment needed for home backup power, too.

EV batteries store electricity as direct current (DC), but our homes use alternating current (AC). To send power from an EV battery into a house, traditionally, that meant installing a pricey external inverter.

Ford has come up with a different approach for its new Universal EV Platform, which will underpin the Fathom.

After acquiring startup Auto Motive Power in 2023, Ford redesigned its bidirectional charging system from the ground up. Instead of relying on a separate wall-mounted inverter, it built one directly into the truck’s E-box, one of the vehicle’s five main modules.

Fewer parts mean lower costs, and Ford says the savings will extend to the home charging equipment, too.

Fathom owners will have two ways to use the truck’s battery to power their homes.

Home Power Management will let owners charge their truck when electricity rates are low, then use that stored power at home when rates are higher. That could mean real savings for households on time-of-use electricity plans. They’ll need Ford’s new Home Power Station, which the company expects to cost about half as much as its first-generation bidirectional charger.

Home Backup Power will let the truck keep the lights on during an outage. For that, owners will need both the Home Power Station and Ford’s Home Backup Panel. Ford says the backup hardware should cost at least 60% less than its first-generation equipment.

Ford hasn’t released prices for either piece of hardware, so we don’t yet know what a complete home installation will cost.

But pairing a sub-$30,000 electric truck with substantially cheaper home power equipment could make bidirectional charging a lot more appealing.

Making the hardware cheaper is one thing. Making sure utilities can handle thousands of EVs that send electricity back into homes and potentially the grid is another.

Ford formed a working group in July with American Electric Power, BC Hydro, Consumers Energy, Exelon, and Pacific Gas and Electric. They’re working on making it easier for customers to connect bidirectional EVs before the Fathom arrives next year.

For example, someone using their truck solely for home backup power during an outage shouldn’t have to go through the same approval process as someone sending electricity to the grid.

The group is also looking at how to update utility procedures to accommodate newer bidirectional charging standards and make it easier for EV owners to participate in virtual power plants (VPPs). In other words, the batteries in parked EVs could become a useful source of electricity when the grid needs it most.

Ford says the Fathom’s system is designed to meet applicable UL 1741 requirements and IEEE 1547 interconnection standards. It also uses SAE J3068 communications technology, which Ford says helps reduce charger costs.

The idea is to get the technical requirements and utility processes sorted out before affordable bidirectional EVs start arriving in much larger numbers.

Affordable EVs already offer some form of bidirectional power. Two of the cheapest electric cars in the US, the Chevy Bolt and Nissan Leaf, are both available for under $30,000.

The Bolt supports vehicle-to-home (V2H) power through GM Energy equipment, while the Leaf offers vehicle-to-load (V2L) capability through an external device plugged into its charging port.

"Friends" fan or not, "The One About Matthew Perry" is one worth watching — and it's on Netflix now.

Hollywood has always been a hall of mirrors. What began as an industry of light and illusion had, by the 1940s, become a machinery of control—an empire built on fantasy, running on fear. Its dreammakers no longer needed scripts or cameras to keep order. They had Louella Parsons and Hedda Hopper.

By then, both women had transcended the ordinary limits of journalism. They were not simply reporters; they were gatekeepers of belief, the quiet regulators of a nation’s imagination. To cross them was to risk vanishing—not merely from the screen, but from the collective story America told about itself.

Hollywood insiders referred to them—half jokingly, half in dread—as “the High Priestesses.” Studio secretaries clipped their columns before the morning dailies reached the lot, marking any mention of the studio’s stars with red pencil. Publicists phoned assistants in panic if a name appeared in the wrong context. “They could wreck you before lunch,” remembered producer Pandro Berman. “And by dinner, they’d make you again—if it suited them.”

It was easy, at first, to mistake their power for personality. Louella, with her sanctimonious charm and the backing of William Randolph Hearst, ruled the studios from her desk at the Los Angeles Examiner. Her typewriter was a throne. Her column ran like clockwork through America’s breakfast tables, a sermon of what was right and who had sinned. Hedda, the actress-turned-crusader, cultivated a different tone—brassy, righteous, vengeful. Her voice carried across radio waves like a call to arms. She didn’t just write gossip; she announced it, her cadence turning every name into an accusation or an absolution.

Both women understood theater. Louella played the role of moral mother, her column written in the soothing cadences of propriety—rarely venomous on the surface, yet devastating in its implications. Hedda was the showwoman. “When she walked into Ciro’s,” recalled gossip photographer Frank Worth, “it was like someone had switched on the lights.” Even those who loathed her found themselves glancing up, afraid she might be looking at them.

Louella and Hedda were two sides of the same coin, minted in a culture that worshipped celebrity and feared moral chaos. Each understood what the studios feared most: scandal, exposure, the sudden rupture of the illusion. And they learned to trade in that fear, turning rumor into regulation.

Louella had built her empire first. By the early 1930s, her column appeared in hundreds of newspapers through the Hearst syndicate, reaching an estimated twenty million readers daily. Hollywood Hotel, her weekly radio show, brought the glamour of opening nights directly into American living rooms. Its mix of studio orchestras, star appearances, and previews of upcoming films created a powerful cycle of cross-promotion: her print column drove listeners to the broadcast, and the broadcast reinforced her authority in print.

She was feared as much as courted. Stars who crossed her found themselves frozen out. Studio executives who annoyed her might find their films receiving tepid coverage. A single line hinting that an actor appeared “unhappy with recent assignments” could trigger studio meetings and contract reviews. Louella’s prose was brief, polite, and devastatingly coded; her readers learned to interpret her tone as carefully as her text. The fear she inspired was not only of what she printed but of what she might choose not to.

The competition she could not have predicted came in the form of a woman she had once treated as a harmless curiosity. By 1935, Hedda Hopper’s film career was essentially over. She was fifty, though she claimed forty-five, watching her savings evaporate and terrified about her future. When a studio executive suggested she could “transition to extra work,” Hedda turned the humiliation into fuel. If she couldn’t star in movies, she would make herself the story. The weapon she would wield was not beauty or youth, but the pen.

When a friend suggested she try writing a gossip column, Hedda was insulted. Writing gossip seemed like a public acknowledgment that she couldn’t make it as a performer. But desperation has a way of making humiliation seem acceptable. She borrowed a typewriter, set up at her kitchen table, and began by writing what she knew best: the petty rivalries of actors who had underestimated her. “I never lied,” she said later, “but I didn’t always tell the truth either.” The early columns were messy but alive, full of energy and vengeance.

Then Hedda got angry. She stopped trying to write like a journalist and started writing like herself— sharp, critical, unsparing, willing to say things other columnists wouldn’t dare print. Twenty years as a working actress had also given her something valuable: relationships with the people Louella ignored. Character actors, crew members, hairdressers, chauffeurs, and wardrobe girls had no loyalty to Louella, and many were happy to talk to Hedda, who had once been one of them.

The Los Angeles Times noticed. Hearst’s primary competitor in Southern California saw Hedda as a weapon against his dominance through Louella. In 1938, the paper offered Hedda a contract with better pay, more resources, and a much larger platform. She would compete directly with the woman who had been Hollywood’s undisputed gossip queen for fifteen years.

Most people thought she would be crushed. Instead, Hedda transformed herself for the opportunity. She began wearing the enormous hats that became her trademark—creations decorated with feathers, flowers, fruit, and even stuffed animals. The hats ensured that everyone at a Hollywood event knew when Hedda Hopper had arrived. She also developed a confrontational style that contrasted with Louella’s cozy approach. Where Louella praised, Hedda criticized. Where Louella protected relationships, Hedda burned bridges. Where Louella sought to be loved, Hedda was content to be feared.

In the beginning, the difference between them seemed simple enough. Louella had the empire; Hedda had the instinct. Louella’s authority came from her link to Hearst—a patriarchal structure she both served and mastered. Hedda’s came from sheer velocity. She moved fast, wrote fast, spoke fast. She had learned that outrage was more contagious than affection.

At first, the two women coexisted uneasily, even politely. Louella sent Hedda a congratulatory telegram when her first radio broadcast went national. Hedda responded with a bouquet of orchids and a note that read, “To the Queen of the Kingdom I am only visiting.” But the civility evaporated as soon as Hedda’s column began pulling readers away from the Examiner. In 1940, when Hedda’s column syndication reached more than six million households, Louella persuaded Hearst’s editors to bar her rival from every Hearst-owned paper.

As the 1940s unfolded, their feud became public theater. Columns answered columns. Louella hinted that Hedda was “overly dramatic.” Hedda fired back, calling Louella “a relic of studio servitude.” Studios courted both women, sometimes feeding them different versions of the same story to see who would publish first. Readers began to choose sides.

In 1945, Photoplay published “Battle of the Typewriters,” complete with caricatures of the two women as dueling queens—Louella seated on a gilt throne, Hedda brandishing her hatpins like weapons. The story outsold every issue that year except the one featuring Clark Gable’s return from the war. The public couldn’t get enough. For a nation recovering from global conflict, the spectacle of two powerful women waging a cold war of words was irresistible.

Their methods echo through the media that followed. The tabloid boom of the 1980s, TMZ, Instagram, celebrity influencers, blind-item accounts, and the daily social-media outrage cycle all trace their lineage to the template Louella and Hedda built. They understood that access could be traded, intimacy manufactured, morality weaponized, and a public image constructed or destroyed through repetition. The machine they built continues to hum behind every red carpet and every scandal recycled for entertainment. What they truly enshrined was attention—and America never stopped worshiping it.

The women themselves could not stop Hollywood from moving on. As the studio system declined and stars emerged, the two gossip queens became relics of the world they had once governed. When the Los Angeles Examiner folded in 1962, Louella suffered a professional blow. Already showing signs of physical deterioration, she nevertheless continued going out, bejeweled and bewildered, like a dowager empress whose country had overthrown her rule. She finally retired in 1965, beset by further medical problems.

Hedda had always advised leaving a party before the glow faded. On February 1, 1966, she died in Los Angeles of double pneumonia at the age of eighty. Her cryptic parting words were entirely in character: “They should know what I haven’t written!”

Louella’s daughter, Harriet, visited her mother at the Santa Monica nursing home to deliver the news that Hedda Hopper was dead. Louella sat in profound silence, then appeared confused. She would live for another six years, largely withdrawn from the world she had once ruled, before dying of arteriosclerosis on December 9, 1972, at the age of ninety-one. But on hearing that her great rival had finally departed, Louella gathered herself long enough to offer one last, unmistakable verdict: “GOOD!”

CoreWeave, a publicly traded neocloud that rents out computing power for AI, has seen its stock price fall to a 12-month low, and Elon Musk is flexing $40 billion in debt for buying computing capacity and predicting that “200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation.” It’s not exactly the ideal week to announce a Series A for a neocloud.

General Compute co-founder and CEO Finn Puklowski is unfazed. He’s bullish on CoreWeave’s chances, and thinks Musk is just talking his book to drive down the prices of the GPUs he wants to buy. Fears of a chip-led collapse are “massively overstated. I have people calling me up every single week asking me to do TPUs, GPUs, any chip, to get them more compute.”

Just as important, he says, Anthropic’s bullish S-1 is “settling fears for today.”

General Compute has found a niche building its cloud out of specialized inference chips built by Nvidia rivals. The company struck a $400 million deal to purchase new chips from SambaNova — the first debt financing of inference chips — and now has added Positron and Cerebras to its portfolio of chip suppliers.

A deal with a large customer worth $100 million in annual recurring revenue has paved the way for the company to raise $100 million in Series A funding led by Blackbird Ventures and Abstract Ventures. Valued at $60 million after a May seed round, General Compute is now worth $800 million post-money to its investors.

The startup’s strategy is to zig where others zag. It focused on chips built for inference while everyone else chased GPUs. And instead of taking on a complex effort to build huge data centers, it uses smaller power sources, rents space in other companies’ data centers, and converts former bitcoin-mining sites into places that run AI models.

That approach means General Compute isn’t going to catapult into the top ranks of neoclouds or compete with hyperscalers any time soon. But it’s also not subject to the constraints that come with building data centers from scratch. When General Compute has chips, it will have someplace to plug them in. Puklowski expects the company to deploy 75 megawatts of computing capacity in 2027, and he suspects he will have access to 200 megawatts of power — “more than we can probably fund and get hardware for.”

Still, the plan has a limit. Puklowski’s financing work isn’t done — “capital is going to be a full-time job for me, both on debt and equity,” he told TechCrunch.

The startup has found key customers in cloud companies that specialize in offering developers high-speed inference; General Compute provides the bare metal, or raw servers with no software layered on top, for them to bring their offerings to market. James Palmer, a principal at Blackbird who co-led the round, said his firm’s confidence in General Compute comes in part from an investment in Baseten, an inference cloud company.

“We’ve been proximate to their journey over the last 10 months and observing the wild growth story that [Baseten] has gone on, and the demand for open weight inference, and for companies owning their own intelligence,” Palmer told TechCrunch. When it comes to General Compute, he said, “Cerebras, Positron, SambaNova, all need distribution partners who can take them to scale, who can navigate the complexity of the physical world.”

Executives at all three chipmakers sang General Compute’s praises to TechCrunch, each noting the importance of the company’s focus on making the economics of AI cloud compute work. “Everyone in this market is selling speed,” Rodrigo Liang, SambaNova’s CEO and co-founder, said. “The real question is whether you can make money doing it.”

#97. Anduril, Qualcomm launch BlueChip, a new kind of US-made laptop

Anduril founder Palmer Luckey is vowing to bring computer manufacturing back to the U.S. And by that, he doesn’t just mean assembly, but the production of every individual component, some of which are exclusively made in China today.

Anduril is partnering with Qualcomm to achieve that mission with a new computer called BlueChip, the companies announced Tuesday. BlueChip will be available for preorder in 2027.

While technical details are scant — Anduril hasn’t even finished contracting with all its component suppliers yet, according to Luckey — this first product will be a hardened laptop. It will be designed to withstand the most grueling physical environments and meet the security-conscious needs of the military, government, first responders, and law enforcement.

For the first generation, about 90% of its components will be made in the U.S. or in allied countries, Anduril says, most notably its CPU. BlueChip will run on a Snapdragon X Series built at TSMC’s massive new facility in Arizona. The CPU uses the same architecture that powers other laptops, including the Microsoft Surface, Qualcomm CEO Cristiano Amon said during the press conference.

“As of right now, the laptop’s bill of materials is about 90% U.S. and allied [ally countries], and we have a technical roadmap to get to 100% domestic over time,” Luckey said. “The intent in the long run is to have an option that is made entirely in the United States, 100% domestic. And I mean everything. I mean the displays. I mean the metallics. I mean the the magnetics. All of it.”

Right now, for instance, Luckey says he’s found an American supplier for the glass that covers the display, but the display itself is one of several components he says are only manufactured in China.

While Luckey says consumers are welcome to order a BlueChip — the company may even create a waiting list for interested consumers — this first PC won’t be designed for them. For one thing, as a hardened computer, it will be heavy and have battery-life constraints because of the engineering required to withstand rain, dust, and other rugged conditions.

The bigger point is to offer U.S. government agencies a product that complies with regulations requiring them to buy U.S.-made computers. In this case, the selling point is a transparent supply chain that customers can track and audit.

“These computers are the anti-surveillance computer. You fully control it,” Luckey described in a recent interview. “You know everything that is in it, every single component, and you know every software component that is running on it.”

Anduril is also working to ensure that the Arm-based machine works well with Windows apps, apps built for certain Linux distributions used by the government, and even archaic software that various agencies still run.

While Anduril hasn’t yet announced pricing, Luckey estimates it will cost about 20% more than typical high-end PCs.

“As much as I love BlueChip, if I was just a normal consumer making a rational decision without a kind of patriotic basis, I’d be better off buying a Dell laptop than an Anduril laptop because it’s going to be cheaper. I’m just talking about your average-person consumer, right, who doesn’t have any real need for this,” Luckey said.

But Luckey clearly hopes Anduril will eventually make U.S.-made laptops more in line with consumer desires. He said the product is the first in what he envisions as a full line of BlueChip computers, noting that he began his career making virtual reality headsets for consumers.

“I’m buying American cars, and then I buy a computer that’s made on the other side of the world, not just China, but you know, Japan, Korea,” he said. “It’s kind of humiliating that as an American and my birthright is computers, in my opinion, because America invented them, that I’m not able to buy an American computer.”

As for the potentially expensive folly of trying to take on China’s electronics components manufacturing industry single-handedly, Luckey joked about his internal reputation for “being a nut” with such big ideas. (He admits that he pushed his board to spend $900 million to build a factory for a jet before the company had even won a contract.)

“This is not one of those things. My investors do not think I’m a crazy person in this particular area,” he said. In fact, rugged computers are already about a $5 billion market.
