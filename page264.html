#4702. "We were wrong": Wardogs devs end Season 2's team-specific cash and XP bonus experiment after a single, tumultuous day

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Season 2 of Wardogs, the multiplayer sandbox FPS currently blasting and/or politely driving its way through early access, introduced an un usual "social experiment" when it launched yesterday. In an attempt to rehabilitate the reputation of its Lonestar faction, a.k.a the infamous Blue Team, developers Bulkhead began granting bonus XP and cash – the latter being the upgrade-focused game’s lifeblood – to Blue players alone. The hypothesis, apparently, was that more skilled players would give Lonestar a chance, helping boost its dismal winrates and perhaps improve its tarnished image.

One day in, then, how’s it going? “We were wrong. We fucked up,” says Bulkhead CEO Joe Brammer in a video announcing the bonus scheme’s immediate cancellation. Ah!

It turns out that while the promise of free money and experience did indeed incentive a wave of Blue Team picks, some players – to use Brammer’s given term, "tryhards" – were earning far more lucre than Bulkhead anticipated. Given the bonuses were a mere 5% to cash and 2.5% to XP, the developers’ expectation was that the experiment’s incentives were "not, for the median player, substantial enough to actually matter." Wardogs’ "top" players, however, have been taking home thousands upon thousands of war dollars in those bonuses alone, potentially upsetting the shooter’s progression balance.

The bonus-bribed rush to join Lonestar during match starts also, it seems, caused issues, as players would ditch severs once the blue crew filled up to go try their luck joining the faction elsewhere. Without enough players to slum it on the red and green teams, matches were delayed until all three teams were sufficiently manned.

Thus: one cancelled experiment, a few thousand players with freshly swollen pockets, and likely zero people having switched to the belief that Blue Team is filled with scholars and gentlemen. Still, the video suggests more conventional rebalancing techniques, namely moving spawn locations on the Bakuriani map, have been more effective and improving the troubled faction’s win percentages. Such changes will stay, even if the XP bonus has already been hotfixed out of existence.

Next on Bulkhead’s hitlist is a widely-used vehicle XP exploit, which I haven’t seen in action, but apparently involves repeatedly driving in and out of a faction’s HQ to rapidly amass minor experience rewards. With the help of Season 2’s more generous XP payouts for logistical feats, among which safe 'round trips' are counted, perpetrators have been prematurely unlocking late-game vehicles like tanks. The temporary fix, in place for now, is the slowing of vehicle progression rates to their Season 1 levels.

#4726. The creator of last year's cult hit RPG The Necromancer's Tale is back with The Lich Wars, a storybook RPG that follows 30 years of undead botheration

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Psychic Software have announced The Lich Wars, a standalone prequel for their widely acclaimed RPG The Necromancer's Tale. Widely acclaimed by me, anyway. I'm pretty wide, these days – the local Morrisons keeps doing discounts on 80-biccy packets of bourbon creams.

I liked The Necromancer's Tale specifically for how it wove a whole RPG around the narrative dynamics of a single RPG class, with players performing rituals in each chapter to develop their morbid spellcraft, while managing the suspicions of nearby townsfolk. I also really loved its choose-your-own-adventure prologue. The Lich Wars sort of spins the prologue out into a full game.

It's a partially voiced, branching storybook experience with skills and stats, evolving relationships and the possibility of character death (and presumably, undeath, if you end up dabbling in tomb wizardry). It casts you as a musketeer in the alt-1700s, battling through two of the titular lich wars in the course of a 30 year career with three main paths that also features plenty of regular, non-lich wars.

Here's how the devs characterise the plot: "The Lich Wars explores the early romantic & gothic themes of technological anxiety, unfettered progress, the ethics of war, and the destructive mental infection brought on by dark magic. This is a time at the cusp of rational thought and superstition; science and magic. The world is dark and dangerous. Courtly intrigue collides with black magic even as chemistry emerges from alchemy."

As regards the presentation, there are still elements of the old 3D world in the shape of maps and tabletop, but the bulk of it appears to consist of hand-drawn illustrations and unfurling paragraphs. "You will visit much of the alt-history kingdom of Rulsthen, near Venice," the devs promise. "You may also travel across Western Europe as you march to the Great Northern War and the War of Spanish Succession." Gosh, whole lotta wars. Can we not just have one big war - a "World War", if you will?

This feels like a more economical production than The Necromancer's Tale, but I think it's change for the better: toing and froing about the 3D town was my least favourite aspect of the older game, much as I enjoyed the writing of the setting. #4734. Cyberpunk 2077 movie "won't be a one-to-one translation of the game's story", CD Projekt Red boss confirms, and boy do I think that's the right move

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A more Edgerunners-esque original tale from Night City looks to be in the offing

Since the existence of plans for a Cyberpunk 2077 live-action adaptation from devs CD Projekt Red and Paramount Pictures was revealed last week, I've been hoping it won't take the form of porting V's tale from the game to the big screen. Such a thing wouldn't necessarily be terrible, but I like my game adaptations a bit more on the original story side of things, as Cyberpunk tie-in anime series Edgerunners has been. The good news is that one of CD Projekt Red's big bosses has now confirmed that this adaptation won't be beat-for-beat retelling of 'Honey, there's a Keanu in my brain'.

There is a caveat in that the interview in question with CDPR joint-CEO Michał Nowakowski has been conducted and printed in Polish by business site PAP Biznes. A machine translation of the article suggests the exec said the following about the Cyberpunk movie:

"Our contract with Anonymous Content expired, and in the meantime, an opportunity arose to establish cooperation with Paramount Pictures, and we decided that we would conduct a live-action Cyberpunk project in a new formula. At this point, we can only say that we definitely don't want to make a film adaptation of the game. We want to tell a story in the world of Cyberpunk, but it won't be a one-to-one translation of the game's story."

Wary of relying solely on machine-translated quotes given they can result in meanings being twisted or made misinterpretable, we've run the quote by a Polish contact in order to try and root out any potential muddling of Nowakowski's words by the auto-translation. I've also reached out to CD Projekt Red for comment.

In a previous statement at the time the movie plans we confirmed, all Nowakowski said was that the studio are "excited about Paramount as a future partner who shares our creative ambitions for the Cyberpunk franchise" and would share more info on the film "at the appropriate time".

Assuming the movie does go the Edgerunners-esque route of telling a Night City story separate from the game's tale, that's definitely the right move in my eyes. Not only would that mean its creators don't have to agonise over how to make the pacing of an open-world game mesh with a film's runtime and could avoid having to decide how square messing with players' preferred headcanons about what V did or didn't do at various points, but it gives the creatives behind the film a chance to offer a fresher tale that perhaps explores some elements of the Cyberpunk world that the game didn't give as much attention to.

Then again, to avoid earning my ire, you've also got to avoid falling into the Fallout Season 2 trap of telling a fresh tale, but being desperate to hammer as many references to a game in as possible without conclusively advancing its setting in a way that makes the events of said game feel as meaningful it should. Basically, I'm saying I might be a bit too picky to be the ideal person to be trying to please with a game adaptation for the big screen.

#4743. Penguin Colony review - the least expected Soulslike you'll play, and a cracking parody of Lovecraftian empires

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Penguin Colony reviewA clever and unforgiving send-up of Lovecraftian race horror, with lumpy platforming and a surprising amount of FromSoftware DNA.

Penguin Colony is probably the best pisstake of Lovecraft and Lovecraftian horror I've played. It's the best because it places Lovecraft's universe at a creaturely remove, amplifying its absurdity without having to drop any punchlines. Entering a ravaged polar campsite, I advance among burning tents and uniformed corpses to a pioneer seated on a crate, who is busily sawing his own face off. Ice and snow shrill overhead and Lenval Brown's narration rumbles in my bones, speaking of eldritch relics and unquenchable ambitions, of "queer creatures" and inexplicable deaths, of madness and glory, of "scientific explanations" and empires from other dimensions. I consider all this, and say: Fnaaarp.

Another man standing at the top of a pile of sandbags catches my attention. I waddle up to him and his head explodes, emitting a gory spark that resolves into the word "sex". I regard the body gravely and say: urk urk urk. Then I flop on my belly and slither out of camp, chasing the whistling wires of the wind. For I am a penguin. These creatures, their flags, their words and their elder gods mean little to me. I might accidentally witness their hubris and self-deception and savage talk of racial hierarchies and destiny, but I am not some befuddled Miskatonic bookworm consumed by sweaty urges toward worlds beyond ken. I am a penguin. I just happened to be hopping through here while all this weird shit was going on.

All told, the penguins of Penguin Colony are a winningly silly source of critical distance from the carnival-mirror imperialism of, in this case, At The Mountains Of Madness, a Lovecraft novella that usefully features a bunch of monster penguins. Created by Umurangi Generation devs Origame Digital, an Indigenous team working in Melbourne on Wurundjeri Woiwurrung land, Penguin Colony retains the novella's broad arc of explorers from the global north venturing into a frozen eldritch necropolis, but otherwise gives the story a kicking.

In this adaptation, set a few years after Lovecraft's death, the explorers have become German Nazi occultists with a few English collaborators, including Brown's temporally adrift narrator. Rather than the impossible architecture of Lovecraft's stories, later areas resemble dessicated modern cities built on stolen land. The game also introduces a redemption narrative in the shape of a group of Māori warriors, who are briefly controllable between chapters. The Māori group are questing to stop the dafty fascists from waking a dormant starspawn, which they have somehow misidentified as the "one true Aryan king". Again, though, none of that's your problem. You're a goddamn penguin.

There are several playable penguin varieties in Penguin Colony, and you can switch between them freely by walking up to one and hitting Y button. Should you accidentally flop into a crevasse or a puddle of wormy Shoggoth juices, the world is full of back-up birds, dozing on bergs or in caverns. There are rudiments of a class system, which factors into optional terrain puzzles: some penguins are heavy enough to press floor switches, while baby penguins can't swim but are able to fit through tunnels, revealing new areas or shortcuts.

While the game recreates the calls of different species, I'm not sure Penguin Colony does much justice to the inner life of penguins. They're just a fun, disposable dramatic device, riffing on Pingu and Happy Feet as much as they do the six-foot dreadsquawkers of Lovecraft's story. I'm also not sure Penguin Colony is the best penguin platformer I've played, which isn't exactly a huge tent, because penguins, bless 'em, are not obvious ground-based acrobats. The penguins in Penguin Colony are, to be frank, janky as hell. They boop and stutter up inclines, often allowing you to 'cheat' the geography. They 'slide' like their bottoms are misfiring turbojets. In general, the game's presentation is a bit of a penguin right now. Tutorial tips reappear erratically, and there's a stamina circle that takes up far too much of the screen.

The penguins are overall performant in getting you from A to B, but this ain't sand-surfing in Journey. Still, one benefit of being a janky-ass penguin is that you often have to move quite ponderously, which enforces a lengthier appreciation of the game's environments - especially, the bleeding helix catacombs and blue-glared "downtown" areas of later chapters. There's some terrific scenery in Penguin Colony, beyond the opening dreary ice sheet: one enormous ruin houses a lumpy, rippled structure that could be either a brain coral or a god's petrified brain, defying both comprehension and articulation in the maze-folded contours of its squamous and primordial immensity. Or as my penguin at the time put it: HOOONK.

While I was expecting Penguin Colony to put the boot into settlers and Lovecraft, after trying the demo, I was startled to find that on some level, it's a Soulslike. Or maybe an anti-Soulslike. Later on, there's a "boss battle" featuring a giant, jerky Shoggowotsit that pays open homage to FromSoftware, complete with harrowing orchestral score and AOE gravity attacks. It's like somebody tried to rebuild the Gaping Dragon out of mops and pieces of accordion. Being a penguin, I merely flopped on my belly once again and scooched through the horror's ankles, onto the next region. I have done likewise with many Souls enemies, unless restrained by fog gates. Is there a Penguin Knight in Dark Souls? There should be.

In the wake of that encounter, I thought anew about all the non-penguin life I'd met during my four-hour Penguin Colony playthrough – all the near-dead fascists in foetal positions or kneeling before idols, their bodies mutilated, bleating in despair. You see hollow folk like that all over Lordran, the difference here being that as one in a million local penguins, I am not perceived as an intruder. No corner ambushes for me. The same likely isn't true of the Māori characters, who are essentially playing a 'real' Soulslike behind the scenes, navigating a more hostile incarnation of the same world.

The giant, jerky Shoggowotsit also features in one of the Māori adventurer sequences. The game dangles the carrot of a 'proper' bossfight, here, but fades mockingly to white when you take the first swing. Hence, 'anti-Soulslike': Penguin Colony invokes certain genre expectations in order to frustrate them. Or perhaps, to redirect them.

In lieu of combat, the FromSofty parallels appear most strongly in the torturous, Blighttown-worthy route design, and especially, the framing of pick-ups. There are collectible glowballs that bestow not gear and XP, but English and Te reo Māori words that often chime with or invite commentary on their surroundings – a really intriguing revising of the poetic distribution of item lore in Dark Souls. There are also photographs, taken by a Nazi propagandist, that self-combust to release snatches of flashback dialogue.

After the credits roll, you're shown a Zodiac constellation of all the lore you've found, a web of secrets and lies the devs offer in-game as a basis for recapturing "pre-social media secret finding and community storytelling". It's an incentive to replay, though Origame Digital's Tali Faulkner has elsewhere commented that the team would rather people don't try to 100% the lore – it's more about swapping notes with other players, like doom-sodden Miskatonic boffins trying to work out which bit of Cthulhu goes where.

Origame Digital plan to make more Lovecraftian games - they do, indeed, have their sights on Cthulhu, having begun their adventure with a Lovecraft story that is simultaneously well-known and less-explored, by virtue of starring a different faction of cosmic deities. I hope they also continue their experiments with Soulslike design, because aside from putting the H.P. in Penguin, their latest game has shed a strange new light on a genre I thought I knew all too well.

#4749. The pleasures of Double Fine's Thank You Bus Driver are not in the destination, nor the journey, but in slapping my disobedient passengers

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Showing some gratitude is the least these scribbly wretches can do. The passengers in Thank You Bus Driver – Double Fine’s new comedy public transport sim – are almost invariably rowdy, entitled hypocrites, happy to sully my single-decker by picking their toes and bellowing down their phones, while only ever being seconds away from a strop if another traveller does the same. Still, here I am, locked in symbiotic misery with them, desperate to choke down the scraps of acknowledgement they’ll spare for arriving at their stop in good time. I hate them. Please love me.

To be clear, I don’t regret playing Thank You Bus Driver. It’s a brisk three hours of chaotic wheelsmanship, dense in visual gags and amusingly exaggerated physics, in which you are both driver and disciplinarian. Catch sight of someone breaking the bus rules, and instead of detachedly tapping a sign, you leave your seat to deliver a corrective slap. This risks the still-moving bus careering off course, and thus a shift-ending missed stop, but silencing the troublemakers is the only way to ensure everyone else arrives happy. And happiness means Thank Yous, the only currency that matters.

It’s a fun little time, with just enough multitasking to stretch your attention without snapping it in frustration. I wonder, though, which Double Fine made it. Was it the newly independent, hugs and jokes, Amnesia Fortnight Double Fine? Or the Double Fine who talked themselves out of a union, and whose flight from Microsoft ownership involved jettisoning a quarter of their staff?

There are ample signs of the 'nice' one. Not that games like Keeper and Kiln were creatively bankrupt trendchasers, but Thank You Bus Driver has that endearing, rough-and-tumble quality of human beings trying something out. Passengers come in the form of Bigfoots and swimming rings and microwaves full of fish, and someone – maybe multiple people! – took the time to give them all uniquely goofy animations for dropping a coin in a slot. Your slapping arm is uncomfortably long and rubbery, a pneumatic limb whose weirdness goes completely unaddressed. Crashes and, indeed, your own acts of open-palmed violence are exclusively played for laughs – usually good ones. I confess to intentionally aiming for the local cows, just because their betrayed expression and excessively long windscreen-sliding motion make for too well-executed a joke to resist.I adore, too, how 'resetting' the bus from a stuck position drops it back on the road... from thirty feet in the air, metal groaning as it slams into the asphalt.

It can be clever, too. There is a heavy randomisation element, from the placement of stops to the timing of passenger behaviour to the vaguely roguelike-ish item upgrades you’re offered by a commuting trenchcoat oddball, but the necessity of juggling bus happiness with bus urgency breeds actual tactics. Do you move carefully, keeping a constant eye on the mirror to instantly slap down transgressions, at the cost of letting the shift clock tick down? Or do you focus on caning it through town, allowing passenger resentment to build before attempting a mass slapfest to restore harmony at the last moment? In what probably doesn’t bode well for whenever I drive my future children to Legoland, I’ve developed a taste for the latter, and have devised a kind of 'unguided missile' technique based on its principles: point the bus somewhat towards the next stop, accelerate, then spin around to beat down on farepayers as we all roll to a gentle stop. Or off a cliff.

For all of its spark, though, I can’t shake the feeling that Thank You Bus Driver has some more cynical thinking behind it. It is, ultimately, a Numbers Go Up game: there’s no real narrative, and no jeopardy beyond having to start a new shift if you run out of time. Your one and only reason to exist is a bigger collection of Thank Yous, which are delivered with slot machine chime effects, and pursued through service to arseholes. Like a less subversive Roman Sands RE:Build, on wheels.

Would it be unfair to suggest this has a lingering whiff of Microsoft about it? You can, at least, throw hands with your customers. But even a full house of swiftly punished fish-microwavers will just be replaced at the next stop with a new, possibly identical batch. The grind for approval never ends, until it does, when the game decides you’ve amassed enough gratitude points to be considered a valuable entity.

That said, Thank You Bus Driver doesn’t have much legs beyond a single playthrough anyway. While those three hours are entertaining enough, in spite of the service industry angst, there’s no getting round the fact that you’re driving the same bus through the same small town with the same handful of rules, every time. Attempts to mix this up fall flat: night shifts, rain, and 'conventions' (where the bulk of your passengers share a single archetype) are eventually thrown in, but none of them meaningfully change how a drive plays out. You’re more at the mercy of the regular randomisations, where an unlucky succession of distant stops can kill a shift even with careful passenger management. Chaining nearby stops, likewise, is the best (if equally chance-dependent) means of accumulating mad TYs.

Still, as a single, foot-down charge to the credits, it’s a decent way to chuckle your way through an afternoon. And to answer my own question, I still think Thank You Bus Driver shows off Double Fine’s creative flair, much more so than it raises worries about the new era. Hopefully that spirit endures into their future independent projects – maybe with a little less gacha ding-dinging.

#4566. Fortnite is coming to Linux so we may soon be able to play it and other games like Apex Legends on Steam Deck and Steam Machine

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Epic Games is officially working on bringing its battle royale shooter Fortnite to Linux. The announcement was tucked away at the bottom of Epic's widely mocked blog post revealing the new, "vibe-coded" Epic Games Store, which is also coming to Linux.

At the time, Epic explained it's going to be working with Linux distro maintainers on a set of security requirements needed to run Fortnite. Distros - short for distributions - are operating system variants of Linux, each with its own goals and prioritised system operations. Valve's SteamOS, for instance, is based on Arch Linux. Because of the many discrepancies between Linux distros, each would need to implement Epic's security requirements individually if they want Fortnite to run on their operating system. Fortnite will arrive on Linux via the EGS launcher, but only if a "critical mass of distro maintainers" is reached. Epic didn't specify numbers.

This is big news for several reasons. For one, the move would likely make Fortnite playable on SteamOS PCs and handhelds, including Steam Machine and Steam Deck. Furthermore, even if that "critical mass" is never reached, Epic says its work will be available to other developers interested in bringing their games to Linux.

Perhaps the biggest reason this is a big deal, however, has to do with Easy Anti-Cheat, the Epic-owned anti-cheat service used in Fortnite and dozens of other games - including Dead by Daylight, Rocket League, Apex Legends, and even Elden Ring. While Easy Anti-Cheat does support Linux, including for some of the games just mentioned, many developers choose not to enable it, simply because its "Linux implementation couldn't offer the same level of protection as our Windows implementation," as Epic explained in a separate blog post. This is why many games that use Easy Anti-Cheat aren't playable on Steam Machine or Steam Deck. The post even names Valve specifically, saying the team will "continue its open line of communication" to support Steam hardware devices "to the maximum extent technically possible".

Part of the reason Linux has been a difficult problem for anti-cheat makers to solve is the platform's open nature, meaning certain - required - system-level protections are not guaranteed. On Windows, you can ask users to enable features like TPM 2.0 and Secure Boot, which create that secure environment once hardware requirements are met.

While some of that work can be done on Linux, it requires the interest and participation of distro maintainers. Easy Anti-Cheat plans to use Linux features such as signed kernel, signed kernel modules, and secure boot with attestation to achieve that goal. "These requirements build on work that distribution maintainers already do," explains Epic, "and the more distributions that meet these requirements by default, the fewer players will need to change their setup to play." The goal is to "bring the level of protection much closer to par with Windows".

There is no timeline for any of this at present, but Easy Anti-Cheat will test the announced plans in Fortnite first before making them available to other developers. In other words, if Fortnite gets a release date on Linux, you'll know the work has paid off and we're closer than ever to achieving anti-cheat parity between Windows and Linux.

#4095. The Difference Between Suunto's Race, Run, and Vertical Series Smartwatches

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This week, Suunto launched its newest addition to its Race line of performance watches, the Race 3S. Suunto may be one of the lesser-known fitness watch brands, but its offerings, aimed at hikers and outdoorsy people, have long been well regarded. The company sorts its sports watches into families: Run, Race, and Vertical. Three families of watches, all with several generations of models and a lot of overlapping specs, are enough to leave you staring at a product page, wracked with decision fatigue. This week’s launch of the Suunto Race 3S, the newest member of the Race family, is a good enough reason for me to help you sort it out.

Best for: Road runners, 5K-to-marathon trainers, and anyone who wants a watch they forget they're wearing, including overnight. It's also a good first step into Suunto's ecosystem.

In her review of the Suunto Run my colleague Beth Skwarecki calls this watch on of her favorite running watches, pointing to features that not only rival, but maybe even outshine Garmin and Coros watches at a similar price point. Both watches in the Run series, the Run and the Run 2, are known for being easy to wear and simple to use, but still with all the core features of a reliable running watch.

Best for: Trail runners, triathletes, and runners who want serious training tools and navigation.

The Race S is the compact option, while the Race 2 has since replaced the original Race and has a bigger screen and larger case. It might best suit people with bigger wrists who want a big display and don't mind the weight.

As of today, the Race 3S is the lightweight premium pick. It combines the Race S's compact size with a titanium and sapphire build, a bigger battery, a brighter screen, and double the storage. Suunto says its battery capacity is 46 percent larger than the Race S's, and its display is 233 percent brighter.

When in doubt, the Race series is the middle of the lineup, and it's a natural sweet spot to land on. If you can't decide between the Run and Race options, Beth's got you covered with an in-depth comparison.

Best for: Ultrarunners, mountaineers, backpackers, and anyone doing multi-day trips where charging isn't an option. It's also a good fit if you want a watch that holds up to rough use.

The Vertical 2 is marketed as an expedition-grade watch. It weighs about 87 grams and measures 49 mm, and it's available in stainless steel or titanium. It's water-resistant to 100 meters. It has a built-in LED flashlight that the lighter Race and Run watches lack, a redesigned heart rate sensor, and more storage and processing power than the original. Its main selling point is battery life—allegedly up to 65 hours of GPS in Performance mode and up to 250 hours in Tour mode.

The original Vertical is the older model, with a solar-assisted MIP display. It's still sold and often discounted, but the Vertical 2's AMOLED screen is a major step up in clarity.

The Suunto Run series is for runners who want a light, simple watch. The Race series is for performance athletes who want a premium build and strong all-around training and navigation tools. The Vertical series is for people who go far from civilization and want the longest battery life and the toughest build.

The Suunto lineup also includes Core outdoor watches and dive computers like the Ocean, but those are a different conversation. For runners and trail athletes, the choice comes down to Run, Race, or Vertical, and which of them fits how far, how long, and how rough you like to get:

If you mostly run on roads and want a lightweight entry-point watch, look at the Run.

If you race, train across several sports, or run trails and want a premium build, look at the Race 3S, or the Race if you're sticking to a smaller budget. Or you can choose the Race 2 if you prefer a bigger case and screen.

If your adventures last days rather than hours, choose the Vertical 2.
