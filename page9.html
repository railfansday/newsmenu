#736. Thousands of German gamblers could sue for their money back

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

German gamblers may finally be able to beat the house, thanks to new court rulings in the EU and in Germany. The decisions could help gambling addicts — but the legal road might be difficult.

A German court has followed the European Court of Justice in finding that gamblers can seek compensation from gambling companies if they lost money when online casinos and fruit machines were illegal in Germany.

The verdicts could have major consequences for gambling addicts, as thousands of people could potentially bring civil lawsuits against gambling companies.

A series of court decisions at regional, federal, and European level have found in favor of gamblers in the last year, beginning with the Higher Regional Court Stuttgart in September 2025, which agreed with a plaintiff in the state of Baden-Württemberg who sued the Malta-based online betting company Tipico Games.

The plaintiff lost around €10,000 ($11,200) between 2020 and 2022, but because online gambling was prohibited in Germany until 2021, the court found that operators had therefore received the winnings without legal basis and must return them.

Tipico Games then appealed to the Federal Court of Justice (BGH), the highest civil and criminal court in Germany. In an initial verbal hearing in mid-September this year, the court followed the principle of the plaintiffs' case on one key point: That gamblers have the right to claim back losses if the companies did not have a license in Germany at the time the losses were incurred. But a final decision on the case is only expected to be made in January 2027.

Online casinos and fruit machines were banned in Germany from 2008 to 2021, but several gambling operators were active in the country because they had gaming licenses in other EU countries.

In April this year, the European Court of Justice ruled that EU law does not preclude a member state from "prohibiting certain services," or from "attaching civil-law consequences to that prohibition." In other words, even if a gambling company has a legitimate license in one member state, that doesn't override national gambling laws elsewhere.

Thomas Dünchheim, a business lawyer at global law firm Greenberg Traurig who specializes in gambling law, said law firms were now gathering clients — sometimes trawling the internet with the help of AI tools — to prepare massive collective lawsuits. Dünchheim himself has people who have lost tens of thousands of euros come to him seeking representation.

"The pendulum is swinging more and more towards the consumer. It's a bit like in America — we're seeing a mass phenomenon like the Dieselgate lawsuits against VW," he told DW. "And there are lawyers who will collect all these little cases, bundle them, and then maybe even drive it forward with the help of a legal financing firm. It's a good business for all involved, and the ones that will have to pay are the gambling firms who operated unlawful gambling in Germany."

The decisions could bring a measure of help for addicts, as online gambling can be particularly dangerous for people with addiction issues. According to Tobias Hayer, a psychologist at the Institute for Public Health and Nursing Research at Bremen University, that comes down to four main features of online gambling: Anonymity and the consequent lack of social control, 24/7 accessibility, the frictionless nature of cashless transactions, and the high frequency of games played.

"These court verdicts are not surprising at all," Hayer told DW, but he added that he had an ambivalent attitude to them. "On the one hand I can imagine that this will be a huge relief to many gamblers who had an addiction problem. On the other hand, if these people suddenly get a lot of money in their hands, but they haven't worked through their problems, then of course there's a danger of recidivism."

But Dünchheim suggested that the gamblers may not have such an easy ride when making their claims. It's true that German law has now concluded that if the gambling contract that the player makes with the company turns out to be unlawful in the country where they live, the player can claim their losses back. But only if the player themselves "did not act illegally," as Dünchheim put it.

"You also have to say: Many of these players deliberately went to games in this gray zone because the winnings are potentially bigger," he said. "Because the companies don't pay taxes."

"Then the defense team of the gambling company will of course say: 'You took part in an illegal game yourself, so you were acting illegally.' And this question of interpretation is not simple," he added. "It's a very interesting intellectual question, but it's also existential for the gambling companies."

The Tipico case is perhaps particularly interesting in the German context because, although the company is based in Malta, it was founded in 2004 by a group of German lawyers. The company is a big employer, with several thousand employees worldwide, and has been a sponsor of more than one top German football club. Tipico has also said that it has rigorous consumer protection measures in place to identify and prevent dangerous betting behavior.

But there was some legal ambiguity about its online operations in Germany up until 2021.

"They always had lawyers who could attest that what they are doing conforms to European law, because it is permitted in a European Union member state," said Dünchheim. "But that is a false assumption, as it has now been established that a gambling permit in another EU state doesn't apply in Germany, because unlike other areas, gambling law isn't harmonized across the bloc."

"Tipico was started by two businessmen and grew big in a gray zone," as Dünchheim put it. "They had professional lobbying firms on board and they hired experienced lawyers, they had private equity firms on board. But of course they are carrying a huge financial risk."

#727. AI-assisted dying? Doris Dörrie's new film addresses prospect of care robots

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While the world debates the dangers of artificial intelligence, filmmaker Doris Dörrie explores how humanoid robots could change the way we deal with aging, caregiving and how people choose to die.

Hamburg in the near future: Instead of human drivers, robots deliver parcels. In cafes, robots roll between the tables, serving diners. On the streets, one encounters humanoid robots walking dogs — or the other way around, people taking their robot dogs for a walk.

This is the setting of Doris Dörrie's new film, "Frau Winkler verlässt das Haus" ("Mrs. Winkler Leaves the House"). At the core of the plot is an elderly woman who can no longer live on her own after a fall. With her only daughter unable to provide permanent care, the solution seems simple enough: a care robot with a built-in EOL program, or "End of Life" mode. This robot not only assists with everyday life but can also provide assisted dying under certain conditions, namely when the person explicitly wishes to end their life and meets the relevant medical criteria.

Dörrie's film tackles several major social issues with remarkable lightness. These include the challenges facing aging societies such as Germany, Japan and China, the question of dying on one's own terms and a mother-daughter relationship that raises questions of guilt and responsibility. Even though the story is set in Germany, such issues resonate globally.

"I write about what personally moves and interests me," Dörrie told DW.

One of the best-known contemporary German film directors, Dörrie achieved her breakthrough in 1985 with the comedy "Men…" She gained further international recognition with films such as 2008's "Cherry Blossoms" and "Greetings from Fukushima" (2016).

Assisted suicide is one of the most complex ethical questions of our time. Laws vary around the world. Though some countries permit active euthanasia under certain conditions, others prohibit and criminalize all forms of assisted dying.

In her film, Dörrie asks what happens when artificial intelligence begins calculating the cost of caring for an elderly person and weighing whether that cost is still "worth" bearing for society.

The underlying idea isn't new, Dörrie said, pointing to the Japanese legend of Narayama, which is also mentioned in the film. According to the legend, the eldest children were responsible for taking their ageing parents to a mountain and leaving them there to die because there was not enough food for everyone. "And that's also the debate we are currently facing to a certain extent: Can we really afford to let older people live longer and longer?"

In Dörrie's film, elderly people are allowed to make their own decisions once they reach a certain state of health — but they can't change their minds afterwards. Yet what does it really mean to make a decision freely? "It's a tricky situation," says Dörrie, because the question is to what extent one is also pressured into making this decision, "not only because it makes more economic sense, but also because one is no longer a burden on one's loved ones — in this case, one's daughter..."

The film often raises these questions indirectly and with plenty of dark humor. At one point, a care adviser presents the EOL feature to Mrs. Winkler as an attractive and, above all, straightforward additional option, making it sound as though there would be nothing more for her to worry about.

Dörrie is not interested in demonizing AI in her film — quite the opposite. In the film, the care robot, which Mrs. Winkler names Pavlovich, helps the elderly woman rediscover some of her zest for life and get out of the house.

"When you travel through Germany, you see small houses everywhere, and statistics show that an elderly person lives alone in every other house," Dörrie said. "If AI can help in these circumstances, why not use it?" She said she sought to explore this ambiguity in her film. Dividing things into black and white would neither be interesting nor, in her view, accurate.

Dörrie and her crew were surprised during filming by how quickly people can develop emotional bonds with robots. She attributes this to the human brain. "We regard anything that moves and speaks as a living being. We can't help it," she explains. And that is precisely what we need to pay attention to when interacting with AI, Dörrie says. "How often are we truly aware that we are talking to a machine rather than a living being? These are things we still have to learn."

As AI develops, it seems that the boundaries between humans and machines are becoming increasingly blurred. That is precisely why we need to talk about it, Doris Dörrie says. In her view, things become problematic "when we can no longer distance ourselves from technology and stop forming our own thoughts."

Human beings need to ask who we are and what truly defines us, Dörrie said. "I believe there is a major difference between robots and us, and that is the ability to play. A robot might be able to initiate a game, she explains, "but play itself is something profoundly human because it has no particular meaning or purpose."

Alongside these philosophical questions about what it means to be human, Dörrie makes a clear call for AI regulation. "At some point, we had to discuss nuclear weapons internationally, too. We've talked about all kinds of issues in the past, and we haven't just talked about them — we've also managed to find solutions," she points out.

"And I believe we need to move very quickly on this, too," Dörrie said.

#718. Resignation of Bärbel Bas shines light on harsh era of German politics

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

German Labor Minister Bärbel Bas has stepped down, citing her need to grieve a loved one and leave behind the harsh realities of government. So how did German politics get so heated?

For a brief moment, a sense of quiet sympathy could be felt in Berlin's often unforgiving political arena: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas, Germany's labor minister and the co-leader of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) announced her resignation from all government and party positions. The 58-year-old politician cited a personal tragedy as the reason for her decision: "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I have come to realize that I need time to work through my grief," Bas wrote in a statement.

Bas also wrote that sustained criticism from the conservative governing parties, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU), had contributed to her decision to step down. In her statement, she wrote that she had been forced to "conclude that public attacks on me, unfortunately also from within the ranks of our coalition partner, continued to intensify during this personally difficult time."

The labor minister had recently been responsible for implementing complex changes to Germany's public pension system — including increasing the retirement age — and had been urged by some conservative politicians, in rather harsh terms, not to delay the project any further.

According to a report in the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper, one remark in particular appears to have weighed heavily on her: During an off-the-record briefing with journalists, Chancellor Friedrich Merz, of the CDU, reportedly criticized the pace of her efforts despite knowing that she was working through a personal tragedy.

Did Bas feel so overwhelmed by pressures both personal and political that she chose to step down rather than continue to endure them?

Mirco Liefke, a researcher with the Institute for Media and Communication Studies in the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the Freie Universität Berlin, told DW that unfairness and harshness have always been part of politics. However, he added: "What I do think is new is the potential for escalation and the speed that social media have brought to public debate. Along with that has come an incentive for individual actors to adopt ever more radical positions."

There are few public spaces left in which politicians can speak with the expectation that their remarks will not be reported. Merz is said to have made critical remarks of Bas during an off-the-record briefing whose contents were not intended for publication.

Though he was fully aware of her personal circumstances, Merz reportedly said Bas was barely visible in public and failing to put forward proposals of her own.

"It has effectively become a business model to leak information that is meant to remain confidential and thereby generate attention," Liefke said.

A spokesperson for Merz declined to confirm or deny the report.

Felix Banaszak, the co-leader of Germany's center-left Greens, has known Bas fior years, with both hailing from Duisburg, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, albeit born a couple of decades apart.

"Toughness and ruthlessness are nothing new in politics," Banaszak told DW. "But we still need to find a different way of doing things if we do not want to keep wearing people down."

Banaszak said the SPD was partially responsible for the treatment of Bas. "The fact that Bärbel Bas encountered so little understanding for her personal situation from within the ranks of the coalition partner, and that apparently no one in the SPD was willing to step forward, defend her, and take her out of the line of fire, is simply not a good sign," he said.

"It would be naive to pretend that the attacks on a man would have unfolded in a similar way," he added.

Liefke offered a similar view: "Women politicians are subjected to an extraordinary degree of misogynistic and sexualized abuse — and they face a level of public exposure that is entirely new."

The debate over how Bas and her grief should have been treated soon took on some of the same characteristics as the attacks that had preceded her resignation. Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of the northeastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, described the attacks by the CDU/CSU as "inhuman," drawing sharp criticism from many conservative politicians. Merz publicly expressed his regret over the labor minister's resignation and wished her all the best.

Liefke said this was another tremnd in German politics. Once a politician decides to step down, he argues, public expressions of concern are often far more intense than they used to be and come from all sides: "This is a facade of concern," he said. "There is something ritualistic, almost reflexive, about it. People react this way because they are following another major trend: Emotionalization."

The attacks on Bas are just one example of the increasingly harsh tone of political debate. Formal reprimands in the Bundestag have risen sharply, particularly since the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) entered parliament in 2017. High-profile resignations have also made headlines, including that of Kevin Kühnert, the SPD's former secretary-general.

Kühnert stepped down in 2024. In April 2025, however, he told Die Zeit newspaper that he had felt constantly threatened by far-right extremists. Political debate, he said, is showing "an increasing lack of respect for the views of those who think differently."

Liefke said politicians once managed to maintain two distinct modes of communication: one public and one private. "When you think of earlier generations of politicians, what comes to mind is the distance they maintained, the relative lack of emotional display," he said. "Take Helmut Schmidt, for example. He did not openly grieve over things that came crashing down on him. At least, he did not show it publicly." Schmidt, of the SPD, served as chancellor from 1974 to 1982.

"Today, politicians such as Bärbel Bas benefit from drawing on their own life stories when they say, 'I come from a working-class background and worked my way up,'" Liefke said. "That can work to their advantage — but it also makes them far more visible, and far more vulnerable, as individuals."

Bas, who served as president of the Bundestag from 2021 to 2025, intends to retain her parliamentary seat. Life is likely to become quieter for her now as other politicians move into the spotlight, both in public and, perhaps, in private as well.

#708. Formula One in Rwanda: Sportswashing claims vs. hopes for African legacy

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Formula One will return to Africa for the first time since 1993. There is concern Rwanda are using it as another sportswashing opportunity, but there is also hope the event's legacy can impact wider African society.

Rwanda's sports portfolio has grown after confirming it will host Formula One races from the early 2030s. The track is to be built in Bugesera near Kigali International Airport, which Qatar Airways, an F1 sponsor, owns 60% of.

"To be an African and to see it happening in real time, for me in my lifetime because Kyalami [the track in South Africa where F1 was last hosted in Africa] was before I was born, I'm more than happy to have it on the continent," South African presenter and content creator Nqobile Khwezi told Sky Sports.

"To be able to give that taste back to the rest of the world and the audience of Formula One would be an absolute delight."

F1 have also highlighted the benefits for the host nation.

"Through this race we have the opportunity to inspire and bring the passion of F1 to our more than 100 million fans across Africa and firmly believe that sport can be a positive force for good," an F1 spokesperson told The Athletic.

DW contacted Rwanda's Ministry of Sports for comment, but did not receive a promised reply.

For the country's President, Paul Kagame, another move into sport is hardly a surprise given his ventures into European football and the NBA in recent years. Rwanda even sponsored Bayern Munich, but fan and media backlash over the deal caused the Bundesliga club to drop the commercial element with the country's tourist arm, Visit Rwanda, last year.

With many critics accusing Kagame and Rwanda of sportswashing, the arrival of F1, a sport valued at approximately $34 billion (€30 billion), according to sports business website Sportico, brings fresh questions.

"This deal may have positive economic and social impacts across Africa, but we should be realistic about circumstances on the ground in Rwanda," Lewis Mudge, Central Africa director at Human Rights Watch, told DW.

"Human Rights Watch wrote to Formula One in February 2025 to ask how it is addressing the human rights impacts of its activities, including regarding Rwanda and other countries’ bid to host a F1 Grand Prix. We did not receive a response," said Mudge, adding that F1 should only approve racing in the country after addressing the human rights impacts of hosting a race there.

"Rwanda, like other countries, has also used high-profile sports deals and races in the past to distract from its abysmal human rights record. Rwanda is currently the power behind the abusive M23 rebel group in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo and its military forces have been implicated in unlawful displacement and deaths of civilians in violation of international humanitarian law."

For Mohamed Keita, returning the sport to Africa is overdue, but the choice of country is telling.

"The fact that F1 excluded the world's second largest continent from its race series for three decades is indefensible in its own right and is actually a stain on a sport which claims to be global," Keita, Senior Policy Officer, Africa, at the Human Rights Foundation told DW.

"The choice of Rwanda, however, is deeply controversial," Keita continued. "Rwanda is a brutal dictatorship. Its regime is under partial US sanctions for its military occupations of territories of eastern DRC. Rwanda is arming and financing a militia involved in rapes, massacres, forced recruitment and violent plunder of natural resources."

Formula One has history on the continent. In 1958, there was a race in Morocco and more recently, in South Africa, where the last F1 race in Africa took place at the Kyalami racetrack in Johannesburg in 1993. Many previous races in the country were overshadowed by the policy of apartheid.

Despite Rwanda securing the deal first, South Africa believes it will host before them. When Rwanda announced their hosting, South Africa's President Cyril Ramaphosa sent his congratulations in a social media post, adding: "This contract is a recognition of the capabilities of the Rwandan nation and of our continent more broadly."

South Africa's Sports Minister Gayton McKenzie also congratulated Rwanda, but pivotally added that South Africa "will get F1, even before 2030."

There is no denying that hosting a race in South Africa would cost significantly less time and money, given the existing infrastructure.

In either country, comes a legacy opportunity. Sports such as Formula One, where the majority of decision-makers, drivers, team principles and engineers are white men, are a living example of the unlevel playing field. The hope of racing in Africa is that it will go some way to changing that.

"I think that's the kind of aspirational drive of hosting a Formula 1 race. It's leveraging STEM [science, technology, engineering, and mathematics] education, technology, aspiring young girls or women, leveling the playing fields, if it's for all those other reasons and we can make it work then I say yes to Formula 1 in Africa," Dr. Kamilla Swart-Arries, an expert in South Africa's events industry and the legacy of sports events, told DW in 2024.

"I hope F1 will consider further investment in developing African talent in motorsport," said Keita. "There's a lot of good that F1 can do if it genuinely wants to grow the sport in Africa, but Rwanda does not make sense at this moment unless you're doing it purely for the benefit of one dictator and the beauty of the hilly terrain."

"The proposition here is to take the world's most expensive sport to Rwanda, which is one of the world's poorest countries. Rwanda is consistently listed among the UN's list of the 25 least developed countries in the world," said Keita.

The return of the fastest show in sports is a huge moment for both Rwanda and Africa. It raises questions about President Kagame's use of sport, but it also offers an opportunity to shape the event's legacy. Unlike the race Rwanda wants to host, this is not about being the fastest but about making the most of the chance.

#699. EU promotes sovereign, trusted AI as it struggles to catch US and China

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At an international AI summit in Dublin, EU leaders promoted trusted and independent artificial intelligence. Critics question whether the bloc can close the gap with the US and China while maintaining strict regulation.

If supremacy in artificial intelligence was an Olympic discipline, there would only be two serious contenders for gold on current trajectories: the US and China. The European Union, as has been the case in many areas of cutting-edge technology in recent times, is barely in the AI conversation.

Former European Central Bank President Mario Draghi, whose 2024 report highlighted the EU's desperate need to be more innovative to become more competitive, said recently that AI is "perhaps the most promising" route the EU has to raise growth today. However, he decried the fact that the European Union controls little of the value chain.

"Being cut off from AI, once the economy runs on it, would be more like being cut off from the US financial system," he wrote in an opinion piece for the Financial Times. "The effects would be catastrophic."

Draghi's spirit was on full display in Dublin on Wednesday at an international AI summit the Irish government convened as part of its six-month stint hosting the rotating EU presidency.

The summit's motto, "Harnessing AI to Revolutionize Europe's Competitiveness," might as well have been dreamed up by the Italian former prime minister.

Harnessing AI has become a lot more complicated. A slew of controversies around AI agents going rogue, from the Hugging Face attack in June when 1,200 agents hacked an online platform, to an Anthropic-designed agent sending a fake tip to US police over an unsolved murder, have caused alarm.

Many from within the tech industry, including Microsoft founder Bill Gates and the leading AI frontier labs themselves, have called for a development pausein order for safety measures to catch up.

Yet positivity was largely the name of the game at the summit, with tech and AI industry leaders joining Irish and EU politicians to declare AI's transformative economic and societal potential — while also emphasizing the need for strong regulation and safety standards.

Irish Prime Minister Micheal Martin made it clear that Ireland was all in on AI. He echoed European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's recent assertion that the technology represented a "tipping point." He also repeatedly highlighted what he sees as the bloc's commitment to safety.

"We are determined to capture the dividend by mitigating the risks," Martin told the event. "In Europe, we have charted our path ... balancing innovation and opportunity with a trustworthy, human-centered approach to AI."

Meanwhile, Henna Virkkunen, the European Commission executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, security and democracy, revealed at the event that the EU had begun its first enforcement actions under its new AI Act, requesting information around security and safety from advanced AI model providers.

However, other topics that concern AI's many critics, from those fearing the massive environmental impact of data centers to others warning about possible AI-related job losses, were largely absent from the discussions.

Outside the venue, a group of protesters led by People Before Profit, a left-wing Irish political party, criticized the summit for not focusing on these issues.

"There is a deliberate blurring of the line here with the type of AI that is being promoted," Alexandra Day, one of the protest organizers, told DW.

"It is really being pushed on workers with very little consent. There's no right for workers to opt out of using AI now that it's coming into the workplace."

A sense of urgency was palpable from the speakers inside the summit. Executives from some of the world's biggest AI and tech firms talked up Europe's potential. Yet the EU evidently has plenty of ground to catch up on in terms of AI development.

Despite ramping up its data center capacity, it still lags far behind the US and China. It hosts 5% of the world's AI computing capacity compared to around 75% for the US.

The EU is conspicuously absent from lists of the leading AI frontier labs, with only French company Mistral coming close to rivaling the top labs. Its production of leading-edge chips is also nowhere near that of the top US or Taiwanese producers.

As part of its plan to make ground on the US and China, the Irish government, in its role as EU presidency host, presented a policy document at the event.

She started taking action against Hitler as a teenager and never stopped until her death in a concentration camp. Berlin's Blücherplatz is now being renamed after her.

In the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

#672. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

In a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

And it’s important to note that a nation’s average salary in the survey can be skewed by sample biases with respect to factors such as location within a nation, employment sector and career stage.

In Brazil, where 21% of respondents report earning less than $15,000, a low or modest salary by US or western-European standards can “provide a reasonably comfortable life locally”, especially outside the most expensive cities such as Rio de Janeiro or Sao Paolo, says Haroldo Ribeiro, a physicist at the State University of Maringá in Brazil. “Still, I would not conclude that Brazilian scientists are generally well paid,” he says. Although salaries in public universities there have increased in the past few years, this comes after a long period of stagnation, he says, making it a recovery, not a raise.

Only 44% of people in the total sample are satisfied with their pay, but this varies between countries. Significantly more scientists in Canada (61%), Germany (68%) and the United States (55%) are satisfied with their income than the global average. The countries with at least 100 respondents that had the lowest numbers of scientists satisfied with their salary are Turkey (27%), China (39%) and Italy (40%).

China’s lower-than-average satisfaction with salary comes despite survey data suggesting that Chinese salaries have risen in US-dollar terms since Nature asked scientists the same question in 2021. Some 11% of Chinese respondents reported earning more than $80,000 this year, nearly doubling from 6% in 2021. Over the same time, in the United Kingdom, the proportion earning more than $80,000 grew from 19% to only 22%.

Salaries can vary substantially between China’s economically developed coastal areas, and its more-sparsely populated west, as well as between universities, says survey respondent Jingrui Sun, a freshwater ecologist at Yunnan University in Kunming, China. He describes his salary as “sufficient for me to live comfortably”, and says that it is common for research-intensive universities and institutions in wealthy parts of the country to offer employees performance-related pay, as well as housing subsidies and laboratory start-up packages.

But good salaries in China come with extra demands, Sun says. “Part of the package is linked to periodic assessments, and if the required targets are not met, remuneration in the following period can be reduced substantially.” He says a few of his colleagues have had their pay docked this year, after their university introduced more-stringent assessment criteria. “Such changes can have real consequences for staff retention, and have prompted academics to leave and take positions at other institutions,” he says.

Annual pay rises are not the global norm, the survey shows. Only 45% of respondents say they had received a raise in the previous 12 months, and 6% report a cut in pay. Industry researchers are more likely than the average result to have had a pay rise (54%), whereas the opposite is true for those who earn less than $50,000 a year (42% of whom received one).

Many survey-takers are frustrated that they aren’t getting paid more. One non-tenure-track US researcher who works in a biology department says that she exceeds her department’s tenure criteria and outperforms several of her tenure-track colleagues. Those colleagues are paid at least $20,000 more than her annually and get more research funding. “Yet my department chairs keep looking me dead in the eye and saying there’s no appetite for raises” for people like her, she says.

When salaries grow, it’s often not enough. One researcher with a fixed-term contract in Pune, India, says his public-sector salary has not kept up with “skyrocketing urban inflation” in the city of seven million people. He says that rent, public transport and groceries consume most of his pay. “At this salary, no one can even think of home or car loans,” he says. Add a child, he says, and “it’s just a matter of survival”.

Kyiv keeps issuing warnings that the satellite internet system Starlink is being restricted in Ukraine. How dependent is the country on the system, and what would happen if it went down?

Few technological systems have played such an important role in the war in Ukraine as Starlink, central to the communications, reconnaissance and drone operations of the Ukrainian armed forces. There are currently widespread concerns that Elon Musk's satellite internet service in Ukraine may be restricted or even switched off. In recent days, Kyiv has repeatedly reported disruptions and restrictions affecting its use.

Since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine has made extensive use of the satellite internet service, which is operated by the US company SpaceX. With it, military personell can continue to communicate and exchange data, even when infrastructure has been destroyed or cellphone networks are down.

According to the Center for Security Studies (CSS) at ETH Zurich, around 50,000 Starlink terminals were in use in Ukraine in 2025. These are the system's specialised receiving and transmitting devices, which enable communications on the battlefield. They are also used to relay orders between command levels and receive drone footage.

The importance of the Starlink network can in part be explained by events at the beginning of the war. According to a CSS analysis, the KA-SAT satellite system previously favored by Ukraine was targeted in a cyberattack just hours before Russia's invasion. Starlink quickly became a vital alternative.

When discussing current affairs, the terms "Starlink" and "Starshield" are often used interchangeably.

However, Starlink is SpaceX's commercial satellite internet service, whereas Starshield is specifically designed for governmental and military use. While based on the same technological infrastructure, it is designed for security and defense applications.

This distinction matters, because the latest reports of restrictions seem to primarily concern systems used for military purposes. Elon Musk recently stated that Starshield systems are controlled by the US government, whereas Starlink is a commercial service operated by SpaceX.

Alongside communications, Starlink is mainly used for drone operations. The system provides an internet connection for ground, naval and aerial drones.

This connection means video footage can be transmitted almost in real time, while drones can be controlled over long distances. Ukraine uses these systems for reconnaissance and attacks deep behind enemy lines.

According to ETH Zurich, images and GPS coordinates of Russian positions are also transmitted via these communication channels. The data is sent to artillery units and used to coordinate operations.

According to Ukrainian media reports, on Tuesday night, SpaceX temporarily introduced a speed restriction for Starlink terminals in Ukraine, limiting terminals in motion to a speed of about 160 kilometers per hour (100 mph).

Such a restriction has direct consequences for fast-moving Ukrainian drones, some of which travel at speeds of up to 300 kilometers per hour (186 mph). If the connection to the drone is lost, live video footage can no longer be transmitted, and the systems can no longer be controlled in real time. The drones effectively have to continue their mission while flying blind.

Restricting or even shutting down a Starlink network would therefore not only make communication more difficult. It would also affect Ukrainian drone operations, and other military activities.

However, Starlink and its military counterpart, Starshield, are only one essential component of Ukraine's defense system. Ukraine also receives reconnaissance data from western partners, including information about Russian air attacks, missile launches, troop movements and anti-aircraft positions, as well as potential routes to avoid them.

This information helps with air defense and offensive operations. Without this intelligence, it would be significantly harder for Ukraine to carry out long-range drone operations against targets in Russian territory.

The two elements are particularly effective when combined. Reconnaissance data provides information about potential targets and routes, while Starlink ensures that this data can be quickly relayed to units.

This is why reports that the US is limiting access to its reconnaissance data are as worrying as the possibility that Starlink might be throttled. Other NATO countries, such as the United Kingdom and France, can also provide reconnaissance data, but not to the extent as the United States.

And while relying on a single provider simplifies operations, it also shows how dependent modern armed forces have become on private technology companies. Experts say this marks a fundamental change in warfare.

#640. Is Germany's eight-hour workday under threat?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Would you work 12 hours straight? The CDU/CSU and SPD are locked in a dispute over reforms to the Working Hours Act. Workplace flexibility, collective bargaining agreements and worker protection rules are at stake.

The town of Leuna, in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, is one of the country's largest chemical industry hub, where more than 100 companies have established operations. Among them is the Finnish biotechnology group UPM, which has recently built a biorefinery for wood-based chemicals in Leuna. It's now hiring.

One of the job listings is for a chemical technician to oversee the rollout of new facilities. But to qualify, candidates must be willing to work 12-hour shifts. Is that legal? According to German law, standard working hours are limited to eight hours per day. It is permissible to extend this to ten hours, provided that workers receive compensatory time off for the overtime, and there must be at least 11 hours of rest between two shifts.

Business associations and the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) parties have long called for these rules to be made more flexible.

Rainer Dulger, president of the Confederation of German Employers' Associations (BDA), points to the European Working Time Directive, which allows for a maximum weekly working time of 48 hours. "The allowance is being taken advantage of almost everywhere else — except here," Dulger told the dpa news agency in August.

Companies say they often find the existing rules too rigid, particularly in industries with seasonal fluctuations — such as tourism — or in project-based work, or during irregular peaks in workload, such as in the restaurant industry. They could deploy their staff more efficiently, says Dulger: "The Working Hours Act dates back to the era of fax machines and rotary-dial phones. It knows nothing of laptops or daycare hours."

In their 2025 coalition agreement, the CDU/CSU and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) agreed to reform the Working Hours Act. "In keeping with the European Working Time Directive, we want to allow for a maximum weekly rather than daily working time — also and especially to promote a better work-life balance," the agreement said.

The SPD would have preferred to leave the law unchanged, but had to accept the conservative parties' plans. The Social Democrats pointed out that longer working hours are already permitted in exceptional cases, such as in emergency services, the health care sector and security services. Workers in power plants, refineries and the chemical industry are also allowed to work up to 12 hours at a time.

Even Chancellor Friedrich Merz himself seemed unaware of this exception until quite recently. After visiting the UPM refinery in Leuna in September, he recounted to business leaders at the German Employers' Day in Berlin that he was "completely surprised" to learn that the facilities operate on a continuous 12-hour shift schedule, thanks to the approval of the works council.

Merz recounted the shift supervisor describing the work schedule as "really great" — with three-day work weeks. "He said we have plenty of time for our families — it's a great way to organize things." Merz went on to conclude that this demonstrates the benefits of relaxing the Working Hours Act.

"Working more than eight hours is not exploitation. Rather, it can be a good opportunity to organize working hours more flexibly — both for businesses and for the people working there," the chancellor said. "We should do it."

But there is resistance from within the SPD and from trade unions opposed to amending the Working Hours Act. The trade union Verdi argues that significantly longer workdays would have negative consequences for employees' health and would disadvantage people with family and caregiving responsibilities. In certain industries, employees could also be put under pressure to accept longer shifts.

An initial reform draft was drawn up in June 2026 by the Labor and Social Affairs Ministry, headed at the time by Bärbel Bas, an SPD politician who has since resigned for personal reasons.

The draft includes some conditions, including a collective bargaining agreement between employers and unions if any schedule changes were made, as well as health and safety regulations, and working hours to be tracked electronically.

Annika Klose, spokesperson for labor and social policy for the SPD parliamentary group, explained the thinking behind the idea. Flexibility, she said, is "not a one-way street." The coalition agreement explicitly aims to promote a better balance between family and work. "So that it's sometimes possible to take your child to football practice in the afternoon and then continue working afterward — but not that my boss tells me against my will: 'You're working the shift from 9 a.m. to 10 p.m. tomorrow.'"

The CDU/CSU immediately rejected the draft. Businesses were also up in arms. "This draft must be withdrawn and completely revised," demanded Dulger in a statement, saying it was "steeped in mistrust." The German Confederation of Skilled Crafts (ZDH) spoke of a "breach of the commitments agreed to in the coalition agreement." The reform has been on hold ever since.

At the Employers' Day event, Merz held out the prospect of progress. "This is indeed long overdue. I believe we can accomplish this relatively quickly and create flexibility in the labor market."

It remains to be seen, however, whether the new SPD Labor Minister, Alexander Schweitzer, shares this view. In early October, his party released a paper on reforming working hours that read like a declaration of war against the CDU. "Self-determination, Not Deregulation" is the title of the seven-page document that was signed by the SPD working group on labor, the youth organization Jusos, the "60plus" working group and the women's wing of the SPD.

In the paper, the Social Democrats not only reiterate the points that already appeared in Bas' draft back in June. They also demand that, in the future, employers and employees should decide on working hours "jointly and by mutual agreement." They argued that it is not "in the spirit of a modern reform of working time law" if only employers can determine when employees finish work.

From a political perspective, the dispute over reforming work hours has long become more than just a debate over legal provisions. It is a fundamental dispute over how much flexibility workers should be expected to tolerate in the future and to what extent the state should protect them. And it is yet another stumbling block for the already beleaguered government in Berlin.
