Election officials reported record turnout as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s far-right coalition faced an opposition bloc led by a former military chief of staff, Gadi Eisenkot. A winner might not be immediately clear.

Aaron BoxermanDavid M. HalbfingerLeo Sands and Max Bearak

Polls have closed in an election that has seen Israelis vote in record numbers. In what is widely seen as the most consequential election in the country’s history, voters were deciding whether to keep Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his far-right coalition in power or chart a new course.

By 8 p.m. local time (2 p.m. Eastern), two hours before polls were scheduled to close, 74.3 percent of eligible voters had cast ballots, the highest rate for that point in the day since 1988.

Turnout appeared to be particularly high among Arab citizens, who make up about a fifth of Israel’s population. By late afternoon, hours before the polls were set to close, turnout in some Arab-majority towns had exceeded the totals from the last election, in 2022.

Responding to the high turnout in those towns, Mr. Netanyahu accused Arab citizens of “trying to steal the elections,” repeatedly using brazen and overtly racial appeals to push his right-wing base to vote. An attempt by his Likud party on Tuesday to have voting hours extended was rejected by the Central Election Committee.

The election is Israel’s first since the Hamas-led attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, which was the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust and ignited three years of war that left Gaza largely in ruins and nearly all its population displaced.

Voters have a choice between two blocs offering starkly different visions. Mr. Netanyahu, the leader of the most far-right government in Israeli history, built a coalition of nationalists and ultra-Orthodox Jews, which faces an ideologically diverse group of Jewish opposition parties loosely led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff whose son was killed in the Gaza war. Parties representing Arab citizens of Israel also staunchly oppose Mr. Netanyahu’s government.

Both sides are framing the election as a make-or-break moment for the country. Mr. Netanyahu’s coalition offers the promise of a more religious Israel with an ever-tighter grip over the West Bank and Gaza. His opponents are casting the vote as the last chance to preserve democracy and pull Israel back from the brink of becoming a pariah state under international sanctions.

Voting will close at 10 p.m. (4 p.m. Eastern time), but there is unlikely to be an immediate determination of who will be the next prime minister. Opinion polls suggest that Mr. Netanyahu’s allies and rivals are roughly tied, and assembling a governing coalition in Israel’s parliamentary system could take weeks or months.

Opposition leaders led by Mr. Eisenkot have called for a reckoning over what they call Mr. Netanyahu’s failure to prevent the catastrophic Oct. 7 attacks. They also have pledged to repair Israel’s shattered image abroad, including by cracking down on Israeli extremist violence against Palestinians in the occupied West Bank.

Mr. Netanyahu has resisted the establishment of an independent commission to investigate the Oct. 7 attacks. He has vowed to move ahead with overhauling Israel’s judicial system, a move that critics say could undermine the country’s liberal democracy. And his allies say they hope to permanently entrench Israeli rule in the West Bank and Gaza despite international condemnation.

The 77-year-old leader has dominated Israeli politics for decades. In recent days, he repeatedly suggested that the country could face annihilation without him in charge.

Mr. Netanyahu’s Likud party allied with far-right and ultra-Orthodox lawmakers to secure a clear majority in the Parliament, forming the most hard-line and religious conservative government in Israel’s history.

The coalition accounted for 48.4 percent of the vote but had a parliamentary majority because two anti-Netanyahu parties — one leftist, one Arab nationalist — fell just short of the share of votes needed to obtain seats.

It was a reminder of how tiny swings among voters can have a big effect under Israel’s parliamentary system.

Mr. Netanyahu has since overseen an acceleration of the country’s rightward shift, including an effort to overhaul the judiciary that prompted huge street protests. Questions about whether his government could have better pre-empted the Oct. 7 attack have also become hot-button issues.

In April, Mr. Lapid and Mr. Bennett said they would reconstitute their centrist alliance of 2021 for the 2026 election. Mr. Netanyahu will also face Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff who leads the largest opposition party.

Days before Tuesday’s pivotal Israeli election, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu shocked his country by offering a bleak economic forecast that critics said could make Israel more like Iran, Russia and North Korea.

The comments were a reflection of Israel’s deepening global isolation, with even once-staunch allies imposing or threatening economic sanctions and embargoes after years of war in Gaza and rampant violence by Jewish settlers against Palestinians in the occupied West Bank.

Opponents of Mr. Netanyahu’s government decried his pronouncements, which also drew a rebuke from the generally reserved central bank.

“Crypto currencies cannot serve as a route to bypass sanctions against Israel,” the Bank of Israel said in a statement. The bank added that the government would do better “to focus on preventing sanctions” and preserving Israel’s economic ties with its main trading partners.

Last year, amid growing global condemnation of the Israeli military’s conduct of the war against Hamas in Gaza, where it has been accused of war crimes, and the imposition of some arms embargoes, he warned that Israel would have to become a more self-sufficient “super-Sparta.” That reference to the ancient, insular Greek city-state that practiced militaristic self-reliance was, to critics, a frightening scenario for a small country whose economy is largely driven by a tech sector that is reliant on foreign investment.

Mr. Netanyahu later walked back his remarks slightly, saying that he had heard the criticism and that cryptocurrency was only one option to deal with potential financial sanctions.

In a somewhat jarring contrast, Mr. Netanyahu’s campaign has argued that Israel’s economy overall is healthy despite years of war, helped by record export revenues, relatively low inflation and unemployment, and a strong shekel.

But many Israelis are not feeling it. The State Comptroller, a government watchdog, warned last year that the cost of living was “skyrocketing” in part because of structural challenges in Israel’s consumer market and low competition levels.

Both Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc and the coalition of opposition parties are making a final push to urge Israelis to vote. Long waits at crowded polling places have been reported across the country, but the election committee has said anyone who is still in line at 10 p.m. will be able to vote.

The stakes of the Israeli elections are high for Palestinians in the West Bank, but they do not have the right to vote in them.

In interviews, some Palestinians voiced objections to not being allowed to vote, especially as they felt Israel’s occupation of the West Bank was becoming more permanent.

“The Israeli government has enormous influence over our lives,” said Montaser Amro, 32, a supermarket owner in Majd, a village near the southern city of Hebron. “It chooses when roadblocks open and close, it controls the borders, and it decides on how much of our economy functions.”

Israel captured the West Bank in 1967 and reached an agreement with the Palestine Liberation Organization to establish the Palestinian Authority in the 1990s. The Palestinian Authority administers parts of the West Bank and has not held parliamentary or presidential elections for roughly 20 years.

Many Palestinians have long hoped the Palestinian Authority would transform into an independent Palestinian state. In recent years, those hopes have dwindled. The Israeli government promotes the expansion of settlements in the West Bank that are widely considered illegal under international law and rejects Palestinian self-determination there and in Gaza. Palestinian officials have also failed to unify their ranks and renew their leadership.

The election in Israel on Tuesday is unlikely to yield quick answers about who might lead the next government.

Voters don’t directly pick a prime minister in Israel’s proportional parliamentary system. They choose a party or slate, the first step in determining who may be selected to try to form a government. This is considered one of the most consequential elections in Israel’s history, and voters turned out in record numbers.

Israeli election officials have said there is no evidence of threats to the integrity of this election in terms of widespread fraud or vote counting irregularities. But experts are concerned about disinformation campaigns undermining the vote or thwarting turnout.

Critics fear that some moves by the governing right-wing coalition may cast doubt on the outcome even before the vote happens or may thwart turnout among some voters.

Absentee voting is mostly prohibited in Israel. But by some estimates, tens of thousands of Israelis living abroad were expected to fly in to vote, a factor that could potentially swing the outcome.

Likud had filed a lawsuit challenging an initiative to fly in Israeli voters from abroad — a move widely perceived as an effort to limit opposition voters from showing up. The party had said the nonpartisan program provided benefits that could sway voters in violation of Israeli law. But it withdrew the suit this month after the initiative’s organizers agreed to limit flights and not to defray travelers’ costs.

Bertha, after all, is only guilty of doing what’s best for her daughter, raising her family’s clout, and preserving the Russell name as essential benefactors of the arts (that new opera house didn’t open itself) rather than greedy railroad tycoons with terminal severance packages. Suggesting anything else was, to be frank, imbecilic, but “The Gilded Age” played it out through the final shot, where a distraught Bertha watched her jealous husband ride off in a huff after informing her he wouldn’t be coming home until he “knows what he wants.”

That’s where Season 4 picks up: months later, but the mood unchanged. Bertha is on the other side of the ocean, caring for a very pregnant Gladys (Taissa Farmiga), while George is in the States, struggling to keep his business in the black. Elsewhere in New York, Peggy (Denée Benton) savors the success of her first novel while her fiancé, the nice doctor William (Jordan Donica), moves up in the medical ranks. Jack (Ben Ahlers) is getting comfortable in his new house, courtesy of his innovative alarm clocks, but he’s less comfortable with his new status (which requires etiquette classes from his servant friends) and his next invention (which isn’t as easy to sell as the first).

Everyone is moving up! Building careers! Making names for themselves! And then there’s Larry (Harry Richardson) and Marian, who bounce between tedious flirtations and equally tedious frustrations, almost like they’re two attractive twenty-somethings who desperately want to bone but can only get there by boring the pants off each other. Admittedly, Marian gestures toward finding a purpose beyond playing house by volunteering to help the less fortunate, but even Elizabeth Marvel, the program’s guiding hand, struggles to keep our aspiring housewife from dreaming of her sole, snoozy passion.

Luckily, Agnes (Christine Baranski) and Ada (Cynthia Nixon) are more than happy to spice things up: The latter falls for forbidden fruit, and the former hosts the President of the United States.

You read that right: the President of America agrees to have dinner at Agnes’ cozy Manhattan mansion, which provides the seasonal centerpiece “Gilded Age” fans are sure to be sick over: Who’s invited? What will be served? What will they wear? All these gossipy questions and more help structure the better part of Season 4, building ample anticipation for the historic evening by pitting as many of its invited guests against each other as possible in a steel-cage death match of affronted expressions and polite self-satisfaction.

The stakes are set as finely as the silverware, with bragging rights — for socialites and their servers — taking dramatic precedence over the fate of American rail travel, as it should be: In “The Gilded Age,” who saves face is always more substantial than whatever unnamed sums are secured or squandered, and the presence of POTUS does little to upend those pleasingly backward priorities.

Speaking of presence, Season 4 does feel the squeeze of its sprawling cast. It’s a little too light on downstairs character development, even if the dinner involves a few favorites and implements one outstanding plot device I shan’t spoil here. Some supporting players go underutilized (Jack as well as the scheming, wannabe power couple, Oscar and Enid), while unworthy regulars become story anchors entrusted with too much emotional weight. (By now, you surely know to whom I’m referring.)

Ah, so it goes in a series with an insatiable appetite for perpetual opulence and little tolerance for legitimate loss. Far be it from me to say what influences Fellowes, but he seems unwilling to repeat past mistakes or kill off a beloved character — any beloved character — too soon, if at all.

And that’s just fine. Really! We need more cheery, soapy TV filled with simple delights. “The Gilded Age” is the rare HBO Sunday-night staple with even-keeled ambitions and no extra-dour drama. It’s a frothy, let’s-leave-’em-smiling show that’s open to acknowledging serious issues — misogyny, bigotry, and oppression all inform the cast’s choices, to one extent or another — so long as they don’t linger long enough to spoil the vibe.

That’s why Bertha’s ascendance comes as such a relief. Nothing ruins a good time like misplaced sympathy, and siding with the man of the house in a series that’s more rooted in women’s perspectives with each passing season just feels wrong. What little deep thinking “The Gilded Age” invites, there’s something to be said for how it depicts women shaping a country that actively tries to operate outside of their reach. Bertha, with her new money and never-say-die demeanor, isn’t afraid to roll up her sleeves and do whatever it takes. Seeing her shine isn’t just good and right given her specific circumstances (re: George); it’s good and right because it bucks historical precedent (rah-rah, feminism!).

She grasps each situation perfectly — and she’s not going anywhere. Thank goodness.

Stephen King’s Carrie is one of those endlessly rebootable stories. Way back in 1974, King zeroed in on a near-perfect horror setup that explored being othered, the emotional and mental health consequences of bullying, and just how crappy being a teen girl can be in the modern world. Those themes—and the iconic visuals King conjured with his words—are evergreen, and resonate with audiences each time the story gets rebooted. Mike Flanagan has proved himself a master of horror and a clear successor to King’s legacy in the genre, and if you thought his Carrie reboot on Prime Video was terrific (and you’ve watched all the similar series out there), you should really watch Jennifer’s Body.

Carrie and Jennifer’s Body share a lot of themes—the corrosive pressure of high school, the casual cruelty of adolescents, the explosive power of feminine anger. While Jennifer Check (Megan Fox) is superficially presented as having more power and control than Carrie White (Summer H. Howell) because she’s more self-possessed (and played by Megan Fox), underneath the hair and makeup, Jennifer is just as naive and confused as Carrie. Both girls endure horrific experiences that set them on a path to horrific violence—and those experiences are symbolic of what women everywhere have to deal with all the damn time.

Just as in Carrie, Jennifer’s Body tracks a teen girl’s descent into monstrousness—but where Carrie leans into the tragic nature of those themes, Jennifer’s Body offers a funnier, more sarcastic take buoyed by Diablo Cody’s sharp dialogue and laugh-out-loud moments. The series highlights Carrie’s suffocating home life that offers her no relief from the grinding hell of being a 16-year-old girl who doesn’t quite fit the expected physical or behavioral norms imposed on teen girls, tracing a slow boil of rage. Jennifer’s Body depicts a character who ultimately chooses the dark path, leaning into her newfound destructive power, and has more fun with it, making it the perfect complement to the series.

Although the stories take different paths to get there, they both wind up at a cathartic and bloody climax centered on a school dance, where a lot of people die and chaos reigns. The film is the perfect way to examine, from a different angle, the themes that Carrie explores. Stream Jennifer’s Body on Hulu or rent it on Prime Video.

Looking for more stories about angry teen girls teaching society a terrifying lesson? Here are a few more films that make a perfect pairing with Carrie.

It’s been 50 years since Brian De Palma adapted King’s novel for the first time, and it’s still worth watching today, especially if you enjoyed Flanagan’s modern take. What could have been a trashy horror movie designed to cash in on the book’s success is instead a well-crafted film that makes every character feel real and consequential, and Sissy Spacek’s performance as Carrie is screen-meltingly amazing. Few horror films have aged this well, and watching a different creator’s vision of the story will be well worth your time. Rent Carrie on Prime Video.

If Carrie’s cathartic presentation of female rage and teen angst is what hooked you, the most relevant movie to jump to is The Craft. The story of four high school girls who harness dark powers to take revenge on, well, high school life itself is anchored by four feral performances from Robin Tunney, Fairuza Balk, Neve Campbell, and Rachel True. Like Jennifer’s Body, the teen protagonists enthusiastically embrace their power in a way that Carrie White doesn’t, but it transforms (and destroys them) just the same. Rent The Craft on Prime Video.

One reason Carrie is terrifying in all its forms? The very idea of a child with an imperfect grasp of ethics and an immature emotional state having thermonuclear-level psychic powers. After all, teenage girls without powers can be terrifying when triggered. The Innocents focuses on sisters Ida (Rakel Lenora Petersen Fløttum) and Anna (Alva Brynsmo Ramstad), who meet Ben (Sam Ashraf) and Aisha (Mina Yasmin Bremseth Asheim), who both demonstrate powers including telekinesis and mind control. Their interaction soon reveals that Anna also has mental powers, and the four begin working to enhance their capabilities—but when one of them begins using their powers to hurt others and seek revenge, things take a dark turn that fans of Carrie will appreciate. Stream The Innocents on Kanopy or rent it on Prime Video.

Anyone who was ever bullied in the teen years (so ... everyone) knows that those wounds never quite heal. That’s one aspect of Carrie’s power, and it’s also what makes Sissy an ideal movie to watch when you’ve finished the series. A mental health influencer, Cecilia (Aisha Dee)—once known as “Sissy”—runs into her old best friend, Emma (Hannah Barlow) and is unexpectedly invited to Emma’s bachelorette weekend—only to run into her childhood tormentor, Alex (Emily De Margheriti). The old trauma hits Cecilia like a bomb, shattering her fragile mental balance and leading to a surprisingly funny series of murders as Sissy works through her lingering anger issues. Stream Sissy on Hoopla or Tubi, or rent it on Prime Video.

If you’re a Mike Flanagan and Stephen King superfan, the obvious movie choice is their other collab, Doctor Sleep. A sequel to one of the most famous horror stories of all time (The Shining), the story finds a middle-aged Danny Torrance (Ewan McGregor) struggling to control his psychic powers, and leading the fight against an ancient group of entities that feed off of people like him. If you want more of that Flanagan/King magic, this is the right move. Rent Doctor Sleep on Prime Video.
