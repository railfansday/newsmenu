#634. Dental implants can hijack your immune system, scientists discover

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For tens of millions of people, dental implants have provided a way to replace missing teeth with a full set of fixed, functional teeth rather than removable dentures. However, an estimated 10% to 20% of implant patients eventually develop peri-implantitis, a serious infection that damages the surrounding jawbone.

Antibiotics frequently fail to control this condition, and scientists have struggled to understand why. Now, researchers believe they have uncovered the mechanism responsible.

A new study published in PNAS Nexus by scientists at the Rutgers School of Dental Medicine reveals that bacteria can gradually corrode dental implants, releasing microscopic titanium particles into nearby tissues. These particles interfere with the immune cells responsible for fighting infection, trapping them in a state of excessive inflammation that progressively destroys jawbone.

The researchers examined human tissue samples, studied human immune cells in the laboratory, and conducted experiments using genetically engineered mice. Their investigation identified a calcium channel in macrophages, the immune cells that engulf bacteria, that becomes activated by titanium particles. When this channel was disabled in mice, the disease was prevented.

This discovery provides the first credible drug target for a condition that affects as many as one in five dental implant recipients and costs healthcare systems worldwide more than $1 billion annually.

"For the first time, we show why all the antibiotic treatments that work around teeth do not work around implants," said Georgios Kotsakis, senior author of the study and assistant dean for clinical research at Rutgers School of Dental Medicine. "Now that we know the cause, we can start developing therapeutics."

Why Dental Implant Infections Resist Antibiotics

One of the biggest mysteries surrounding peri-implantitis has been its similarity to periodontitis, the gum disease that develops around natural teeth. Both conditions begin with the same types of bacteria found in the mouth, yet they respond very differently to treatment.

For natural teeth, antibiotics combined with routine professional cleaning can resolve the infection. Around dental implants, however, the same medications are successful in fewer than half of cases. Even when treatment is attempted, the underlying jawbone may continue to deteriorate.

For the past two decades, scientists have largely concentrated on the bacteria responsible for these infections. Researchers in Kotsakis' laboratory decided to investigate another possible contributor: the implant material itself.

Their work showed that bacteria accumulating on implant surfaces create acidic biofilms that slowly damage the titanium. Over time, this corrosion can release billions of microscopic metal particles, each smaller than a red blood cell.

Titanium particles can also be dislodged during routine dental cleaning. This is particularly concerning when instruments designed for natural teeth are used on implants, because they can scrape and damage the metal surface.

How Titanium Particles Hijack the Immune System

Once released into the gums, the tiny titanium fragments attract a bacterial toxin known as lipopolysaccharide, which coats their surfaces. This combination creates a deceptive target for the immune system, making the particles resemble unusually large bacteria that cannot be broken down.

Macrophages, the white blood cells responsible for engulfing and destroying harmful microorganisms, attempt to consume these particles. But because the cells cannot digest titanium, they become stuck in a prolonged state of excessive inflammation.

As a result, the macrophages produce large quantities of inflammatory signaling molecules, including interleukin-1 beta. This protein is also associated with inflammatory processes involved in rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease.

The resulting immune response gradually breaks down the bone supporting the dental implant. At the same time, the macrophages become less effective at fighting the bacteria that triggered the infection in the first place.

Laboratory experiments revealed just how severely their defenses were disrupted. Macrophages exposed to titanium particles engulfed fewer than half as many bacteria as immune cells that had not encountered the particles.

"These particles are little magnets that attract the bacterial toxin, and they hijack the immune system, preventing it from clearing bacteria," said Kotsakis. "You have a perfect storm that defies antibiotics."

Scientists Identify a Potential New Treatment Target

After uncovering how titanium particles disrupt immune defenses, the researchers investigated the molecular pathway responsible for the damaging reaction.

They traced the process to TRPC1, a calcium channel (a specialized, pore-forming protein structure within cell membranes) found in macrophages. This channel plays a central role in the inflammatory response triggered by titanium particles and bacterial toxins.

To test its importance, the team used genetically engineered mice that lacked TRPC1. When these animals were exposed to the same combination of titanium particles and bacteria, their immune systems responded much more effectively.

The mice developed dramatically smaller abscesses, produced fewer inflammatory signaling molecules, and regained the ability to clear bacteria. Disabling the channel prevented the destructive disease process observed when TRPC1 was present.

The results point toward a potentially new approach to treating peri-implantitis by targeting the immune system response rather than relying exclusively on antibiotics.

With funding from the National Institutes of Health, Kotsakis and his colleagues are now evaluating potential drugs that act on the same molecular pathway in human cells.

Why Proper Dental Implant Cleaning Matters

Although the research opens the door to future therapies, it also reinforces an important preventive measure for people who already have dental implants.

Regular professional cleaning remains the strongest known protection against peri-implantitis. However, the instruments and techniques used during those appointments can make a significant difference.

Until approximately a decade ago, many dentists routinely cleaned implants with the same metal scalers used to remove deposits from natural teeth. Research by the Rutgers team and other scientists has since demonstrated that these tools can damage and corrode titanium surfaces, potentially releasing additional particles and accelerating the disease.

Today, nonabrasive cleaning methods have become standard practice, helping protect implant surfaces while reducing the risk of complications.

By revealing how corroded titanium can interfere with immune defenses, the findings offer a new explanation for why some dental implant infections are so difficult to treat and identify a promising direction for developing more effective therapies.

#2142. How John Murtough will supercharge Birmingham City's Premier League mission: Cutting out expensive mistakes, elite contacts book and academy revolution

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 11:11 GMT, 23 October 2026 | Updated: 23:18 GMT, 23 October 2026

If anyone thought Birmingham were scaling back their ambitions after failing to reach the Premier League last season, they should think again today.

As exclusively revealed by Daily Mail Sport, Blues have appointed former Manchester United transfer chief John Murtough as their new director of football.

The remit is wide and yet narrow. He will oversee every area of the club but when the 55-year-old was approached for the job, the priority will have been clear – help us reach the Premier League.

Daily Mail Sport looks at why Blues went for Murtough and what he will be expected to deliver at St Andrew’s...

Murtough was director of football for three years at Old Trafford and left in April 2024, soon after Sir Jim Ratcliffe took control. He had also been United’s academy manager and director of development.

Knighthead’s plan for Blues is predicated on reaching the Premier League. They have invested heavily in transfers but not always made the right calls, as the summer 2025 recruitment drive proved. High wages were paid to players like Kyogo Furuhashi, who had little impact and left after a year.

John Murtough - Birmingham City's new director of football - is pictured with Erik ten Hag at his Manchester United unveiling

Blues have made some costly mistakes in the transfer window in recent years - including signing Kyogo Furuhashi (right)

During 11 years at Old Trafford, Murtough became known as ‘The Fixer’ and was instrumental in the appointment of Erik ten Hag. While the Dutchman divided opinion, he did deliver the FA Cup and the Carabao Cup – no small achievements in the post-Sir Alex Ferguson era.

Murtough was a driving force behind the women’s team at United. He also overhauled the approach to academy recruitment to target some of the best age-group players overseas. One of those who arrived on his watch was Alejandro Garnacho, who scored 26 goals and had 22 assists in 144 games and was sold to Chelsea for £40million.

Even though Murtough was criticised when players like Antony and Rasmus Hojlund struggled, there are those at United who feel his influence has been missed since his departure.

Birmingham hope to enjoy the fruits of that now. When Knighthead took control three years ago, they spoke of hiring ‘exceptional leaders’. The Americans are convinced Murtough fits the bill.

Nobody would doubt Craig Gardner’s commitment or work ethic, yet his approach to recruitment could be haphazard at times.

Blues are convinced that will change under Murtough. The summer window was overseen by the impressive Joe Carnall, whose reputation in the game is growing, and he would be a strong lieutenant to Murtough.

Carnall is an expert in coding and analytics tools. There are numerous metrics within Blues’ data model, most of which are guarded closely to ensure rivals do not gain a competitive edge. But no player will be signed unless he clears the data checks.

In recent times, Blues under Knighthead have insisted any transfer target should have up to three seasons’ senior football under his belt. They have also prioritised durability and have prioritised players in their mid-20s, although Colombian midfielder Jhon Solis is a notable exception. Solis has only just turned 22 but is already worth at least double the £6.5m Blues paid Girona for him last summer.

It will be interesting to see whether Murtough tries to alter this. Blues believe his vast experience and long list of contacts across the global game will open doors for them. Though Gardner did a fine job as technical director and was admired by Knighthead, Murtough is the kind of guy who can make football’s big players pick up the phone.

Jhon Solis is an exception to Birmingham's transfer rule of recent seasons - but already worth far more than Blues paid for him

Birmingham chiefs will hope there are more good times ahead with Murtough in the boardroom

Birmingham's youth set-up has been one of the top priorities for Knighthead since they took charge. After years of neglect by previous ownership groups, the Americans feel they have been playing catch-up for too long.

While Aston Villa have invested heavily in their academy and West Bromwich Albion’s is admired across the country, Blues have struggled. Blues’ academy was awarded category one status in May 2025 – a significant step forward – but attracting top talent across the region and beyond has still been a challenge.

Of course, Birmingham did produce Jude Bellingham, possibly the finest English footballer of his generation and one of the best on the planet. His younger brother, Jobe, and Jordan James, also made the first team before moving on but Blues want more.

If anyone can rectify that, it is Murtough. During his first spell with Everton’s academy, Wayne Rooney advanced to the first team. He also helped shape the system that delivered Jack Rodwell and Ross Barkley to the senior side. At United, Kobbie Mainoo progressed from a nine-year-old hopeful to a Premier League starter while Murtough was in place. Anthony Elanga and Garnacho also had useful United careers.

In the era of strict financial rules, developing homegrown players is essential – even if they do not establish themselves in the first team. As unromantic as it is, these players can be sold for pure profit with the money reinvested into recruitment.

The best place to implement such plans is the Premier League. Along with boss Chris Davies and his squad, Birmingham are counting on Murtough to take them there.

A flaw in PHP triggered a worldwide scramble to patch vulnerable websites, and now, further instructions have been issued by the PHP Project to prevent “an unlikely Remote Code Execution (RCE) vector” in PHP2Shell attacks.

Also: Chrome warns you about suspicious websites now

PHP2Shell refers to a pair of PHP vulnerabilities, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, that have each earned critical severity scores of 9.2 and impact most PHP builds. The PHP Project released patches to fix these bugs on Wednesday, and the critical nature of these flaws prompted a worldwide scramble to apply PHP’s fixes as quickly as possible.

Why the rush? Because PHP (a recursive acronym for Hypertext Preprocessor) is a general-purpose programming language that provides the backbone for many of the websites and services we access daily — everything from WordPress websites to e-commerce platforms.

According to Wiz’s security team, which discovered the vulnerabilities with the help of AI, PHP can be found on 70% of the world’s websites.

“These vulnerabilities include memory disclosure and unauthenticated remote code execution in PHP itself, independent of the application running on top of it, putting every affected website in immediate risk,” Wiz says.

Armed with these vulnerabilities and targeting an unpatched system, attackers could execute code on affected servers or read sensitive process memory, stealing data and infiltrating corporate networks in the process.

Also: AI a ‘force multiplier’ for low-skilled threat actors

Wiz goes on to make an interesting point: While AI assisted in the discovery of these vulnerabilities, AI could also be used to exploit them. Developing an attack chain and demonstrating the exploitability of these bugs “required an exceptionally complex chain of steps,” according to the researchers, which would be “at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve.” However, frontier AI models “substantially reduced that barrier, turning a longstanding flaw into an exploit.”

According to Amazon Threat Intelligence, attempts to exploit P2P2Shell were already happening four days before the vulnerabilities were made public. Proof-of-concept works started appearing hours later. A threat actor tied to P2P2Shell attack chains is attempting to deploy a Mirai-style botnet, and Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince says that the Cloudflare network is now recording “millions” of exploitation attempts.

In an advisory published October 23, PHP Project urged organizations to do the following: set session.upload_progress.enabled=0 until the next round of scheduled bug fixes in PHP. While already listed as a mitigation in the October 21 release, now that there has been time to investigate PHP2Shell further, applying this mitigation can protect against a different Remote Code Execution (RCE) vector.

Because this RCE, now recorded as CVE-2026-115560, requires an unusual configuration and application code setup, the attack vector is considered “unlikely”; therefore, mitigation will suffice until it is handled by PHP Project in the regular release cycle.

“An application must set session.serialize_handler to php_binary or php_serialize in its configuration and then switch it to php at runtime with ini_set() before calling session_start(),” PHP Project said in its advisory. “Session upload progress must also still be enabled. Applications that never change the serialize handler at runtime can keep the feature enabled.”

PHP Project is urging users to act immediately by updating to PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 or 8.2.35. If it isn’t possible to patch immediately, organizations can implement mitigations included in its advisory.

The National Security Agency (NSA) advised “heightened awareness” of PHP2Shell over the coming weeks, saying that any and all vendor-issued patches tied to PHP2Shell should be prioritized. Companies should also consider applying mitigative steps, such as enhanced logging and replacing end-of-life PHP packages, when appropriate.

“All users should immediately conduct an inventory, where feasible, of hardware and software services reliant on now-vulnerable PHP versions,” the agency added. “Vigilance in applying patches related to those systems is paramount.”

Speaking to ZDNET, Matthew Weier O’Phinney, principal product manager of Perforce Zend and OpenLogic, said that most open source software and commercial LTS software distributions had patches available either at the time of the coordinated release or within a few hours following, and if you’re using PHP, it’s likely a fix is available to you now.

“Assuming you use one of those, using your system package manager to update is a quick remedy,” O’Phinney added.

#5553. Overhead wired charging comes to Austin electric bus fleet

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CapMetro’s Austin transit fleets electrified two public bus lines and opened two new park-and-ride facilities featuring overhead pantograph-style wired charging equipment to help Austinites breathe a little bit easier.

Originally announced in June, CapMetro’s two new park-and-ride stations are now open. Dubbed the Goodnight Ranch and Expo Center facilities debuted alongside the agency’s Rapid 800 and 837 electric bus routes, adding upgraded passenger amenities and end-of-line charging infrastructure to support its growing fleet of electric transit buses.

At CapMetro’s main bus depot, infrastructure partner Camber Charging supplied and installed two 1,440 kW charging systems connected to 48 industrial dispensers. The installation provides 2.9 MW of total charging capacity, and is designed to support overnight charging for up to 48 electric buses simultaneously.

The new Goodnight Ranch and Expo Center terminals extend that infrastructure along the Rapid 800 and 837 routes with the overhead pantograph chargers that are the big news here, allowing buses to top off their batteries at the end of each line and eliminate any lingering range anxiety for the trip back.

“Instead of a typical bus holding 8 hours of charging, on-route charging takes that out to 20 to 22 hours,” explains CapMetro Chief Operations Officer and Executive VP, Andy Skabowski. “Every time that pantograph drops, we charge that bus. That’s what makes a commitment like (every) 10- to 15-minute service possible.”

The buses themselves are built on Canadian manufacturer New Flyer’s Xcelsior battery-electric transit platform (battery specs aren’t included in the release, but New Flyer claims up to 660 kWh of available capacity). And, with up to 320 all-electric hp with all its torque available at 0 rpm, the city’s 48 electric buses will have no problem navigating Austin’s stop-and-go traffic.

This seems like a logical cost effective solution. In theory, this should enable transit systems to purchase busses with smaller batteries which would surely offset the cost of the new charging infrastructure.

Camber Charging provides preventive maintenance, charger and energy management software and remote diagnostics across all three locations.

#4723. World’s largest wildlife crossing nears completion in California

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 11:03 AM EDT

California is known for its giant, multi-laned highways bustling under bright blue skies. And while this infrastructure is useful to humans, the Golden State’s many highways represent an almost impenetrable barrier for wildlife.

Soon, habitats on either side of the 10-laned 101 Freeway in Agoura Hills, California will be reconnected thanks to the world’s largest wildlife crossing, according to the Los Angeles Times. The Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing is scheduled for completion on December 2.

Wildlife crossings are structures that help animals safely cross roads. In the United States, collisions between motorists and large animals take place more than a million times a year. Experts estimate that this results in 400 human deaths every year. But the problem isn’t just road accidents. Habitat fragmentation is also a concern, especially for species that require a large range. Inbreeding can occur if a small enough group of animals becomes isolated.

In the Los Angeles area, mountain lions (Puma concolor), also known as pumas or cougars, are currently the species most vulnerable to genetic isolation caused by roads and development, according to the project’s website. And the 101 Freeway appears to be the most important obstacle, out of all the roads in the area, to the region’s ecological health.

As such, the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing aims to reconnect local plants and animals in the ecosystem of the Santa Monica Mountains.  It’s about 210 feet long and about 170 to 174 feet wide. A thick, sturdy-looking vegetation-covered bridge already straddles the busy freeway—but the weather might throw a wrench in the project’s schedule.

“We’re just hoping El Niño‘s on our side,” Beth Pratt, president of the Wildlife Crossing Fund, tells the Los Angeles Times, in reference to the climate pattern that typically brings wetter winter weather along the United States’ southern border. Flooding would put work on pause, according to the outlet. “I keep saying, ‘Mother Nature, we’re trying to help you. Let’s not throw any more complications our way.’”

#655. Breakthrough PTSD treatment achieves 68% remission rate in clinical trial

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new approach to treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has received FDA clearance, offering a personalized alternative for people who have not found sufficient relief through existing treatments. Developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, the technology uses brain activity measurements to guide targeted magnetic stimulation.

Early clinical trial findings have been encouraging. According to Wave Neuroscience, patients experienced a 52% reduction in PTSD symptoms, while 68% achieved remission. The full findings are still being prepared for public release, but researchers believe the technology could significantly expand treatment options for millions of people.

The need for more effective PTSD care is substantial. In the United States, approximately 17.5 veterans die by suicide each day, and about 7% of veterans receive a PTSD diagnosis annually.

PTSD can develop after traumatic experiences such as military combat, serious accidents, natural disasters, or violence. An estimated 6% of Americans will experience PTSD during their lifetime, with higher rates among women and veterans.

Why Current PTSD Treatments Are Not Enough

Since PTSD was formally recognized as a diagnosis in 1980, treatment has largely centered on psychotherapy and medications known as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including sertraline and fluoxetine.

Although these approaches can help, many patients continue to experience symptoms despite receiving treatment.

A 2020 review of PTSD medications found that patients reported improvements ranging from 6.8 to 10.1 points on symptom assessments. However, remission rates reached only 10.9% at most.

Wave Neuroscience reported substantially stronger preliminary outcomes in its clinical trial. Erik Won, DO, the company's chief medical officer, described the results as a major advance over previously reported medication outcomes.

“In comparison to that study, our FDA trial reported a 52% reduction in symptoms, so more than a four-fold increase in efficacy,” Won said. “And more importantly, we saw 68% remission in our clinical trial. So, it’s really a quantum change, an inflection point in what we can offer to patients. From a clinical care perspective, that’s really meaningful.”

These findings are promising, although the results come from different studies and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of treatment effectiveness.

The newly cleared system uses Magnetic e-Resonance Therapy (MeRT), a technology that combines measurements of brain activity with targeted magnetic stimulation.

First, an electroencephalogram (EEG) records the brain's electrical signals. This information helps guide a personalized treatment plan using transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS.

During TMS, a coil positioned against the scalp delivers magnetic pulses that stimulate brain activity. The technique has already been used for other psychiatric conditions, including treatment-resistant depression.

What distinguishes MeRT is its use of an AI-assisted algorithm to customize stimulation protocols based on each patient's brain activity rather than relying on a standardized approach.

“Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, was previously FDA cleared for treatment-resistant depression,” Won said. “By adding an augmented AI derived multivariate algorithm, we were able to personalize protocols that received FDA clearance for post-traumatic stress disorder, which should open up market access to about 13 million people who may be struggling with PTSD.”

Dr. Kenneth S. Ramos, a physician-scientist at Texas A&M Health with extensive experience in precision medicine, helped guide the research.

His work focuses on adapting medical care to the characteristics of individual patients instead of assuming that everyone will respond to the same treatment.

“Most clinical interventions for the treatment of PTSD do not work very well,” Ramos said. “So, this is a huge area where identifying interventions that could hopefully add quality of life to the people impacted by this disorder would be tremendously impactful.”

Wave Neuroscience CEO Fred Walke said Ramos was a natural fit for the project because of his expertise in personalized medicine.

Conventional TMS generally follows treatment protocols that are less individualized. MeRT builds on the same underlying technology but adjusts treatment according to the patient's specific brain activity.

Ramos emphasized that even small biological differences between people can have major implications for medical care. Humans are approximately 99.9% genetically alike, yet the remaining 0.1% contributes to differences that can influence how individuals respond to treatment.

By accounting for these differences and tailoring stimulation to each person's brain, researchers observed substantial reductions in PTSD symptom severity.

Ramos illustrated the distinction with an analogy involving a leaking roof.

He likened conventional TMS to covering a leak with a tarp to manage its effects. MeRT, in contrast, aims to provide a more targeted solution, comparable to identifying and replacing the damaged shingles responsible for the problem.

The broader goal is to move beyond treating patients with standardized protocols and toward interventions guided by measurable biological information.

From Breakthrough Designation to FDA Clearance

The regulatory process reached an important milestone in 2024, when MeRT received an FDA Breakthrough Device designation.

Dr. Rick Silva, executive director of clinical trials at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, explained that the designation helps promising technologies move through regulatory review more efficiently.

It does not eliminate scientific or medical requirements. Instead, it can streamline communication and reduce administrative delays while preserving the standards needed for FDA clearance.

Silva said the progress was particularly meaningful because the project began during the COVID-19 pandemic and advanced through the regulatory process unusually quickly.

According to Silva, medical device development and clinical evaluation can often take around 10 years before clearance is obtained. This project completed the relevant process in just over two years.

He credited the experience and dedication of the Wave Neuroscience team with helping make that accelerated progress possible.

“I’ve been involved in probably close to 1,000 projects in academic medical centers over the years at varying degrees of depth and detail,” Silva said. “Patients are why I do what I do. It’s hard, and the failure rate is high, and it’s a lot of work. But when you get to a point where something is going to benefit patients, it’s very professionally gratifying and personally gratifying in many ways as well.”

Dr. Israel Liberzon, distinguished professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at the Texas A&M Naresh K. Vashisht College of Medicine, served as the study's lead psychiatrist and PTSD specialist.

He believes the clearance represents an important expansion of the available treatments for PTSD.

According to Liberzon, MeRT will become the only FDA-cleared nonmedication treatment specifically for PTSD once it reaches patients.

“Right now, the FDA has approved only two medications for PTSD,” Liberzon said. “It doesn’t mean that only two of them are used, but both are SSRIs. This is the first time that in addition to the medication, we have a non-medication option. There is psychotherapy that people use for PTSD, but psychotherapies require a lot of effort. People must participate actively, and many drop out because it’s too anxiety provoking. So now we have a unique alternative.”

In the clinical trial, participants received five treatment sessions each week over a period of five to six weeks.

Researchers reported that the improvements appeared to persist after the treatment sessions ended, suggesting the benefits may extend beyond the active therapy period.

For patients who struggle with medication side effects or find psychotherapy difficult to complete, a treatment based on personalized brain stimulation could provide another option.

“People living with PTSD have been left with trial-and-error treatment, medications and their side effects, and limited outcomes—even when treated by the most dedicated and experienced physicians,” Won said. “Wave’s FDA clearance signals a fundamentally different future: one in which PTSD can be objectively evaluated, precisely treated and meaningfully healed.”

Won also believes the technology could eventually be adapted to other psychiatric conditions, including depression and anxiety.

Could Personalized Brain Therapy Transform Mental Healthcare?

Dr. Leslie S. Prichep, chief scientific officer at Wave Neuroscience, sees the FDA clearance as an important step toward treatment decisions guided by objective measurements of brain function.

“The FDA clearance of the MeRT System reflects decades of scientific research using electrophysiological biomarkers in identifying abnormal brain activity profiles and guiding personalized intervention strategies,” Prichep said. “We believe this represents an important advancement toward a more objective, data-informed model of mental healthcare.”

Walke believes that giving patients clearer information about their brain activity could also help change public attitudes toward mental illness.

He compared the idea to treating a broken bone with a cast. By making mental health conditions easier to understand through measurable physical information, the company hopes to reduce stigma and help patients better understand their experiences.

“One of the goals of the company is to create a platform for mental healthcare that uses objective imagery to help patients understand why they’re experiencing life the way that they are,” Walke said. “That insight, compounded with proven technologies, promises incredibly high efficacy when personalized.”

An important next step will be ensuring that patients can actually obtain the treatment. Wave Neuroscience and Texas A&M Health are working with other stakeholders to expand availability and make the therapy more affordable through insurance coverage.

“Our hope is that this research and technology can be a catalyst for more widespread development and innovation of more effective, personalized and precision-guided treatments,” Won said. “This is proof positive that population-scale personalized medicine is not just an aspirational goal but is an achievable reality today.”

The project combines years of work across medicine, neuroscience, clinical research, and technology, all directed toward improving the lives of people affected by PTSD.

For Ramos, the opportunity to help veterans carries particular significance because of Texas A&M's longstanding involvement in military and veteran initiatives.

“Texas A&M has a very rich history of military and veteran priorities,” Ramos said. “It’s a no-brainer that Texas A&M faculty would be interested in and supportive of an initiative that hopefully would bring positive solutions to a big problem. And anything we could do to help alleviate that problem is going to go a long way towards making a difference in the individual’s life as well as the people who are close to them. I feel very fortunate that we at Texas A&M were meaningfully involved in this project.”

‘I see him like a lighthouse, he will light (up) the others and improve the others, and make the team better. The way he talks, the ambition he has and the passion for the game is exactly what we need.’ These were the words of Mikel Arteta upon signing Declan Rice in the summer of 2023.

A few weeks ago, I wrote about how there had been a clear and obvious attempt to get Bukayo Saka into more optimal shooting positions. I deduce that having failed to secure any one of Rogers, Vinicius or Alvarez during the summer, Arteta and his staff had to consider how to juice more goals from the Arsenal orange with existing personnel.

Christos Tzolis was Arsenal’s only attacking signing and if he simply replaces the output of Trossard and Martinelli from last season, he will have been a wildly successful signing. I think getting Saka into more shooting positions is a simple case of banking on the team’s best attacker to do a little bit more.

When Arteta talked about Declan Rice no longer being a ‘lighthouse’, I think it is an acknowledgement of two things. 1- that Rice is being tasked with doing more in the final third this season and 2- a midfield of him, Bruno and Odegaard ought to operate more like a carousel where all three players are eights in the aggregate.

What I mean by this is that they are all capable of playing as sixes, eights and tens simultaneously within games. There was a move towards greater flexibility in midfield when Martin Zubimendi signed last summer. Whereas Thomas Partey and Jorginho were players who had to sit at the base of midfield for mobility reasons, Zubimendi and Rice offered the opportunity for both players to sit or push forward depending on the phase of play.

I think the signing of Bruno Guimaraes has furthered this sense that the midfield operates flexibly and the upshot is that Declan Rice is becoming far more creative this season. It’s not the first time that Arteta has asked Rice to be more impactful in the final third.

Towards the end of the 2024-25 season, with Arsenal suffering with attacking injuries and playing Mikel Merino upfront, Rice was asked to push forward more often. This season so far, Rice’s data is shape shifting yet again towards creativity and away from his more rumbustious qualities.

As you can see from the table (all data taken from Fotmob and expressed per 90 minutes), Rice’s creative statistics are all trending upwards compared to last season. He has four assists already this season. But as well as his creative data trending upwards, some of his more out of possession duties are reducing ever so slightly too.

In short, Rice is doing a little less scurrying and collecting and a little more passing and creating. I suspect the logic for this is the same as the clear decision to ask Saka to take up more shooting opportunities. Arsenal have to create more threat this season without having really improved their final third personnel in the market.

That being the case, Mikel Arteta has asked two of his best players to adapt their games in pursuit of that aim. It reminds me somewhat of that Simpsons episode where Marge finds a cheap Chanel dress and she keeps redesigning it so as not to let on to some rich friends that she cannot afford to buy another Chanel dress.

The signing of Bruno Guimaraes also represents another change for Rice, Partey and Jorginho were very similar players and that gave Rice a higher physical burden. The addition of Martin Zubimendi changed that somewhat and both players adopted a mix and match partnership whereby they would consistently swap between deeper and more advanced positions.

The rise to prominence of Myles Lewis-Skelly offers a very different and very useful variety in midfield too with his press resistance and ability to progress through dribbling. Bruno Guimaraes is a relatively similar player to Rice, capable of playing deeper and in more advanced areas, as well as being highly attuned to the physical demands of being a Premier League central midfielder.

We are yet to see exactly how a Rice, Odegaard and Bruno midfield will operate in practice; but we can see that Rice’s role has evolved yet again. It speaks to his quality and his intelligence- at West Ham the iteration of his partnership with Tomas Soucek evolved season on season too. Sometimes he would sit deeper and allow Soucek to push on and some seasons he would play in a more advanced position with Soucek behind him.

Rice has always shown a high aptitude for adding bows and whistles to his game season on season. Once again, he is being asked to do something a little bit different by Mikel Arteta and he is responding in kind. His capacity for learning and application is, for my money, unrivalled by any English midfielder I have seen in the last 25 years.

#4256. MIT scientists build a tiny robot that swims using living muscle cells

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A swimming robot does not necessarily need a bulky motor or powerful mechanical parts to move through water. MIT researchers have demonstrated that a single, carefully engineered layer of living muscle cells can generate enough force to propel a tiny robot forward.

The engineers developed an extremely thin swimming robot that uses living muscle tissue to move its fins. Described in a new study published in Advanced Functional Materials, the device has a flexible gel framework roughly the length and width of a stick of gum. The gel forms two fins, each coated with a layer of muscle cells far thinner than a human hair. These cells have been genetically modified to contract when exposed to light.

By directing light onto either fin, the researchers can trigger muscle contractions that make the fin flap and push the robot through water. Adjusting which fin receives light and how frequently it is illuminated allows the team to control both its speed and direction.

In experiments, the remarkably thin robot successfully swam and turned through a simple underwater maze. At maximum speed, it covered approximately four times its body length per minute. Although that is much slower than an Olympic swimmer, who can travel up to 65 body lengths per minute, it is comparable to the swimming pace of a cow shark moving leisurely through the ocean.

"It takes a lot of force to move through water versus air," says study author Ritu Raman, associate professor of mechanical engineering at MIT. "The robot's quite strong, given its size. "

A New Generation of Robots Powered by Living Muscle

The device represents the first demonstration of a very thin, two-dimensional robot that can move from place to place using living muscle tissue.

Most existing biohybrid robots rely on relatively large, three-dimensional structures made from laboratory-grown skeletal muscle. Producing these structures typically requires millions of cells. Raman explains that thinner designs could reduce manufacturing costs while potentially improving movement efficiency.

"Currently, biohybrid robots from our group and others' are built from bulky, 3D chunks of lab-grown skeletal muscle that require millions of cells to fabricate," says Raman, who notes that thinner, less bulky designs such as the team's new bot could be cheaper to build and could move more efficiently. "We believe that biohybrid robots powered by living muscle could one day perform delicate jobs like exploring environments too fragile or unpredictable for conventional hardware, because living tissue is soft, responsive to its surroundings, and can heal itself."

The MIT research team also included first author Maheera Bawa, along with Arielle Berman, Laura Schwendeman, Ferdows Afghah, and Seanbiron Johnson.

From Artificial Muscle to a Swimming Robot

The new swimming robot builds on an earlier achievement by Raman's laboratory. Last year, the researchers created an artificial muscle disk inspired by the human iris, demonstrating how a thin layer of living cells could produce precisely controlled movements.

For that project, the engineers patterned a gel disk with grooves extending outward from its center and forming concentric circles. They then placed living muscle cells on the surface, where the cells grew into a thin layer following the patterned channels.

When illuminated, the muscle tissue contracted in different directions, causing the disk to stretch and compress. Its movements resembled the way the iris of the human eye changes shape as the pupil expands or narrows.

The experiment established that researchers could grow extremely thin muscle layers in intricate arrangements and use light to control their movements along multiple directions.

"People hadn't seen this muscle architecture engineered from scratch before," Raman says. "And the cells were moving in multiple directions. But they only moved about 100 microns. From a robotics perspective, their movements were tiny."

For the latest project, the researchers wanted to dramatically increase the amount of movement and force generated by the muscle cells. Their goal was to produce contractions powerful enough to propel a robot through water.

They determined that achieving this would require a better foundation for the living muscle tissue.

The earlier artificial iris had used fibrin, an exceptionally soft gel, as its underlying structure. However, the team found that fibrin could quickly shrink under the forces exerted by contracting muscle cells, limiting the effectiveness of the resulting movement.

To overcome this problem, the engineers investigated three characteristics of the gel foundation: its chemical composition, its stiffness, and the dimensions and geometry of the grooves used to organize muscle growth.

"For engineering any type of tissue, it's known that these are knobs you can tune," Raman says. "And we wanted to optimize all these parameters to support live muscle cells."

Engineering a Stronger Muscle-Powered Robot

Finding the right supporting material required testing gels with different formulations, stiffness levels, and surface patterns.

The researchers compared several groove shapes, including narrow channels with square profiles and longer channels with curved bottoms. They discovered that muscle cells aligned more effectively inside square-shaped grooves.

This alignment was important because muscle cells that grow in the same direction are more likely to merge into fibers. Those fibers can then form stronger muscle tissue that contracts in a coordinated way. The square grooves therefore provided a more effective structure for producing force.

The team also replaced fibrin with gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), a material commonly used to grow and engineer biological tissue.

By adjusting the formulation of GelMA, the researchers produced supporting gels with varying degrees of stiffness. They then examined how muscle cells developed on each surface and measured their ability to generate force.

Stiffer gels produced the best results. On these firmer surfaces, muscle cells grew in better alignment and generated stronger contractions.

The engineers next investigated how thick the gel should be. They determined that a film measuring approximately half a millimeter offered an effective balance. It was sturdy enough to support a single layer of muscle cells while remaining sufficiently lightweight to prevent the contracting cells from detaching from the surface.

The team further improved muscle performance through a form of exercise. Repeated pulses of light stimulated the cells to contract, strengthening the muscle tissue before it was incorporated into the swimming robot.

With the gel structure optimized and the muscle cells strengthened, the researchers assembled their swimming device.

The finished robot consisted of a thin gel framework divided into two fins. Both sides of the gel were patterned with square-bottomed grooves and covered with living muscle cells. As the cells developed, they fused into organized muscle fibers capable of contracting together.

Each fin could be activated independently using light, giving the researchers a simple way to control the robot's movements.

"You can think of the robot as having two independent muscles," Raman says. "If we shine a light on just one, only that muscle moves. If shining on both, they both flap."

To test the device, the researchers placed it inside a large petri dish filled with water. They then moved a light source by hand, illuminating the fins to guide the robot through a maze positioned inside the dish.

As the muscles contracted, the fins flapped and propelled the robot through the water. By changing the light stimulation, the engineers successfully controlled its movement and helped it navigate the obstacles.

Although the robot demonstrated the ability to swim and steer, its current shape remains relatively simple. The primary objective of the experiment was to establish that an exceptionally thin layer of engineered muscle tissue could generate enough force for swimming.

The researchers now plan to refine the robot's body to improve its speed. Beyond demonstrating a new approach to biohybrid robotics, the technology could eventually be useful for monitoring aquatic environments or operating in locations where conventional rigid machines might cause damage.

"Our next goal is to optimize the body design to enable faster swimming," Raman says. "But even at slow swim speeds, one could imagine a muscle-powered swimmer being used for purposes like environmental monitoring in aquatic environments."

When mortgage rates climb, buying a home can start to feel like trying to hit a moving target. Every jump in rates makes monthly payments bigger, and that pushes a lot of buyers to look for another way in.

More and more of them are finding it in the adjustable-rate mortgage, or ARM.

Here’s how an ARM differs from a more traditional mortgage. With a regular fixed-rate mortgage, your rate stays the same for your whole loan. But with an ARM, your rate is locked for the first few years, then it can move up or down based on market conditions.

Some people don’t like that level of uncertainty – and that’s okay. But others are drawn to this option specifically because there’s usually a lower rate to start when you opt for an ARM. And in an affordability-strapped market, that’s going to gain popularity.

Bankrate recently found that, in September, rates for ARM products were over a full percentage point lower than a regular fixed-rate loan. Depending on the ARM product, that can make a difference of $150-300 in the monthly payment on a median-priced home.

And for some buyers, that’s enough to turn “not right now” into “let’s do this.” You can see it in the numbers. Data from the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA) shows that, in October, roughly 1 in 10 people applying for a mortgage chose an ARM (see graph below):

If you think you may want to consider this yourself, here’s what you need to know about how it works.

An ARM is like a phone or cable plan that has an introductory deal for new customers. You start out at a lower price, and then it adjusts after a specified amount of time. A popular loan is the 5/1 ARM.

The “5” means your introductory rate is locked for the first five years, and the “1” means that the rate can change every year after that. The rate change is called the “reset.” And here’s the really important part.

When your rate resets, your mortgage payment could go up or down. The good news is that most ARMs have caps, which are limits on how much your rate can rise each year and over the life of the loan. But either way, you have to be ready.

Before you decide, ask your lender to work up the numbers and walk you through the different potential scenarios you could run into with an ARM. That way, you go in with your eyes open.

And, if you do go with an adjustable-rate mortgage, just know that you’ll have choices when the reset time arrives. You could:

Do nothing and keep your loan in place if the new payment still fits your budget.

Refinance into a fixed-rate loan with a payment that never changes.

Refinance into another ARM with a new lower rate locked for a specified timeframe.

Talk to the loan servicer about assistance or modification.

Remember, a refinance is a brand-new loan that you’ll have to qualify for – even if it’s on a home you already own. So, keep tabs on your credit and debt levels and ask your loan officer how major financial moves like job changes and major purchases will affect your mortgage readiness. It’s important to keep your credit and finances in “mortgage shape.”

One more quick note. ARMs got a bad name after the financial crisis of 2008. Back then, there were some risky loans with small starting payments that later shot way up. Others let people borrow without proving how much money they earned.

But now, there are new laws to protect both borrowers and lenders. The biggest change is the Ability-to-Repay Rule, which says that before a lender makes a home loan, it has to check real paperwork (like pay stubs, W-2 forms, and tax returns) to make sure you can afford it. The lender also has to look at what your payment could be after the reset, not just the low starting payment.

So, you don’t have to worry that today’s ARMs are the same as the ones we saw back during the crash.

#4265. A tiny defect in a crystal reveals a big quantum surprise

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When light strikes a tiny imperfection inside a material such as diamond or silicon carbide, it can transfer energy to electrons and push them into an excited state. These imperfections, known as spin defects, could become valuable building blocks for future quantum technologies, including quantum sensors. Because they can function as stable qubits whose states can be read, scientists are eager to understand exactly how they behave. However, predicting how their energy changes over time has remained a major challenge.

Researchers at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering (UChicago PME) have now developed a computational method that provides a more complete picture of what happens when these defects interact with light. Their approach can simulate the entire optical cycle, including a particularly difficult process known as internal conversion.

The findings, published in Physical Review Letters, could help scientists design better materials for quantum technologies. The method may also prove useful for studying materials involved in renewable energy conversion and storage.

"This internal conversion process is not only relevant to spin defects -- it's also related to processes of interest to energy technologies," said Giulia Galli, the Liew Family Professor of Electronic Structure and Simulations in UChicago PME and the Department of Chemistry and the senior author of the study. "That's one of the reasons this work is so exciting."

When a laser excites a spin defect, the defect can return to its original, lower-energy state through several different processes. It might release a photon of light, transition between states with different spins, or transfer its energy into vibrations of the surrounding atoms.

That final pathway, called internal conversion, has been especially difficult for scientists to model accurately. Although the fundamental equations of quantum mechanics can describe the other two processes, calculating how energy is lost through atomic vibrations presents substantial computational obstacles.

"Although the equations are known, it's hard to describe the process accurately because of computational difficulties," said Stefano Villani, a UChicago PME postdoctoral researcher and the study's first author. "You need to account for complex interactions between the electrons and surrounding atoms, and that's challenging in realistic solids represented by models with hundreds or thousands of atoms"

To overcome this problem, Galli, Villani, and Yu Jin combined several computational techniques that their research group had developed over many years. Jin, a former graduate student in Galli's group, is now a postdoctoral scholar at the Flatiron Institute in New York.

The researchers used mathematical descriptions known as many-body wavefunctions to represent the excited states of spin defects. Through exact mathematical derivations, they determined how strongly each excited state interacts with the movements of nearby atoms.

This allowed them to calculate how individual electronic states connect with all the possible vibrational configurations of hundreds of surrounding atoms, rather than having to analyze those configurations individually.

"In the past, a clear mathematical derivation and efficient algorithms were not available," explained Yu Jin. "The new derivation came together after years of work on the optical cycle of spin defects."

The researchers incorporated their new framework into WEST, an open-source simulation program optimized for graphics processing units (GPUs). Galli's group has spent the past decade developing WEST through the Midwest Integrated Center for Computational Materials (MICCoM), which has received funding from the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Basic Energy Sciences since 2015.

A Surprising Discovery Inside Silicon Carbide

To test their approach, the researchers first examined a nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond, a type of crystal defect widely studied for quantum applications.

Their calculations closely matched recent spectroscopy measurements. For triplet states, internal conversion contributed relatively little to energy loss. In singlet states, however, it was the primary mechanism responsible for releasing energy. Both findings agreed with what scientists had previously expected.

The results changed when the team investigated a divacancy defect in silicon carbide.

Their calculations revealed that internal conversion has a much greater influence on the lifetime of the defect's excited triplet state than earlier models had accounted for. This previously underestimated contribution helped explain a long-standing disagreement between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

"We were expecting to see an effect, but of a smaller magnitude," Galli said. "What we found is that this internal conversion process turns out to be more important than we thought."

Understanding this unexpected energy-loss pathway could help scientists more accurately predict the behavior of silicon carbide defects and improve their potential use as qubits in future quantum devices.

A New Tool for Quantum Computing and Energy Research

With the framework now available through WEST, researchers at other institutions can begin applying it to their own studies. However, Galli noted that the simulations still demand considerable computing power.

The team is exploring ways to reduce those requirements, including the use of artificial intelligence.

"We are working on ways to integrate machine learning into excited states calculations to further improve the efficiency of our method," Villani said.

Meanwhile, the scientists are expanding their approach to investigate spin defects in silicon, as well as materials used in energy technologies where internal conversion influences how energy is transferred or lost.

By addressing one of the most difficult parts of modeling how spin defects respond to light, the researchers have made it possible to predict their behavior more completely and accurately.

"We provided the missing piece of the puzzle needed to completely describe the optical cycle of spin defects," Galli said. "Our work can now be used to make more accurate predictions about many materials of interest for quantum technologies," added Yu Jin.
