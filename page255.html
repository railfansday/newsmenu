#919. Your aging brain may be trading memory for something more valuable

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Growing older is often associated with memory loss and declining mental abilities. However, scientists are questioning whether these changes tell the whole story. Rather than simply becoming less capable, the brain may be adapting to a new stage of life in which accumulated knowledge, wisdom, and understanding become more important than remembering every detail.

Researchers at the University of Arizona have proposed a different way of understanding cognitive aging. Drawing on findings from molecular biology and brain imaging, they suggest that the changes occurring in older brains may reflect a natural stage of human development rather than deterioration alone. Their work, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, offers a new perspective on why thinking and memory change with age.

The researchers call their proposed explanation the adaptive aging hypothesis. It suggests that the brain gradually adjusts its priorities to make better use of a lifetime of accumulated experience. This could help explain why older adults often have valuable strengths in teaching, mentoring, storytelling, and passing knowledge to younger generations.

"If we look at normative aging as a story of just decline, we are really missing the boat," said study coauthor Fabian-Xosé Fernandez, an associate professor of cognition and neural systems at the University of Arizona. "The older brain is optimized for different things than the younger brain, predominantly the sharing of stored expertise and social relationships."

How the Brain Shifts From Memory to Meaning

Two brain regions play important roles in how people remember and interpret their experiences. The hippocampus helps preserve specific information about events, including their details and circumstances. The frontal cortex, meanwhile, is more involved in extracting broader meaning, recognizing patterns, and understanding the central message of an experience, sometimes called its "gist."

According to the researchers, the balance between these systems changes during healthy aging. As people grow older, the brain tends to place less emphasis on activity in the hippocampus and rely more on the frontal cortex. This shift may allow the brain to focus more on interpreting information through existing knowledge rather than recording every new experience in precise detail.

"The fact that you see this across people and animal models suggests there is an evolutionary driving force," Fernandez said. "One could argue you are developing your entire life, and development occurs in lockstep with the needs of your brain. When you're younger, the hippocampus turns on because you don't have a knowledge base or know how the world works. A kid absorbs information like a sponge because they are building an archive of information. In early life, the balance is tilted toward recollecting declarative memories with precision, detail, and context."

Young brains need to gather enormous amounts of new information because they are still building a foundation for understanding the world. By later adulthood, however, people have already developed extensive stores of memories, concepts, and experiences. Connecting new information to what they already know may become more useful than retaining every individual detail.

The researchers suggest that this changing balance reflects a shift in how the brain processes experience. Instead of concentrating primarily on collecting detailed memories, it increasingly emphasizes understanding relationships, identifying underlying meaning, and applying previously acquired knowledge.

Why Evolution May Favor Wisdom in Older Adults

Evidence of similar changes in animal models raises the possibility that this transition has an evolutionary explanation. Fernandez suggests that the aging brain may be adapting to help individuals preserve and share the knowledge they have acquired throughout their lives.

From this perspective, the ability to draw on decades of experience could be especially valuable for making judgments, guiding others, and transferring important information between generations. Older adults may therefore play a distinct role in supporting younger people through mentorship, teaching, and storytelling.

"Given those goals, it makes sense that you aren't interested in nitty-gritty details," Fernandez said. "You are interested in safeguarding the vast knowledge you already have. Evolution may be saying that if you make it long enough, there's a need to transmit this information, so we put into motion biological changes that enforce and protect your semantic and autobiographical memories."

Under this hypothesis, changes in memory and thinking are not necessarily signs that the brain is functioning less effectively. They may instead reflect a different set of priorities, with greater emphasis on preserving meaningful knowledge and using it to understand new situations.

Why Healthy Brain Aging Is Different From Alzheimer's

The adaptive aging hypothesis could also provide scientists with another way to examine the differences between normal cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease.

Although both involve changes in memory, Fernandez emphasizes that Alzheimer's should not be understood as ordinary aging taken to an extreme. The biological processes involved are fundamentally different.

"When I was in grad school, people thought about Alzheimer's as a more intense version of cognitive aging, but that is just not the case," Fernandez said. "They are completely different."

During typical aging, the brain's gradual shift away from relying heavily on the hippocampus and toward greater involvement of the frontal cortex generally unfolds between the fifth and seventh decades of life. In Alzheimer's disease, however, abnormal processes involving proteins such as amyloid and tau may interfere with this transition.

One important distinction involves activity in the hippocampus. While healthy aging is generally associated with reduced emphasis on this region, Alzheimer's can involve unusually high levels of hippocampal activity. Rather than supporting the normal progression of cognitive development, disease-related changes may disrupt the brain networks responsible for memory and understanding.

"In normative aging, hippocampal activation is deemphasized, which is probably what should be happening in a 70-year-old. But in many cases of Alzheimer's, it turns out the hippocampus is hyperactive," he said. "This beautiful developmental transition is completely disrupted in Alzheimer's disease by pathological processes that mess with episodic memory and interfere with circuits that allow you to think about your own life, lexicon, and vocabulary."

These disruptions can affect the ability to recall personal experiences and access stored knowledge, including words and their meanings. Understanding how these changes differ from the adaptations associated with healthy aging may help researchers develop a clearer picture of normal brain development and neurodegenerative disease.

Embracing the Hidden Strengths of the Aging Brain

The researchers argue that society may need to reconsider what successful brain aging looks like. Encouraging older adults to process information exactly as younger people do may not always be the most productive approach.

Fernandez suggests that placing excessive emphasis on remembering precise details could work against the brain's changing priorities. "For many individuals, the weight of how you process information should probably not be so hippocampal-centric by the sixth or seventh decade."

Instead of judging older adults primarily by abilities that tend to weaken with age, the researchers encourage greater recognition of the capabilities that may become especially valuable later in life.

Creating more opportunities for social interaction could be one way to support these strengths. Relationships between older and younger generations may be particularly beneficial because they provide opportunities to exchange experiences, share expertise, and preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost.

"People see older individuals and look at them as not being on par with younger individuals across a lot of metrics," Fernandez says. "The way I look at it is that you've got someone with a brain that has been active for several decades and has seen a lot. There is tremendous value there, and we don't do a good job of unlocking it."

The central message of the adaptive aging hypothesis is that growing older may involve more than a gradual reduction in cognitive abilities. Although some mental skills become less reliable with age, the brain may also be adapting to make better use of the knowledge and experience accumulated over a lifetime. Recognizing those strengths could change how society understands aging and the contributions older adults can make.

#4339. GTA 6 is so detailed no single Rockstar employee knows it all - which is how we got shrinking horse testicles in Red Dead Redemption 2

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Red Dead Redemption 2's infamous animated horse testicles, which are realistic enough to contract in cold weather and expand again in the warmth, were apparently not a feature mandated by Rockstar. As iconic as they've become - a symbol of extraordinary detail in the game - they didn't originate from a list of features that needed to be added to the game. The horse testicles were instead the unexpected work of one person, or a group of people, autonomously working on the Cowboy epic.

Journalist Jason Schreier heard this anecdote from Rockstar head of development Aaron Garbut while visiting Rockstar North to write an expansive report for Bloomberg about the making of GTA 6. "Garbut actually brought this up," Schreier said in a video on YouTube. "[The horse testicles were] not something that was on some big list of features to make; it was not something that an art director told an artist, 'Alright, we've got to get those horse testicles in here - better put in nights and weekends so you can get these horse testicles in here.'

"That was something that people, or a person on their own, autonomously decided, 'OK, I'm going to do this because I think it's a really cool detail and I have enough time to work on it." We're checking the veracity of this anecdote with Rockstar, by the way.

And that anecdote is important because it relates to another thing Schreier was told by Garbut, which was that no one person at Rockstar knows everything that's in GTA 6 because it's too big and detailed to do that. "There's no one person that has this whole thing in their head," Garbut apparently said.

That, coupled with the fact that developers apparently have "autonomy to put in things that are cool", Schreier said, means there could be another unexpected feature like horse testicles in GTA 6. And given the nudity-related GTA 6 video leaks we've already had, perhaps the features aren't that far removed.

Schreier's video revealed a few other tidbits of information learnt on his trip to Rockstar. He was told that roads in GTA 6 are "significantly narrower" than in GTA 5, apparently, so players feel the effects of traffic more - a detail that relates to the increased levels of realism in GTA 6.

He also heard how some artists had been working on GTA 6 for 11 years, and whereas there were "five of six" people in GTA 5's art production team, there were - or are - 65 people in GTA 6's art production team. Note that Rockstar, as a company, has roughly doubled in size since the release of 2018's Red Dead Redemption 2, growing from 2,441 employees to 4,711 across 12 offices.

There's also some information about the origins of the contextual 'hold left trigger' interaction system introduced in Red Dead Redemption 2, which again, apparently came from someone autonomously working on the game - Phil Hooker (now director of technology) - rather than from one of the game's creative leads. Also, many experiments were apparently done to find a replacement for GTA's radial menu but in the end, the team reverted back to it.

Grand Theft Auto 6 is, of course, releasing a month from now on 19th November. The road to launch has been bumpy, however, with Rockstar North currently embroiled in an employment tribunal in Scotland over the alleged unjust firings of 31 employees, and with a series of gameplay footage leaks exposing GTA 6 in ways that are outside of Rockstar's control.

#4909. New comet accidentally discovered by space telescope

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 20, 2026 11:15 AM EDT

Launched in 2023, the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Euclid space telescope observes some of the most distant regions of the visible universe for signs of dark matter and dark energy. But even while focused on the “big picture,” Euclid’s onboard equipment is surprising astronomers by capturing tiny photobombers like errant comets.

Its recent portraits of one specific dusty space rock are especially impressive, however. Euclid spotted a never-before-seen comet sailing through space more than 807.7 million miles from Earth. The telescope isn’t designed to automatically flag an image interloper. Instead, the bright blemish seen in multiple images was spotted by a moving object detection program designed by Mikko Pöntinen, an astronomer at the University of Helsinki in Finland.

“I was excited to see my software flag a ‘streak’ smeared across several images—the result of a fast-moving object,” said Pöntinen. “Further inspection revealed that it had a fuzzy cloud around it, indicative of an active object like a comet that is shedding dust and gas.”

Pöntinen quickly relayed his findings to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center (MPC), which then passed the news along to researchers around the world. On October 8, the MPC confirmed the newly discovered comet, which is now classified C/2026 S1 (Euclid).

C/2026 S1 was seen from five different fields of view over multiple days in the Euclid Deep Field North region of the telescope’s scans. Astronomers now know the comet’s trajectory isn’t located on the same horizontal plane as the planets. Instead, its path is technically taking it over the solar system along a multi-thousand-year orbit.

Euclid’s discovery is particularly striking because the telescope’s six-year mission is specifically mapped to avoid areas of the sky that are laden with comets and asteroids.

“Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid, so it was a happy coincidence,” added Euclid archive scientist Bruno Altieri.

#4604. ACOG Strongly Recommends Hormone Therapy for Menopause Symptoms

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published updated recommendations for menopause management in Obstetrics & Gynecology on October 15.

These expanded recommendations are the most comprehensive guidance to date. They include new clinical guidelines for menopause hormone therapy (MHT), nonhormonal medications, and nonpharmacological treatment of vasomotor symptoms (VMS), which include hot flashes and night sweats, and sleep and mood changes.

According to the ACOG guidance, 50%–92% of females experiencing menopause report VMS.

Around 16%–66% of females in the United States report menopause-related sleep issues, and more than one-third of perimenopausal and postmenopausal females report experiencing depressive symptoms.

“In recent years, misconceptions about the risks of hormone therapy have been widespread, which has made some clinicians reluctant to prescribe it and some patients hesitant to use it,” Bruce Pier, MD, clinical professor and associate fellowship director at the University of North Carolina and collaborator on the ACOG clinical practice guidelines, told Healthline.

“This guidance offers a comprehensive review of the evidence to support individualized counseling and shared decision making. Our hope is that it clears up those misconceptions and expands access to treatment.”

The ACOG is also developing companion guidance for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, expected in 2027.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of MHT with estrogen and progestogens (or estrogen alone for people without a uterus) for both VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance, in individuals who can use hormones.

It also conditionally recommends conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene (Duavee) as an alternative for these issues.

According to a meta-analysis from 2021 that was cited by the guidelines, estrogen therapy, with or without progestogens, significantly reduced VMS frequency and severity compared to a placebo or no treatment. A 2017 meta-analysis ranked transdermal estradiol plus a progestogen as the most effective option for reducing VMS frequency.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, also cited by the guidelines, found that MHT was associated with improved self-reported sleep quality. Once again, transdermal therapies showed some of the largest effects.

The ACOG recommends initiating MHT before age 60 or within 10 years of the onset of natural menopause..

Research shows that factors such as race, culture, socioeconomic status, gender identity, and sexual orientation can influence how individuals experience menopause and the care they receive.

According to the SWAN study, Black women reported more menopause symptoms than white women but were less likely to be treated for them.

“This updated document shines a light on these inequities. Clinicians can proactively help right now by asking every patient in midlife about symptoms, rather than waiting for them to bring it up. Long term, we hope this document drives more research funding to understand these gaps and begin closing them,” said Pier.

The ACOG stated in the guidelines that, “The available evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against the use of estrogen–progestogen or estrogen-only MHT for the treatment of menopause-related depressive symptoms.”

It recommends that perimenopausal and postmenopausal individuals who meet the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) or those whose most noticeable symptom is depression should be treated according to depression management guidelines.

Current treatment guidelines for depression include therapy, medication, or a combination of the two.

Psychological treatments for depression include psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), while antidepressants are among the most commonly prescribed medications.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) for the management of menopausal symptoms like VMS and depression in individuals who don’t want to use MHT or can’t use it.

Currently, paroxetine is the only antidepressant that is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of VMS.

The ACOG also strongly recommends the use of neurokinin receptor antagonists to manage VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbances in people who don’t desire hormone therapy or can’t use it.

There are currently two neurokinin receptor antagonists approved by the FDA to manage menopause symptoms. Fezolinetant (Veozah) was approved in 2023 to help manage hot flashes. In 2025, the FDA approved elinzanetant (LYNKUET) for the management of moderate to severe VMS due to menopause.

Another nonhormonal treatment for VMS that the ACOG conditionally recommends is gabapentin.

It lays out a range of safe, effective options for managing menopause symptoms. My message to anyone struggling with these symptoms is simple: talk to your clinician. Beginning Saturday, October 24, BLM will post photos of the competitors to their Facebook and Instagram accounts and ask you to vote for the best-looking one. All of the competing bats are part of wild populations that live on land managed by the public agency and are photographed by wildlife technicians. The winner will be announced (appropriately) on Saturday, October 31.

The bats in this competition are known for their squishy faces and clever names. In 2024, a hoary bat (Lasiurus cinereus) from Oregon named Hoary Potter took the crown. Other contestants included a long-eared myotis (Myotis evotis) named Honey Bunches of MyOtis, a myotis bat (Myotis myotis) named Robert Battinson, and western small-footed bats (Myotis ciliolabrum) named Batt Damon and Batlor Swift. The contest was not held in 2025 due to the 43-day-long federal government shutdown.

The contest is not all bat puns and a way to keep the spirit of Fat Bear Week alive. It falls during Bat Week (October 24-31). Bat Week raises awareness about the Earth’s only flying mammal. More than just a Halloween symbol, bats play a critical ecological role as plant pollinators and through eating insects that can threaten crops and forests around the world. According to Bat Week, one bat can eat up to its body weight in insects every night. Only three species of bats eat animal blood, with two specializing in bird blood. Female lesser long-nosed bats even make tequila possible. Every spring, they migrate from south-central Mexico up to the southwestern United States, following “nectar corridors” of columnar cacti and agave plants. They pollinate agave plants, which are then used to make tequila and mezcal. When they feed on cactus fruits and nectar, they may also help disperse cacti seeds. Other bats eat pollen, nectar, or fruit.

Bats are increasingly threatened due to habitat loss, light pollution, and diseases like white-nose syndrome. This fungal disease infects the skin of the muzzle, ears, and wings of hibernating bats. In about 20 years, it has killed millions of bats across North America and affects about half of the species on the continent. Bats also are often misunderstood as carriers of diseases such as rabies.

The planet is home to over 1,400 species of bats, and they make up almost 20 percent of all mammal species. Bats also can be found almost everywhere on Earth, even in desert and polar regions. To look up which bat species live near you, you can use Bat Week’s state bat database.

#931. Scientists find a way to fight Alzheimer’s brain damage without removing toxic tau

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Protecting brain cells from destruction remains one of the biggest challenges in treating Alzheimer's disease and related neurodegenerative conditions. These disorders, known as tauopathies, involve the buildup of abnormal tangles of a protein called tau inside the brain. As the tangles accumulate, nearby neurons begin to die. However, growing evidence suggests that tau itself may not be responsible for most of the damage. Instead, the immune system's reaction to the protein appears to play a major role in the loss of brain cells, memory, and independence.

Scientists at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis have now identified a promising strategy for interrupting this damaging immune response. In mice with tau accumulation similar to that seen in Alzheimer's, researchers used an antibody to block a protein called CXCR3, which helps immune cells known as T cells enter the brain. After several months of treatment, the number of T cells inside the brain had fallen by approximately half.

The results were striking. Compared with untreated mice, animals receiving the antibody retained roughly 40% more tissue in brain regions involved in memory and performed better on a memory test. These improvements occurred even though the amount of accumulated tau remained unchanged.

The findings were published September 28 in the journal Neuron.

"In tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease, there's no treatment right now that actually decreases neurodegeneration," said David M. Holtzman, MD, senior author of the study and the Barbara Burton and Reuben M. Morriss III Distinguished Professor in the Department of Neurology at WashU Medicine. "If we can show that we're really decreasing brain cell death, it's certainly worth trying to pursue that pathway to prevent the most devastating consequences of these diseases."

Why Immune Cells May Be a Key to Alzheimer's Brain Damage

Current Alzheimer's treatments primarily focus on removing amyloid, another protein associated with the disease. Two approved medications, lecanemab and donanemab, target amyloid plaques that develop between brain cells during the early stages of Alzheimer's. These deposits interfere with communication between neurons, contribute to the formation of tau tangles inside cells, and ultimately lead to neuronal death.

Although these medications can slow cognitive decline, they have not been shown to prevent brain cells from dying. They also offer no treatment for primary tauopathies, conditions in which tau accumulates without the presence of amyloid. Alzheimer's is classified as a secondary tauopathy because both abnormal amyloid and tau are present.

Holtzman's laboratory has been investigating whether the immune system offers another way to address these diseases. In a 2023 study published in Nature, the researchers discovered that T cells enter the brains of mice with abnormal tau accumulation, concentrating in regions containing the most tau and contributing to neurodegeneration.

A related study published earlier this month in Nature Neuroscience provided another important clue. It showed that the signals directing these T cells originate in lymph nodes located outside the brain.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

Scientists Identify the Pathway That Guides Immune Cells Into the Brain

Despite these earlier discoveries, researchers still needed to understand how activated T cells found their way into the brain.

T cells navigate through the body by following chemical signals known as chemokines. Earlier experiments in Holtzman's laboratory had identified unusually high levels of one such signal, CXCL10, in mice with tau accumulation. Other researchers had also reported elevated CXCL10 levels in people with Alzheimer's disease.

Activated T cells carry a surface receptor called CXCR3 that recognizes CXCL10, allowing the cells to follow its chemical signals. This relationship suggested that the CXCL10 and CXCR3 pathway might be responsible for guiding harmful T cells into the brain.

To investigate, the researchers studied mice that developed abnormal tau deposits resembling those associated with Alzheimer's. The team included co-senior author Jason Ulrich, PhD, a professor of neurology at WashU Medicine, and first author Joshua T. Emmerson, PhD, a postdoctoral researcher in Holtzman's laboratory.

The experiments revealed that T cells could not infiltrate the brain when mice lacked either CXCL10 or its matching receptor, CXCR3, on T cells. This protection persisted even when the scientists deliberately triggered inflammation to stimulate an immune response.

Experimental Treatment Preserves Brain Tissue and Memory

With the pathway identified, the team tested whether blocking CXCR3 with an antibody could reduce brain damage.

The researchers selected young mice that already had tau deposits in their brains but had not yet experienced substantial loss of brain cells. They administered the antibody once every five days for three and a half months.

By the end of the experiment, treated mice had approximately half as many T cells in their brains as untreated animals. They also showed less evidence of neuronal injury and retained substantially more brain tissue, including roughly 40% more tissue in memory-related regions. Their performance on a memory test was also better.

Importantly, these protective effects occurred without any detectable reduction in tau tangles. The treated and untreated mice had similar amounts of abnormal tau, suggesting that limiting the immune response could reduce neurodegeneration even when the underlying protein deposits remain.

Another finding could have major implications for future drug development. The antibody reached the boundary of the brain but did not enter the brain tissue itself.

This suggests that it may be possible to reduce neurodegeneration by targeting immune activity from outside the brain. Such an approach could avoid one of the major difficulties in developing neurological treatments: getting drugs across the blood-brain barrier, which prevents many substances from entering brain tissue.

A Potential New Direction for Alzheimer's Treatment

Although the results are encouraging, the treatment has so far been tested only in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine whether the approach is safe and effective enough to evaluate in people.

Holtzman suggested that medications already used to control T cells in other diseases could provide a starting point. Certain treatments for multiple sclerosis and other autoimmune conditions, in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue, might be worth investigating for their potential to reduce Alzheimer's-related brain damage.

This would represent a significant shift in treatment strategy. Rather than concentrating exclusively on the abnormal proteins associated with neurodegeneration, researchers could also target the immune responses that contribute to the destruction of neurons.

"Tauopathies aren't thought of as autoimmune disorders, so they haven't been treated the same way, but this study shows for the first time in an animal model that these diseases respond to a specific T-cell therapy," said Holtzman, who also directs WashU's Hope Center for Neurological Disorders and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center at WashU Medicine.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

The findings raise the possibility that protecting the brain from harmful immune activity, rather than removing tau itself, could become an important strategy for treating Alzheimer's and other tauopathies.
