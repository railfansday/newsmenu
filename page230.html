#4186. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 Review - Silver Bullet

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 arrives at a crossroads for the series, thanks in large part to the inconsistent quality of recent entries. With a shaky predecessor, Infinity Ward had a lot to repair and conclude, especially with 4 set to bookend the rebooted Modern Warfare series. Fortunately, backed up by a fun campaign, thrilling multiplayer, and loads of new modes and content, Modern Warfare 4 makes up for the past and then some.

Modern Warfare 4 continues after the events of 2023’s Modern Warfare 3, with Captain Price hellbent on stopping Makarov and his higher-ups. The story features shifting perspectives, from a new group of ragtag Korean soldiers to familiar characters like Gaz. The character dynamics harken back to the original trilogy, especially in the boots-on-the-ground missions with the South Korean unit, led by Park (played by Beef actor Young Mazino).

For a series that, at least recently, has been more spectacle than story, giving the stakes the time they deserve is nice. While it moves away from the stealthy and modernized elements, it makes sense for the matters at hand. The majority of Modern Warfare 4’s attempts at wrapping up the overarching plot of the rebooted series works, but it rushes through some character arcs and story beats in uneven fashion. Furthermore, much of the game’s cast, especially Mads Mikkelson’s Archer and Prisca Kim’s Ri Ju-Young, are underutilized, which is a shame since they both deliver great performances.

Despite the shortcomings of the campaign, Call of Duty's bread and butter, multiplayer, still delivers. While it doesn’t have the fluidity of Black Ops’ newer entries, the customization and design of weapons and maps make it worth digging into. From small, intimate shooting galleries with Mumbai and Nautical to wide free-for-alls like Coal and Reactor U92, the range helps maintain excitement, even if it gets repetitive over time.

The variety of multiplayer modes keeps things fresh on the battlefield. Returning modes like standard Team Deathmatch and Kill Confirmed continue that enjoyable competitive flair that made them the stars that they are. However, new modes like Combat Outpost and Hijack fit right into the already great lineup. In particular, Combat Outpost upscales your typical 6v6 player count to 24v24, leading to more action and teamwork. Hanging onto a team’s tank, shooting enemies from afar, and taking back outposts is exhilarating, even if it can all go bad moments later by a well-placed RPG shot.

Even after hours of gameplay, the variety of options to craft your perfect gun is impressive. Figuring out the best scope, ammo, stock, and muzzle, as well as the advantages and disadvantages of them, is a fun way of customizing your playstyle. Gunny, a mechanic that helps optimize your gun for certain styles, can help with decision paralysis, but I rarely used it. Not many FPSs go that far and Modern Warfare 4’s commitment to the feature makes it stand out.

The newest mode, DMZ, impresses out of the gate. After a few flat attempts in the past, this is the definitive Call of Duty extraction shooter, whether you play solo, or in duos or trios. Immediately, you’re thrust into the wide open world of Hajin and left to fend for yourselves with what you have and what you can gather and extract. Fans of Arc Raiders and Marathon should feel right at home as the gameplay borrows from the typical extraction shooter experience. Intense, action-packed moments keep you coming back after every win or loss. And the excellent gunplay and level design elevate it into something worthwhile, no matter how you play.

Despite not encountering many live players during my time in Hajin in DMZ, it still proves challenging, even for seasoned pros. The world is not your friend, and taking risks can come at a high cost. A good team, as well as a thoughtful loadout and traits, are required to survive. The unpredictability of it all keeps you on your toes, making for an exciting addition to the already strong multiplayer suite.

Regardless of the mode, Modern Warfare 4 is one of the best-looking Call of Duty titles to date. New York and Paris are lifelike, and it truly embodies the realistic, modern aesthetic the series has always aimed to achieve. However, I encountered a few technical hiccups, especially in the campaign. Slow framerate plagues some high-octane scenes and texture loading is frequent across the board. These distractions are far from game-breaking, but enough to be noticeable.

While the plot execution is a bit shaky, engaging multiplayer options, including DMZ, aptly demonstrate that Infinity Ward can still deliver compelling military-FPS experiences. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 is a strong return to form that serves as a solid sendoff for the rebooted subseries.

#5515. Optivolt Raises $32M to Build the Brain for Solar Panels of the Future

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Series A led by VXI Capital, with backing from the people who built modern solar, including founding Enphase CEO Paul Nahi, former SunPower CEO Tom Werner and former New York energy czar Richard Kauffman

Nine years in the making, Optivolt’s energy intelligence turns passive solar hardware into adaptive power systems by managing energy in real time from within the panel

San Francisco — Optivolt, a power electronics company building the intelligence layer for solar, yesterday announced a $32M Series A led by VXI Capital, a new hard tech fund founded by the former CTO of the Defense Innovation Unit of the U.S. Military, with participation from RA Capital, Valo Ventures, and Atlas Innovate. This round brings Optivolt’s total capital raised to date to $42M.

The solar panel has become a commodity. Optivolt gave it a brain. Its Power Balancer embeds active power electronics directly into the panel, managing energy among cell groups in real time to recover power otherwise lost to shade, weather, dirt and uneven degradation. This creates a foundation for adaptive, connected solar systems. With more than 20,000 panels incorporating the Power Balancer already shipped, the company will use the new financing to expand manufacturing and bring the technology to solar systems at every scale, from individual homes to utility-scale projects designed to help power AI data centers.

“Solar became cheap enough to compete. I founded Optivolt to make it intelligent enough to power the world,” said Rohit Kalyanpur, founder and CEO of Optivolt. “For nine years, we’ve rebuilt the solar panel from first principles, embedding aerospace-grade power electronics so it can respond to Earth as it actually is, not the ideal conditions of a lab. The result is the first solar panel system that thinks for itself – one that adapts to shade, weather, dirt and degradation in real time, recovering energy conventional solar leaves behind. The next step is putting it everywhere.”

“The next era of energy will not be built by simply installing more of the same passive solar panels we already have. It will be built by making solar intelligent enough to become the backbone of the global power system,” said Orin Hoffman, Managing Director at VXI Capital. “Optivolt has spent nine years building the architecture for that future. This financing is about taking it from a technical breakthrough to the platform on which the next generation of solar is built.”

“At Enphase, we transformed rooftop solar by managing power at the output of each panel. Today, rising panel power, the widespread adoption of storage, and the urgent need to reduce costs demand a new approach. Optivolt is bringing a new class of power electronics inside the panel itself to unlock the next leap in performance and economics,” said Paul Nahi, the founding CEO of Enphase and Optivolt investor. “I helped build the last generation of solar power electronics. At Optivolt we are building the next.”

Despite decades of progress, the conventional solar panel remains fundamentally passive hardware. Solar panels are rated under controlled laboratory conditions, but operate in a world of shade, dirt, debris and uneven degradation. Inside a conventional panel, one underperforming cell can constrain the output of an entire cell group – or cause a bypass diode to take that group out of production altogether. A localized problem can therefore suppress the output of a much larger section of the panel.

Optivolt redesigned the electrical architecture inside the panel around a power electronics brain. Its Power Balancer redistributes energy among the panel’s cell groups in real time, addressing mismatch before it becomes a larger loss. It replaces conventional bypass diodes and builds rapid shutdown and monitoring into the panel, allowing solar systems to be designed without separate module-level optimizers, microinverters or rapid-shutdown devices.

Putting active power electronics inside a solar panel created a fundamental engineering problem: heat. To solve it, Optivolt assembled a team with experience designing power systems for satellites, bringing the discipline of aerospace – where every watt matters and hardware must survive without repair – to a product built to spend decades inside a solar panel. That team developed the Power Balancer, which operates at 99.4 percent conversion efficiency under typical conditions.

Optivolt holds 19 issued patents and has 27 additional patent applications pending, covering key elements of its panel-integrated power electronics architecture. Optivolt has earned UL certifications covering its integrated junction-box enclosure, panel-integrated electronics, rapid-shutdown system and solar-module safety.

In Optivolt’s internal testing, panels incorporating the technology produced up to six times as much power as panels using bolted-on optimizers under defined shade scenarios.

“At SunPower, we spent nearly three decades pushing the limits of how efficiently a solar panel could turn sunlight into electricity. Optivolt is attacking the other half of the problem: how much energy that panel actually delivers in the real world once it leaves the factory,” said Tom Werner, former CEO of SunPower and Optivolt investor. “The next great solar panel won’t just produce more power under ideal conditions. It will deliver more energy across the imperfect conditions of its working life.”

“I’ve spent most of my career making clean energy financeable at scale – building new financing tools, redesigning markets and driving deployment costs down,” said Richard Kauffman, former Chairman of Energy and Finance for New York and an Optivolt investor. “But finance can only take the industry so far. When the same solar project produces more energy over its lifetime, the economics change everywhere. Optivolt rewrote that equation inside the panel itself, dramatically expanding the share of the world’s energy demand that solar can economically serve.”

Optivolt is a power electronics company building the intelligence layer for solar. Its panel-integrated Power Balancer redistributes energy among cell groups in real time, helping solar systems deliver more energy under real-world conditions while simplifying the hardware around the panel. Optivolt’s long-term goal is to make every solar panel an intelligent, connected node in a global energy network, capable of both managing its own performance and coordinating with the system around it. Founded by Thiel Fellow Rohit Kalyanpur, Optivolt is headquartered in San Francisco, CA.

#5519. What The Battery Show Told Us About the Next Chapter of Electrification

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Each fall you can learn a lot about the battery industry by walking through the halls of Huntington Place in Detroit. This year’s Battery Show North America, held October 12th – 15th at Huntington Place, brought together thousands of engineers, executives, and suppliers from cell chemistry and vehicle integration to manufacturing, supply chain, and grid storage.

What stood out wasn’t a single product or chemistry. It was a shift in the questions being asked. We saw less of “what’s possible?” and more of “what’s proven?” That shift matters for every OEM and fleet planning its next electrification program.

Energy storage dominated the agenda. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s keynote spotlighted how automakers including Ford, GM, and Volkswagen have converted existing EV battery manufacturing assets to serve surging grid storage demand. Across the industry, battery makers are moving from passenger EVs toward energy storage, defense, industrial, and rail applications.

Some will read this as electrification losing momentum. We see it differently. The industry is sorting out where batteries create clear, measurable value today. Commercial and industrial applications, with predictable duty cycles and a total cost of ownership that can be calculated, sit squarely in that zone.

The opening session came away optimistic about the U.S. battery industry, and the most important word in the ExxonMobil keynote was “competitive.” The goal isn’t to wall off the market but to build an American supply chain that wins on quality, reliability, and delivery.

For OEMs, domestic content now matters on two fronts. It’s a compliance requirement under Buy America and BABA, and it’s a way to gain shorter lead times, closer engineering collaboration, and greater supply certainty.

One of the clearest signals at the battery show wasn’t a single product but in how quickly the ecosystem around batteries is adapting. Cell testing labs and validation partners are retooling to support requirements well beyond passenger vehicles, from grid storage to off-highway equipment to defense.

That adaptability runs from research to production. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s Grid Storage Launchpad, a 93,000-square-foot facility backed by the Department of Energy, includes its own prismatic cell production line and was built to close the gap between concept and commercialization. The University of Michigan’s expanded Battery Lab is designed to serve applications ranging from EVs and grid storage to drones and defense.

This is American industrial manufacturing doing what it does best: adapting and improving. The talent, equipment, and expertise built for the automotive industry now serve a much broader range of industries meaning a deeper bench of domestic partners to test and validate battery systems.

If one idea echoed across the sessions, it was this: claims don’t move markets, deployments do. On a panel about scaling energy storage, Peak Energy’s chief scientist said it directly: “What’s really going to drive adoption is just proven deployments.”

So, what comes next? Every technology on display, from new chemistries like solid-state and sodium-ion to wireless battery management, advanced cooling, and second-life storage, now faces the same test: validation, scale, and performance in application. That test isn’t passed in a conference hall or on a spec sheet. It’s passed over thousands of operating hours, in real-world duty cycles, where reliability shows up as uptime.

That’s the right standard. A battery pack is more than its cell. Modules, thermal management, battery management software, enclosures, wiring, connectors and design all determine whether a system performs on day one and beyond.

Heavy-duty vehicles and equipment demand exactly this kind of proof. Vibration, heat, long shifts, and years of hard service leave no room for a battery that only looks good on a spec sheet.

That’s the work Proterra is currently powering. With more than 2,000 commercial vehicles on the road and over 50 million service miles powered, our track record comes from the real world. Our Onyx Battery Platform is engineered to power the most demanding of applications from Class 8 trucks, school bus, transit, industrial equipment and beyond. Its modular architecture allows battery technology proven on the highway to be adapted for additional markets like off-highway, defense, rail, marine and more so OEMs can start from a platform with real-world deployments behind it instead of a clean sheet.

We’re also acting on the supply chain conversation that dominated Detroit. Proterra now offers a U.S. cell option for the Onyx platform, with shipments beginning in 2027. Beyond the cell, we’re investing to onshore and nearshore critical pack and module components, as well as other major internal subsystems. Anchored by that U.S. cell, our onshoring effort is expected to increase American content by more than 600%. As CEO Chris Bailey put it, “The market is asking for greater supply chain certainty and stronger domestic content. This investment ensures we can deliver both while continuing to lead in performance and safety.”

Detroit made one thing clear. The next chapter of electrification belongs to the partners who deliver domestic content, proven real-world performance, and complete battery systems built for the most demanding jobs. That’s the standard we hold ourselves to every day.

#5523. Hyundai Motor Delivers Bali’s First Electric Buses for Public Transport

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

BALI, Indonesia, October 23, 2026 — Hyundai Motor Company today announced that it has supplied 11 electric buses for Bali’s first electric public transportation initiative in partnership with local bus body manufacturer Laksana as it pushes to expand electrification across Indonesia.

Hyundai Motor participated in the launch ceremony for the Bali Sustainable Transportation System Official Development Assistance (ODA) Project on October 22, where it presented 11 units of 8-meter mid-size COUNTY EV buses. Hyundai Motor supplied the electric bus platforms, on which Laksana built the bodies.

Led by the Global Green Growth Institute (GGGI), in cooperation with Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment (MCEE) of South Korea and the Government of Indonesia, the project is the first electric bus public transportation deployment initiative in Bali, a globally renowned tourism destination.

The project launch ceremony was attended by Rachmat Pambudy, Minister of National Development Planning of Indonesia; I Wayan Koster, Governor of Bali; Soon-gu Yoon, Ambassador of the Republic of Korea to Indonesia; Rowan Fraser, Country Representative for Indonesia at GGGI; and Young Shin Kim, Vice President, Commercial Vehicle Total Body Development Group at Hyundai Motor Company.

“We are pleased to contribute to Bali’s transition toward sustainable transportation through the supply of electric buses. Building on our experience and growing presence in Indonesia, we aim to expand our electrified commercial vehicle business across key cities and regions in this country. Going forward, we will continue to pursue new business opportunities across the Asia-Pacific region, leveraging our competitive products and field support capabilities.” —Chul Youn Park, Senior Vice President and Head of Global Commercial Vehicle and Light Commercial Vehicle Business Division, Hyundai Motor Company

The project aims to support Indonesia’s climate change response and greenhouse gas emissions reduction efforts, while enhancing Bali’s image as a leading tourism destination. It also offers a public-private collaboration model for transportation projects in the Asia-Pacific region.

The project follows a memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed between the South Korean and Indonesian governments last year to collaborate on transitioning Bali’s transportation system toward electric mobility. In the second half of 2025, GGGI led the tender for the supply of electric buses, and Hyundai Motor was selected as the supplier based on its electric vehicle quality, after-sales service capabilities, and experience in ODA transportation projects.

Hyundai Motor already supplied 11 COUNTY EV buses for public transportation service in Surabaya, Indonesia’s second-largest city, in November 2024. With the Bali project supply, Hyundai Motor demonstrated its global expertise in electrification technology and commercial vehicles solutions.

The County EV for the Bali project is a 20-seat mid-size electric bus equipped with a 128kWh battery, offering a driving range of more than 200 km on a single full charge. Hyundai Motor will also support the initial deployment phase of the Bali project through a dedicated EV Bus Field Quality Assurance Program, including the establishment of a local field quality situation room. It will monitor vehicle quality and driving performance as the buses begin service, supporting the project’s operation alongside the vehicle supply.

Moving forward, Hyundai Motor aims to expand its electrified vehicle business in Indonesia, including in Jakarta, Semarang, Surabaya and Medan. Building on this Indonesian presence, Hyundai Motor plans to expand regional market presence and establish competitive advantage in commercial vehicle electrification across Asia-Pacific markets, leveraging its product quality and after-sales support capabilities.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

SEOUL/PROVIDENCE, RI – Hyundai Motor Group (the Group) and Rhode Island School of Design (RISD) are pleased to continue their pioneering multiyear research partnership exploring the relationship between nature, art and design, and the future of mobility. This year’s theme, “Motion Rewild,” investigates adaptation in uncontrolled environments and using energy the way nature does, exploring motion across extreme environments.

Now in its seventh year, the unique partnership promotes innovative ways of seeing and making through a range of approaches. The collaboration brings together RISD faculty and staff members, student researchers, and designers from Hyundai, Genesis, and Kia working together to study biomimicry and biodesign as a pathway toward developing innovative materials, products, manufacturing methods, services, and experiences. The Regeneration Studio, led by an advanced scientific team in RISD’s Edna W. Lawrence Nature Lab together with studio faculty and experts from across RISD’s networks, brings to life new concepts in bio-innovation, decoding natural environments, systems, processes, and organisms and applying nature’s principles to designs that are more circular and less harmful to the planet.

Through the lens of “Motion Rewild,” this year the Regeneration Studio undertakes a pioneering study of physical AI mobility — robots operating in uncontrolled environments. Researchers are taking their cues from nature, the oldest engineer, which works in unstructured spaces including disaster sites, where mobility is needed most.

The programming for the year includes a fall advanced biodesign for mobility studio course, a second advanced studio course in the spring, and an extended summer research opportunity, including two-week intensives and five-week fellowships for Hyundai Motor Group designers and engineers.

The two studio courses combine scientific rigor with creative inquiry, supported by RISD’s Industrial Design department and the Nature Lab, and operate as an interdisciplinary workspace for a cohort of students from multiple departments on campus. Within this framework, faculty and students work in conversation with Hyundai Motor Group design engineers to discover and decode nature’s motion principles, how to design and build working forms based on those principles, how to push further through prototyping, and how to validate findings within the context of the mobility industry. The materials and methods explored through this immersive process will support the Hyundai Motor Group’s research on the future of mobility, creating design solutions that demonstrate real-world applications and potential impacts.

In the summer, teams of Hyundai Motor Group designers and engineers will engage in a nine-day intensive on campus, and select Fellows will fully immerse themselves in developing regenerative strategies and structural innovations within the Nature Lab during a five-week stay.

“Hyundai Motor Group has become a significant academic and creative collaborator, and we are pleased to continue this partnership. The Regeneration Studio is an incredible opportunity for RISD students, faculty, and staff to exchange knowledge and ideas with designers who are trusted international colleagues and thought partners. Applying principles that are present in the natural world and focus on sustainable explorations of AI will allow us to have another year of productive learning and discovery through this forward-looking relationship.” —Crystal Williams, RISD President

“At a time when technology is advancing at an unprecedented pace, our design begins with a deep understanding of people and nature. Nature has adapted to countless changes over time, accumulating its own solutions. We have made these natural principles the foundation of our design and technology, and I believe this is the essential asset that enables us to respond responsibly to any change.” —SangYup Lee, Executive Vice President and Head of Hyundai & Genesis Global Design

“As the years pass, RISD’s relationship with the Hyundai Motor Group strengthens and our common vision expands in ways that respects each entity’s identity and goals. We are heartened to see design explorations through nature’s endless gifts serve to reinforce our ethos, move innovation forward at a rapid pace, and provide future-proof solutions at a large scale.” —Touba Ghadessi, RISD Provost

“Nature teaches us that enduring motion is not about overcoming every obstacle through force, but about adapting intelligently to changing conditions. By studying how living systems move through complexity and adapt to uncertainty, we uncover new principles for mobility. At the intersection of nature’s ancient intelligence and emerging artificial intelligence, we see an opportunity for human creativity to shape technologies that are not only more resilient and responsive, but more responsible in how they serve people and help sustain the world we share.” —Karim Habib, Executive Vice President and Head of Kia Global Design

The Regeneration Studio continues to build upon the distinctive collaborative research platform designed solely for Hyundai Motor Group in 2019 by RISD Academic and Creative Partnerships and the Nature Lab. #724. 40 years later, Chernobyl's nuclear fallout is not what scientists expected

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Four decades after the Chernobyl (known today as Chornobyl) nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles remain scattered throughout the surrounding soil. The explosions that devastated the reactor released enormous quantities of debris, leaving behind contamination that continues to pose serious health risks. Even after 40 years, particles measuring just 8 to 50 micrometers remain highly radioactive, and entering affected areas still requires protective clothing.

Scientists have now taken a closer look at these tiny remnants and uncovered a surprising finding: some have preserved much of their original internal structure despite decades of exposure to the environment.

"There are three classes of these particles," explains Tobias Weissenborn, a physicist and doctoral candidate at Leibniz University Hannover. "First, there are particles that are chemically and physically still very similar to the nuclear fuel uranium dioxide. Then, there are particles that are partially or fully encased in, or completely fused with, their zirconium layer."

The second category originated during the extreme temperatures reached inside the reactor. As the nuclear fuel melted, it combined with zirconium, a highly resistant metal used in the fuel's protective outer layer.

"The third type of particle was created when the graphite-moderator caught fire," Weissenborn adds.

Under normal operating conditions, the graphite moderator slows fast neutrons, helping sustain the nuclear chain reaction. During the disaster, however, the graphite ignited and continued burning for ten days.

"In the process, the fuel oxidized into various uranium oxides" - such as U3O8.

Unlike more stable materials, this uranium oxide is mechanically fragile and can readily break apart into microscopic fragments. Wind can then carry these radioactive particles through the environment, where inhaling them presents serious health hazards.

Scientists Examine Chornobyl's Radioactive Dust in Unprecedented Detail

Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why radioactive particles from Chornobyl break down at different speeds. As these materials weather, they can produce several forms of uranium oxide, making their long term behavior difficult to predict.

Investigating these changes requires exceptionally precise equipment, including synchrotron X-ray diffraction, a technique that reveals the arrangement of atoms inside crystalline materials.

Dr. Christoph Hennig, a crystallographer at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf (HZDR), has now completed the first successful structural analysis of radioactive fragments of this kind at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble.

Working with Weissenborn, Hennig analyzed six particles recovered from the Chornobyl region. By identifying the different crystalline phases within them, the researchers gained information that could help determine how quickly radioactive substances escape into the surrounding environment.

"The colleagues from Hanover and I used various methods to isolate the particles from Ukrainian soil samples and attach them to tungsten electrodes," Weissenborn explains.

Because the samples were highly radioactive, they had to be enclosed within several layers of containment before being transported to Grenoble. There, Hennig's research team used X-ray diffraction to examine their internal structures by measuring how X-rays scattered as they passed through the material.

"First, we had to figure out how to examine the particle structure," Christoph Hennig explains. "Ultimately, we fully rotated the particles in an X-ray beam focused down to 100 micrometers - about the thickness of a human hair. We measured each particle from 2,000 different angles to seamlessly capture all the reflections."

The measurements allowed the scientists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the radioactive fragments, revealing details that had previously been inaccessible.

A Surprising Discovery About 40-Year-Old Nuclear Fallout

The results revealed something unexpected. Despite spending four decades in the environment, the nuclear fuel inside the six examined particles had retained much of its original crystal structure.

This suggests that at least some of the radioactive debris produced by the Chornobyl disaster is more chemically stable than researchers previously believed.

That stability could have important environmental implications. Rather than rapidly releasing radioactive substances, the particles appear capable of retaining fission products within their structures. If this behavior occurs more widely, it could help limit the movement of radioactive contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, the researchers emphasize that their findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Chornobyl's radioactive contamination is becoming harmless.

"However, every single particle has a different structure," warns Weissenborn, "and our experiment only studied six such particles from two different locations."

The relatively small sample means the findings cannot yet establish how radioactive particles behave throughout the entire contaminated region. To understand their stability more comprehensively, scientists would need to investigate many additional particles collected from a much broader range of locations.

Even with substantially more data, predicting the long term dangers of Chornobyl's radioactive debris would remain challenging. Individual particles can differ in composition, structure, and resistance to environmental weathering, potentially affecting when they release radioactive materials.

"And even if we obtained some averages at some point, we still wouldn't be able to make universal statements about health risks in the region," Weissenborn cautions. "Because even if the particles decay in a largely uniform patten, there will always be outliers, more persistent particles, that will release radionuclides at a later point in time."

These persistent particles could continue releasing radioactive substances long after other remnants have broken down, complicating efforts to assess future contamination and health risks.

For that reason, the new findings do not suggest that restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone can be removed anytime soon.

Research into the disaster's enduring consequences is continuing. Weissenborn and Hennig are already conducting additional experiments focused on highly radioactive transuranic phases found in the remaining debris. Their work could provide a clearer picture of how Chornobyl's nuclear materials change over time and how long their environmental effects may persist.

#4286. Scientists discover a hidden immune weapon against deadly lung cancer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a previously overlooked type of immune cell that can detect and destroy small cell lung cancer, the most aggressive form of lung cancer worldwide.

The discovery, reported in the journal Cancer Cell by researchers at WEHI, revealed that patients with greater numbers of these specialized immune cells tended to survive longer. The findings point to a previously unrecognized immune defense that could provide a new way to attack tumors that are notoriously difficult to treat.

The research team also demonstrated that medications already in use could enhance the ability of these immune cells to kill cancer. This approach could eventually improve existing treatments and increase the number of patients who benefit from immunotherapy.

Why Small Cell Lung Cancer Is So Difficult to Treat

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is among the deadliest cancers, with fewer than 7% of patients surviving for five years after diagnosis.

Immunotherapy has dramatically improved treatment for several types of cancer, but its success against SCLC has been much more limited. Only a small proportion of patients experience lasting benefits.

A major challenge is the cancer's ability to escape immune surveillance. SCLC tumors frequently avoid detection by the conventional immune cells that many immunotherapy treatments depend on, allowing the disease to persist despite the body's natural defenses.

The findings published in Cancer Cell suggest there may be another way for the immune system to recognize and attack these tumors. By identifying a previously overlooked immune pathway, researchers have uncovered a potential strategy for overcoming the cancer's ability to remain hidden.

Professor Kate Sutherland, the study's senior author and a laboratory head at WEHI, said the discovery could help address a longstanding challenge in lung cancer research: finding ways to make immunotherapy effective for more patients.

"For decades, we've known that small cell lung cancer is exceptionally good at hiding from the immune system," Prof Sutherland said.

"We've found an immune cell that appears to be able to see through that disguise.

"Strikingly, patients whose tumors contained more of these specialized immune cells often had better treatment outcomes.

"This is the strongest evidence yet that gamma delta T cells are present in small cell lung cancers and appear capable of fighting the disease.

"It suggests these cells may offer an alternative immune attack route for tumors that are able to evade conventional cancer-fighting immune cells, opening up new possibilities for treating a cancer that has seen few advances over the past decades."

Miniature Tumors Reveal a Powerful Immune Response

To investigate whether gamma delta T cells could directly attack lung cancer, the scientists developed miniature tumors in the laboratory using tissue samples donated by patients. The samples were obtained through the Victorian Cancer Biobank in partnership with the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

These miniature tumors, often called "mini tumors," provide one of the most realistic laboratory models of SCLC created so far, allowing researchers to study how cancer behaves and responds to potential treatments.

The team then introduced gamma delta T cells collected from the same patients' blood into the tumor models. The results showed that these immune cells were highly effective at identifying and destroying SCLC cells.

The scientists went a step further by testing whether two medications already approved for other uses could make this immune response even stronger. The drugs were tarlatamab (a cancer treatment approved for SCLC) and zoledronate (approved for osteoporosis and cancer-related bone disease).

Each medication contributed in a different way. Zoledronate increased the number of available gamma delta T cells and improved their ability to identify the lung cancer tumors. Tarlatamab helped bring the immune cells and cancer cells together, effectively directing the immune attack toward the tumors.

When used together, the drugs substantially increased the ability of gamma delta T cells to kill cancer cells in laboratory experiments. The findings suggest that combining existing treatments could provide a new strategy for strengthening the immune system's response to SCLC.

Could Existing Drugs Lead to New Lung Cancer Therapies?

Dr. Jin Ng, the study's first author, said the discovery could also support a treatment approach known as adoptive cell transfer. This involves collecting immune cells, multiplying them outside the body, and then delivering large numbers of those cells back into a patient to help fight cancer.

Because methods for expanding and reinfusing gamma delta T cells have already been developed, the researchers believe their findings could help accelerate the development of new treatments.

"For patients with lower gamma delta T cell levels, therapies such as zoledronate may offer a way to boost their cancer-fighting immune cells and responsiveness to treatments," Dr. Ng said.

"As therapies that expand and reinfuse gamma delta T cells already exist, our findings raise the exciting possibility of rapidly translating this discovery into new treatment strategies.

"This could open an entirely new avenue for personalized treatment approaches in small cell lung cancer and other solid cancers that are often missed by conventional immune cells."

Clues From Major Lung Cancer Immunotherapy Trials

The investigation began with an examination of data from two influential clinical trials, IMpower133 and CASPIAN. Both studies helped establish immunotherapy as a standard treatment option for patients with small cell lung cancer.

Researchers noticed evidence pointing toward an important role for gamma delta T cells and used sophisticated genomic and computational techniques to investigate further.

Analyses conducted by WEHI's genomics and bioinformatics teams revealed that patients with stronger gamma delta T cell signatures were among those who benefited most from immunotherapy.

Together with the laboratory findings, these results strengthen the evidence that gamma delta T cells play an important role in the immune response to SCLC and could become useful targets for future therapies.

The WEHI team is now working with scientists at the Doherty Institute to build on the discovery, with the goal of translating the findings into a new class of immunotherapy drugs for small cell lung cancer.

The research received support from Cancer Council Victoria, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), The CASS Foundation, and The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer.
