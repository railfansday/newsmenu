#3195. Investors are doing just as well this year by shunning AI. How to invest if the tech trade keeps fizzling

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A version of the S&P 500 without AI briefly pulled ahead of the traditional index this year and is now neck-and-neck as investors rotate into other areas of the market.

A proprietary Goldman Sachs index tracking the S&P 500 without stocks classified as "AI enablers" was up 12.4% through Oct. 22, edging ahead of the standard S&P 500's 12% gain. The Goldman index is now neck-and-neck with the S&P 500 as tech rallied in Monday trading.

Tech and AI-related companies make up about 45% of the S&P 500. That heavy weighting has helped power the index for years, but it's turned into a liability lately; the S&P 500 tech sector is down about 7% this month while other sectors have held up.

Investors have soured on debt and equity issued by companies like CoreWeave and Oracle. Shares of Meta Platforms sank almost 7% Thursday after it outlined more plans to spend heavily on capex and issue additional shares.

Companies supplying power and equipment for the buildout have been hit hard — names like GE Vernova, Bloom Energy, Vistra and Quanta Services all suffered steep declines during the recent selloff.

Goldman, which launched the measure earlier this year with S&P, uses the S&P 500 as its starting universe and excludes companies perceived to be enablers of AI before applying S&P 500 Index weighting methodology to the remaining universe of companies.

The ex-AI index lagged the S&P 500 for most of the year as investors continued to favor chipmakers, cloud-computing companies and other firms benefiting from heavy AI spending. However, that advantage has been narrowing and it disappeared last week.

Meanwhile, investors are rotating into defensive areas. ETFs like State Street Consumer Staples Select Sector SPDR ETF, State Street Health Select SPDR ETF and State Street Utilities Select Sector SPDR ETF are up 7% to 10% this month. Energy is ahead 10% too, though that's more related to the war in Iran.

Some analysts recommend sticking with non-AI companies for diversification and protection against more pain in tech. The "AI pendulum has swung back in favor of non-AI exposure over the last two weeks," Oppenheimer technical analyst Ari Wald said in a note on Oct. 24.

Two industries that Oppenheimer favors now are commercial services and insurance. The firm upgraded those industries to overweight last week, recommending Republic Services, Cintas, Verisk Analytics, Broadridge Financial Solutions, MetLife and Arthur J. Gallagher.

Berkshire Hathaway is also capitalizing on the rotation out of tech. Its Class B shares are up 8% this month, though it's still lagging behind the S&P 500 this year.

Berkshire owns a wide swath of operating companies, including insurance giant Geico and Burlington Northern Santa Fe railroad. It also has a $364 billion stock portfolio including companies like Apple, Coca-Cola, Alphabet and Bank of America. That diversification may help Berkshire continue to outperform if the tech sector drags down the S&P 500.

Trivector Research sees potential in "non-tech compounders." Healthcare giant Eli Lilly is one idea. The stock is up 22% on the strength of Lilly's weight-loss drugs. The company has more in the pipeline, including a next-generation obesity drug that Lilly plans to file for FDA approval early in 2027.

Viking Holdings, a cruise operator, is also proving durable. The company said on its August earnings call that 40% of its river capacity and 60% of its ocean capacity was already booked for 2027. "Based on recent events, we've seen our guests have proved to be resilient and are continuing to book 2027 and future seasons," the company told investors.

Viking stock is up 15% this year but it may still have upside. The average Wall Street target is $109, above recent prices around $82. Barclays recently upgraded the cruise operator to overweight with a $102 target, citing the company's "compelling risk/reward."

Brazil's left-wing president had been trailing right-wing challenger FlÃ¡vio Bolsonaro, but managed to turn the race around in just three weeks.

At a moment when many doubted Lula could reverse the result, a WhatsApp channel called Lula's Army emerged, and rapidly grew to 10.8 million followers.

Led by Congressman AndrÃ© Janones, the operation encouraged supporters to share videos, promote hashtags and recruit new voters.

Its dramatic growth between the two rounds was particularly notable because it appeared to reverse a long-standing imbalance in Brazilian politics.

For years, conservatives aligned with former President Jair Bolsonaro had been widely seen as dominating online political mobilisation, especially on platforms such as WhatsApp and Telegram.

Lula called AndrÃ© Janones "our General" after his major contribution paid off

The emergence of Lula's Army convinced many supporters that Brazil's left had finally found a way to compete in a digital arena. The shift was particularly significant because social media has played a central role in Brazilian politics since Jair Bolsonaro's 2018 election victory, when WhatsApp networks became a defining feature of political campaigning.

Lula's Army messages often included ready-made templates for followers to share with friends and family. Each one assigned supporters a specific task aimed at boosting Lula's visibility on social media.

Janones frequently instructed his "troops" to "blow up" the latest campaign video or help a new hashtag "take off".

"Guys, urgent!", "Don't go to sleep yet", "No food before the mission!", and "I have one more task before you're dismissed", were some of the instructions.

On the eve of the election, the congressman also hosted a 24-hour live event - dubbed the "turnaround livestream" - even as polls continued to suggest a victory for Bolsonaro.

The tactic worked, despite worries by some members of the Workers' Party that Janones's often-confrontational style on social media could undermine the more moderate image they hoped to project for Lula.

In his victory speech, Lula referred to Janones as "our General" and thanked him for "raising the morale of our troops every day".

Measuring the electoral impact of online mobilisation, however, is notoriously difficult.

While Lula's allies credit Lula's Army with helping fuel the comeback, political scientists caution that it is impossible to determine how many votes, if any, were directly influenced by the WhatsApp operation.

Political scientist Denilde Holzhacker argues that a shift in Lula's campaign strategy was key to his comeback.

Rather than relying solely on online engagement, she says the Workers' Party candidate succeeded in connecting digital campaigning with grassroots mobilisation on the ground.

According to Holzhacker, the campaign intensified local efforts and adapted its messaging - linking a Bolsonaro victory with concerns about potential changes to social programmes and the loss of rights.

"It was to Lula's credit that he recognised his own weakness and completely changed his style," she says. "And Janones managed to do something that had seemed unthinkable for Brazil's left - dominate social media."

By contrast, Holzhacker argues that FlÃ¡vio Bolsonaro entered the run-off overly confident of victory and failed to communicate his message to his supporters as effectively.

Sociologist and political scientist Mayra Goulart, a professor at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, also cautions against attributing Lula's comeback solely to social media.

"It was a combination of social media and street-level mobilisation," she says. "The physical world enabled a form of mobilisation capable of reducing abstention and persuading undecided voters."

In her view, Lula's supporters succeeded in turning online enthusiasm into real-world political engagement.

"Those committed to Lula's victory worked to bring people together and persuade them to vote," Goulart says.

She argues that the effort not only helped increase turnout but could also prove valuable for Lula's next administration.

"I believe this collective effort was an important learning experience and will prove valuable for a government that still faces many battles ahead. It not only helped reduce abstention, but also converted a significant number of undecided voters."

Lula himself suggested that the mobilisation behind his comeback could shape the way he governs in the years ahead.

In his victory speech, he said the election had taught him many lessons and pledged to change the way Brazil is governed.

"We are going to give people a greater say in decision-making, and digital communication will play a much bigger role in my government."

The Lumina Project spans 2 billion years and will allow scientists to test theories and predictions about the origins of the cosmos.

The origin of the universe is one of the greatest scientific mysteries of all time. Scientists have developed numerous theories and testable predictions about how the universe began, including the Big Bang model, cosmic inflation and quantum-cosmology proposals.

To test those theories, researchers from the Center for Astrophysics, a collaboration between Harvard and the Smithsonian, assembled the Lumina Project, a simulation of the early universe far larger than any prior one.

The details of the simulation, published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (PDF), describe how regular matter, dark matter and radiation likely interacted during the first 2 billion years of the universe, covering a region equivalent to a cube roughly 1.5 billion light-years on each side.

For reference, the prior champion for the largest early-universe simulation was the Thesan simulation in 2022, which simulated about a billion years of the early universe across a region roughly equivalent to a 300-million-light-year cube.

It does all of this in incredible detail, largely due to observations made by NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope and other telescopes that spend considerable time observing the oldest celestial objects. By looking at those ancient space objects, scientists have been able to piece together at least some ideas of what the early universe may have looked like.

“With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics,” said Oliver Zier, lead author of the study, in a statement. “To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions,” Zier said.

The simulation was made on the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee. Frontier has around 37,000 GPUs across its system, and the Lumina Project used about 15,000 of them.

That’s what helped the simulation become the largest and most detailed simulation ever made, giving researchers plenty of unique features to work with.

One example is helium reionization, a process that scientists believe took place in the early universe. Back then, the universe was so hot that virtually all matter was ionized, meaning it consisted of charged particles. When the universe cooled about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, atoms started combining together to make regular hydrogen and helium. Then, a short time later (comparatively speaking), the first generation of stars emerged and emitted enough ultraviolet light to reionize nearby hydrogen and helium atoms.

“Lumina follows both hydrogen and helium reionization,” Zier told CNET in an email. “This allows us to study these processes together in one self-consistent simulation, meaning it captures reionization as it occurred while allowing its effects to immediately influence the evolving universe within the model.”

Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas and an author on the paper, said the team is incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina. “For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation,” Smith said in a statement.

The main function of Lumina is to help scientists test and confirm theories about the early universe and gauge whether their understanding of physics aligns with the simulation’s results.

The team has already used the simulation to make some interesting predictions, including that there were “fewer massive galaxies” in the early universe than James Webb Space Telescope observations suggest. The simulation also houses the mysterious little red dots spotted by JWST, which some scientists now believe are black hole stars.

A side benefit is that the simulation has some truly gorgeous imagery, showing off a lot of interactions of the early universe, including gases at various temperatures mixing together, chemical reactions and the locations of early galaxies and stars. Since the simulation also fills in where dark matter and dark energy would be, the entire thing is a gorgeous litany of colorful clouds, threads and filaments.

#473. NASA SEES Interns Tackle Real-World Challenges in Lunar Exploration

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The NASA STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) Enhancement in Earth Science (SEES) Internship Program at the University of Texas Center for Space Research places high school students on small, mission-focused research teams mentored by NASA, academic, and industry experts. During summer 2026, the SEES Mission Architecture Tool for Research, Integration, and eXploration (MATRIX) team focused on applying a method for measuring propellant mass in microgravity, where liquid fuel clings to tank walls instead of settling – a challenge central to NASA’s Artemis missions.

The MATRIX team was mentored by Dr. Kevin Crosby, who, along with colleagues at NASA, developed the Modal Propellant Gauging (MPG) approach to microgravity propellant mass gauging at Carthage College in Kenosha, WI. MPG uses the acoustic response of propellant tanks to estimate the mass of fuel inside a tank. The method has been used successfully in several spaceflight experiments and is scheduled for demonstration on the Intuitive Machines IM-3 mission. The IM-3 lander, Trinity, is scheduled to touch down on the Moon in the Reiner Gamma region in 2027. The team developed models for predicting when MPG can take reliable readings during the mission, giving the interns direct experience with a technology preparing for spaceflight and lunar surface operations.

Reconstructing a Lunar Mission from Public Data

The MATRIX team relied entirely on publicly available sources, including Intuitive Machines’ published mission data. The NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory’s Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility (NAIF) provided detailed trajectory records for the IM-1 and IM-2 missions. By comparing the IM-2 spacecraft’s reconstructed flight path with a model of its motion without engine thrust, the team identified engine burns and estimated the change in velocity associated with each burn.

Because a complete IM-3 trajectory is not public, the team wrote a reconstruction algorithm using published IM-1 and IM-2 flight data. To model fluid behavior, the team ran computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations to estimate slosh dynamics within the tanks and conducted Monte Carlo simulations to predict the range of possible propellant states.

The team’s analysis showed that MPG can take reliable readings during the two primary burns, Lunar Orbit Insertion (LOI) and Powered Descent, and in some cases during Descent Orbit Insertion. The spacecraft’s starting mass had the greatest effect on how long each gauging window lasts. The MATRIX team also identified candidate IM-3 launch opportunities for 2027 and produced a landing analysis for the Reiner Gamma site that showed the distribution of possible landing sites near the target.

The team briefed the Intuitive Machines propulsion group, walking the engineers through their methods and results and demonstrating that a rigorous analysis of an active lunar mission could be built from public data. The students presented their research, “Computational Models to Estimate Modal Propellant Gauging (MPG) Windows on the Intuitive Machines IM-3 Mission,” at the SEES Annual Symposium and the Wisconsin Space Grant Consortium Annual Conference. The team will present again at the American Geophysical Union (AGU) Annual Meeting in San Francisco in December 2026.

"This high school team, in a period of less than a month, developed a depth of understanding and facility with advanced methods that rivals advanced graduate students in the field," said Crosby. "Their work product is exceptional, and their presentation to Intuitive Machines engineers impressed everyone with the sophistication and creativity of their work. The SEES program has consistently provided a unique environment for students to demonstrate they can achieve far more than what is asked of them, even in the most advanced classes."

The SEES program gives 11th and 12th grade students direct experience with the data, tools, and analytical methods used across the aerospace industry. By applying NASA and commercial mission data to an active lunar mission, the MATRIX team contributed analysis relevant to propellant management, a capability needed for in-space refueling, fuel depots, and sustained exploration of the Moon and beyond. Thank you to all the mentors, project assistants, and volunteers who have made SEES possible for over a decade.
