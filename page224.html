The 122nd World Series begins Friday night at American Family Field in Milwaukee. The National League-best Milwaukee Brewers will host the American League-best Tampa Bay Rays, and one way or the other, baseball will have a first-time champion this season. Neither the Brewers (established 1970) nor Rays (established 1998) have ever won a World Series.

"The 2020 World Series was kind of a blur. The whole postseason was a blur. I'm a heck of a lot happier we're in this situation now coming out on the other side of that," Rays manager Kevin Cash said on Thursday. "Those games, they were intense, there was a lot of anxiety in them, but I felt that same intensity and anxiety in the ALCS just two or three days ago. Excited to go and watch our guys do their thing in the next week to ten days."

Historically, the winner of Game 1 of a best-of-seven has gone on to win the series 65% of the time. It's 64% in the World Series, so basically identical. Just last year, though, the Los Angeles Dodgers lost Game 1 of the World Series at home, then rallied to beat the Toronto Blue Jays in seven games. Winning Game 1 certainly gives you a leg up, but it does not mean the series is over.

Here now are three key questions heading into Game 1 on Friday night.

Jacob Misiorowski, the likely NL Cy Young winner, has not been himself this postseason. He failed to complete five innings in either of his two starts, not necessarily because of ineffectiveness, but because of high pitch counts. Misiorowski threw 88 pitches en route to allowing two runs in four innings in Game 1 of the NLDS. In Game 1 of the NLCS, it was one run on 94 pitches in 4 ⅓ innings.

Misiorowski's high-octane fastball has played down this postseason. The velocity is plenty good. He averaged -- averaged -- 101.0 mph with his heater in the NLDS and NLCS. Hitters are not swinging and missing nearly as much as they did during the regular season, though, and the resulting foul balls have run up his pitch count.

The Rays had the lowest strikeout rate (18.8% of plate appearances) and lowest whiff rate (20.7% of swings) during the regular season, by a lot too. The Blue Jays were a distant second with a 22.2% whiff rate. Misiorowski has had trouble missing bats with his heater this postseason and he'll now face the best contact-hitting offense in the sport. That could be a problem.

"He hasn't been as crisp," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said about Misiorowski on Thursday. "I don't think people are up there thinking it's going to be 104, and I think they're elated if it's anything less than that. They're obviously looking at an area. They're trying to exploit everything. He's exposed, like all these guys get at some point. He's exposed. They're going to attack a certain way, and we're going to try to counter that attack the way we counter it."

The Brewers are expected to carry five lefties in their World Series bullpen: Aaron Ashby, Shane Drohan, DL Hall, Kyle Harrison, and JoJo Romero. Ashby, Hall, and Romero are the trusted late-inning guys. Drohan started a bullpen game in the NLCS. Harrison is more of a break-glass-in-case-of-emergency option. He'll get the ball deep in extra innings, something like that.

Murphy will be able to roll out lefty after lefty behind Misiorowski and that's bad news for the Rays, who hit righties way better than lefties during the regular season. Here are their numbers:

The Rays have shown little power against lefties this year, which is hard to believe with right-handed thumpers Junior Caminero and Yandy Díaz anchoring the lineup. Ryan Vilade, Tampa's go-to righty platoon bat, will undoubtedly get several key pinch-hit at-bats in the World Series, and perhaps as soon as Game 1. The Rays must solve Milwaukee's lefties to win the title.

The Brewers swept the Dodgers in the NLCS and have not played since last Thursday. They've played only eight games in the last 25 days. Rest is good. Believe me, everyone on the Brewers is happy to take it. Still, the fact of the matter is they haven't played much baseball the last three weeks, or any baseball over the last week.

"You can't recreate real, you know what I mean?" Murphy said about his team's downtime. "It's a long layoff, and if we want to use it as an excuse, then let people use it as an excuse, but I refuse to do that. We needed a little bit of a break. It was an emotional series in L.A. Cross-country trip, all that. We had a few days where it was a little lighter, but then we've picked it up and gotten a lot of live ABs, but nothing simulates the game the way it is."

In the three-wild-card era, teams that have five or more days off going into a postseason series are 13-10 in Game 1. Teams have so many tools available to them (Trajekt hitting machines, etc.) that it is easier to navigate that downtime than it was even five years ago. Murphy's right and you can't recreate real, but you can come pretty close. The layoff is definitely a subplot going into Game 1.

The robotic Griffin lunar lander has passed a host of critical tests ahead of its first-ever moon mission, which is scheduled to launch later this year.

At NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California, Voyager Technologies' Griffin Mission One (or Griffin-1) lunar lander has gone through an absolute gauntlet of tests. The spacecraft was put through environmental testing that explored how the vehicle will fare in the extreme conditions it will experience on its ride to the surface of the moon.

This testing simulated the extreme temperatures, shaking and even the sounds that the spacecraft will experience from launch to landing. And, as JPL announced recently, the lander passed this series of tests with flying colors, as we can see in this image snapped Aug. 31 of the lander inside the 25-foot-wide (7.6 meters) space simulator at JPL.

"NASA JPL’s Environmental Test Laboratory has been putting robotic explorers to the test since the Ranger program gave us NASA’s first up-close views of the moon's surface in the mid-1960s. We’re proud to provide our expertise and facilities to support this new phase of commercial lunar exploration," Charles Whetsel, manager for the Exploration Systems Development Office at JPL, said in a statement.

Griffin-1 is expected to launch to the moon later this year as part of NASA's CLPS (Commercial Lunar Payload Services) initiative. The spacecraft will carry over 1,100 pounds (455 kilograms) of cargo to its landing site near the lunar south pole.

In this cargo will be science experiments and new technologies that will be tested on the moon's surface, including Astrolab's FLIP rover, which will explore the lunar surface with a history-making supercomputer onboard.

This suite of experimental hardware won't just expand our understanding of the moon up close. Griffin-1's exploration will also help set the stage for a future, crewed moon base, which NASA aims to build near the south pole over the next half-decade or so.

#42. Mystery over source of Ebola infection in Nigeria fuels concern about spread of virus

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Switzerland says patient is wife of Swiss diplomat and believed to have contracted virus while travelling in Uganda

Health officials across Africa are racing to determine how Nigeria’s first Ebola patient ⁠in more than a decade ⁠became infected, amid fears ​the outbreak could spread further after reaching a fourth African country.

The Swiss foreign ministry said on Friday the patient in Nigeria, the Ugandan-born wife of a Swiss diplomat based in Abuja, had not travelled to the Democratic Republic of ⁠Congo, where the outbreak was first detected in mid-May before spreading to Uganda, Kenya and now Nigeria. The woman was believed to have contracted Ebola while travelling with her husband in Uganda, the ministry added.

The new information contradicts accounts from Uganda, which previously said the woman had travelled to eastern DR Congo, and will raise concerns about the ability of authorities to contain the outbreak, which is already the most geographically widespread in history.

The Ugandan government reported Ebola cases in May and June before WHO declared it Ebola-free in August. A Ugandan government spokesperson told Reuters on Thursday that Uganda had no ​Ebola cases and that, if ​it did, it would make that information ​public.

The World Health Organization said investigations were continuing to establish the likely source of infection for the Ebola patient in Nigeria.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention is investigating whether the patient may have come into contact with an Ebola patient in Kenya, as both travelled from Uganda’s Entebbe airport on 3 October, said Dr Wessam Mankoula, head of emergency preparedness and response at the organisation.

Catherine Smallwood, a WHO Ebola incident manager in DR Congo, told a UN briefing in Geneva via video ‌link on Friday that WHO staff in DR Congo had no information showing the patient in Nigeria had travelled to the country.

Smallwood said a link to the case in Kenya was possible, ​but added that officials would need to compare the timing with when the Nigeria patient’s symptoms began to tell whether the infection more likely happened at the Ugandan airport or elsewhere.

Marion Koopmans, a Dutch virologist and member of the WHO’s Ebola emergency committee, cast doubt on the theory. “I think that is very speculative unless there is evidence of sharing the same ​space in a manner that could explain that,” she said. “Random presence at the same location, a large airport, is not a supported epidemiological link by any standard of what we currently know.”

The Nigerian health minister, Muhammad Ali Pate, said Nigeria’s case remains contained and that contact-tracing was continuing. “We are trying to contain it so it doesn’t go out to the communities. Hopefully, it won’t get to that and we are working hard to ensure that,” Pate said.

Melaku Yirga, vice-president for Africa at Prosper Global (formerly Mercy Corps), said the confirmation of Ebola in Kenya and Nigeria reinforces the need for countries to work closely together to prevent further transmission.

“What matters most now is speed, sharing information across borders without delay and ensuring frontline health workers and local organiaations have the resources to respond quickly,” he said.

Ebola is a highly contagious and often fatal viral disease that affects humans and non-human primates. It spreads through body fluids or contaminated materials and causes organ damage, blood vessel impairment and sometimes severe internal and external bleeding.

The current outbreak is caused by the rare Bundibugyo virus, which has no approved treatment or vaccine. It was first announced on 15 May and has infected more than 9,500 people and killed nearly half of them.

The outbreak is the largest and deadliest in DR Congo’s history and second only ​globally to ​the 2014-16 outbreak in west Africa, which infected ​more than ‌28,600 people ​and ​killed more than 11,000.

#321. Restaurant earnings will test whether owning more brands pays off

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Restaurant stocks have split sharply this year. And it could be tied to how many brands they own.

Shares of Brinker International, Darden Restaurants and Restaurant Brands International are up roughly 32%, 18% and 12%, respectively, year to date. Those companies all own more than one restaurant chain, with brands collectively ranging from Chili's to Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Burger King and Tim Hortons.

Meanwhile, stocks like McDonald's, Chipotle, Cava, Wendy's, Shake Shack and Domino's have all dropped this year, with several down double-digit percentages. Those companies all have one core chain to their names.

Those share moves underscore what some analysts see as the benefits of a diversified restaurant business, especially at a time when any misstep can make cash-strapped consumers go elsewhere when they dine out.

Investors will get another look at how operating multiple chains — or not — is affecting performance when major restaurant companies report quarterly results starting next week. One company that could be watching is Inspire Brands, the owner of chains like Dunkin', Buffalo Wild Wings and Jimmy John's, which is moving toward going public.

A business built around one main restaurant concept depends more heavily on that brand holding consumers' attention, whereas a company with a portfolio has other potential sources of sales if one brand loses momentum.

That cushion can be critical, but only if another chain is strong enough to pick up the slack, according to some Wall Street analysts.

"Darden is probably the best case of the scale and size and diversity of business," said Nick Setyan, a restaurant analyst at Mizuho Securities. "If one business isn't doing well, the other ones are."

Setyan said that dynamic has played out between Darden's key brands — Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse — over recent quarters. LongHorn's growth has outpaced Olive Garden's for months, and in the latest reported quarter, LongHorn's comparable sales rose 6.2% compared with Olive Garden's 1.1%.

KeyBanc Capital Markets also cited the range of Darden's portfolio as a positive in an October research note, saying it gives the company "exposure to a broad range of consumers." The company's fine dining and casual offerings can be beneficial in a K-shaped economy where consumer spending can vary by income.

The firm has a buy-equivalent rating on Darden and a $237 price target, compared with a price of around $215 as of Thursday.

Restaurant Brands International, similarly, offers exposure to different categories and markets through Burger King, Tim Hortons and Popeyes. But its portfolio does not fully insulate the company from a weak brand.

In the second quarter, Burger King's U.S. comparable sales accelerated to 8.5%, from 5.8% in the first quarter, while Tim Hortons' Canadian comparable sales slowed to just 0.1%, from 1.5% over the same period. That left Burger King's turnaround carrying more of the portfolio as Tim Hortons stalled.

Setyan said Burger King's recent strong performance has been central to the company's momentum, helped by its ability to better communicate its value items to customers as McDonald's sales growth slowed.

"If it weren't for Burger King's current performance, I don't know if [Restaurant Brands] would be going so well," he said.

Mizuho initiated coverage of Restaurant Brands on Oct. 20 with a buy-equivalent rating and an $85 price target. Shares traded at about $76 as of Thursday.

A focus on one brand can also be a strength. Starbucks and Cheesecake Factory have been restaurant-stock standouts this year, showing how a clear brand identity and investment can drive outperformance when execution and customer demand click. Their shares have climbed 21% and nearly 123%, respectively, this year.

And Starbucks, for its part, said it would not buy Chipotle after reportedly considering a massive buyout that would have diversified its business.

The current round of tensions began on Sept. 21, when the Azerbaijani government approved a more restrictive protocol on school uniforms that effectively banned students from wearing the hijab in the country’s state schools. Two days later, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan, whose Justice and Development Party (AKP) ended Turkey’s own headscarf ban, condemned headscarf bans—without naming Azerbaijan specifically—as an attack on women’s fundamental freedoms during his speech to the U.N. General Assembly.

Within Azerbaijan, rare unsanctioned demonstrations took place following the order’s implementation on Oct. 12. While arrests and internet restrictions curtailed them, small protests, bringing together both religious people and secular left-liberals, have continued.

At the same time, what began as veiled barbs and grumbling from Turkey’s ruling AKP has now burst into a stream of condemnation. On Oct. 12, Oktay Saral, chief advisor to Erdogan, and Leyla Sahin Usta, a deputy parliamentary leader for the AKP, expressed their outrage over the Azerbaijani policy in posts on X. Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev and Erdogan themselves have made passive-aggressive comments while stopping short of any explicit criticism.

But the rift goes beyond the hijab. Notably, the recent tensions coincided with Israeli President Isaac Herzog’s state visit to Baku. Azerbaijan’s close relationship with Israel, long a subject of discomfort for Turkey, has become a more salient issue amid Ankara’s own rapidly deteriorating relationship with the country. Israel has given Azerbaijan military equipment and surveillance technology. Azerbaijan has allegedly provided Israel with a crucial forward base for operations into Iran. This summer, the Israeli Knesset dropped a resolution recognizing the Armenian genocide—intended as a jab at Turkey—over Azerbaijani objections.

At a deeper level, there is also a fundamental disagreement about sovereignty. Erdogan and his allies imagine Turkey as a leading power in a world increasingly divided into quasi-imperial spheres of influence. In such a worldview, there’s no way for two states to maintain relations without hierarchy. Azerbaijan, though ruled by a glitzy petro-oligarch nomenklatura, is poorer than Turkey, and its population is roughly the same size as that of the European side of Istanbul.

The Azerbaijani government and its supporters present their country as a rising, independent middle power capable of balancing between difficult, larger neighbors. Growing self-confidence and a hunger for strategic autonomy among leaders in both Baku and Ankara lie squarely behind the clash over the hijab ban. Ironically, in this regard, it is Azerbaijan’s similarity to Turkey that has created strain between the two countries.

Despite the intense rhetoric of brotherhood and unity that could be heard from both countries in recent years, Turkish-Azerbaijani ties were not always so close. In March 1995, an Azerbaijani colonel launched a coup attempt against Azerbaijani President Heydar Aliyev with the active involvement of Turkish citizens, allegedly including pan-Turkist elements within the Turkish security establishment. These pan-Turkists, as well as their ideological allies in Azerbaijan, were apparently unhappy that the former Communist Party leader Aliyev had himself risen to power in the wake of the coup that deposed President Abulfaz Elchibey, a charismatic radical nationalist intellectual. Turkey’s then-President Suleyman Demirel tipped off Aliyev to the plot, but the incident nonetheless did damage to the two countries’ bilateral relationship.

Changes in Turkey and the region would help bring the two countries closer. In 2003, Heydar Aliyev died and his son, Ilham, assumed the presidency. Azerbaijan was at peace and increasingly prosperous. A pipeline, finished in 2006, brought Azerbaijani oil from the Caspian Sea fields to the Turkish port of Ceyhan on the eastern Mediterranean. Meanwhile, Erdogan, who took power in Turkey in 2003, was fighting his own battle against coup plotters and marginalizing members of the pan-Turkist movement.

By the time that the Azerbaijani army made its decisive move on Nagorno-Karabakh, Azerbaijani and Turkish leaders were eager to forget their complicated past. Azerbaijan’s 2020 victory over Armenians in the Second Nagorno-Karabakh War brought Azerbaijan and Turkey together as never before. The euphoria of that event was embodied in the victory parade of Dec. 10, 2020, which featured both Ilham Aliyev and Erdogan at the rostrum surrounded by Azerbaijani and Turkish flags.

Behind the public fanfare, though, unease remained. In private, Aliyev was wary of the growing power of Turkish Islamists. According to cables published by WikiLeaks, Aliyev stated to U.S. officials in 2009 that he had banished Turkish soap operas from Azerbaijani television broadcasts to avoid promoting women wearing headscarves. In 2015, Erdogan also began his own reconciliation with his country’s pan-Turkists, who eventually grew into some of his closest allies.

In 2021, the Azerbaijani Ministry of Education blocked an effort by pan-Turkists to build a school in Shusha, a city formerly controlled by Armenians. They were presumably wary of inviting the same pan-Turkist Idealist Hearths organization that participated in the 1995 coup attempt into the highly securitized and symbolically significant city.

Credit for the 2020 victory also became a touchy subject. In the summer of 2024, Erdogan stated, “as we entered Karabakh and Libya, we’ll do similarly to them,” referring to Israel. This prompted a scolding in Azerbaijan’s official state-owned gazette, which rejected the notion that Turkish or any other foreign soldiers had participated in Azerbaijani campaigns.

In early October, Aliyev suggested obliquely that foreign arms suppliers had engaged in profiteering in their dealings in Azerbaijan. Turkish firms, which prominently supplied drones and weapons to Azerbaijan in the buildup to the 2020 war, had just struck the latest of many deals to sell arms to Baku.

Azerbaijan occupies an ambiguous and increasingly uncomfortable space within expanding Turkish regional ambitions. Erdogan has typically made his first post-election international trips to Azerbaijan and northern Cyprus. Turks often refer to northern Cyprus as yavru vatan, or baby homeland—an outpost of the motherland that must be nurtured and defended. An AKP official tasked with relations with the Turkic world addressed conflict with Azerbaijan by stating, “we know how to discuss our problems within our own family setting—in a manner that befits us.”

As the Azerbaijani government is likely realizing, these family analogies are loaded. The internationally unrecognized Turkish Republic of North Cyprus is largely dependent on and subservient to Turkey. Last year, the Turkish Cypriot government tried unsuccessfully to lift a hijab ban to appease Ankara. Saral, the same Erdogan advisor who criticized Azerbaijan’s ban, responded to the effort’s failure by insulting the Turkish Cypriot prime minister.

In contrast to their effective domination of northern Cyprus, Turks have long shown meaningful deference to Baku on key issues of mutual concern. Turkish normalization with Armenia has been slow-walked for almost two decades out of Ankara’s respect for Azerbaijani sensitivities. Responding to the public break, higher-level figures in the Turkish government have also tried to assuage Baku. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said in a press conference that any differences would be resolved respectfully. Devlet Bahceli, Erdogan’s coalition partner, was even more deferential, calling for dialogue between “two strong states” that “span the same geography of the heart.”

Still, Turkey’s AKP elite have long been a better gauge for the direction of Turkish policy than government officials such as Fidan or political allies such as Bahceli. Baku clearly understands this and is increasingly worried by those elites’ unwavering ambition to make Turkey the predominant power in their shared region. The ruling New Azerbaijan Party’s response to Saral and Usta is instructive. Its statement addressed the hijab ban issue almost as an afterthought, instead focusing on defending Azerbaijan’s position on Israel-Palestine and attacking Turkish “hegemonic tendencies” in the Islamic world.

Azerbaijan’s state-controlled media was far less measured. One article, deleted shortly after it was posted, accused Fidan, who is also the former head of Turkish intelligence, of giving secret instructions to a Turkish nongovernmental organization during his visit to Baku. It accused Fidan and other Turks of “plans to interfere in internal affairs,” “intelligence operations,” “coup and chaos plans,” and “recruiting agents of influence,” and it invoked Turkish involvement in the 1995 coup attempt. The Turkish Embassy in Baku sternly condemned these allegations.

An outlet linked to SOCAR, the Azerbaijani state oil company, chimed in to protest Turkish “attempts to turn brotherhood into a tool of economic pressure” and objected that “Azerbaijan is not a vassal state” and would not accept Turkish “‘big brother’ dictates.” Other recent posts in state-aligned media have charged that Turks historically sought to establish an Armenian state at Azerbaijan’s expense and that AKP’s foreign policy is an existential threat to the national identity of the states comprising the “Turkic world.”

The hijab ban protests are hardly the work of an invisible Turkish hand, but Aliyev is right to worry about Turkish ambitions to shape his country’s future. One of Heydar Aliyev’s achievements was fortifying his authoritarian state against the designs of Iran, Russia, and Turkey. Ilham Aliyev appears to have things under control for the moment, but he is undoubtedly worried about any potential complications, particularly as he begins promoting his only son, the 29-year-old Heydar Ilhamoglu Aliyev, as a would-be successor. The sensational allegations of Turkish plotting against Azerbaijan, which follow an alleged pro-Russian coup attempt last year, reflect the profound insecurity of Azerbaijan’s rulers, who perceive manifold threats to their power from home and abroad.

The Azerbaijan-Turkey alliance will almost certainly survive this crisis—their economies and societies are now so deeply intertwined that a deeper break is difficult to imagine. The Azerbaijani government has recently insisted that more of the weapons that it buys be built in Azerbaijan, and that they will continue to fortify their country against alleged foreign interference. It remains to be seen how Turkey will respond to Azerbaijan’s decision to issue international arrest warrants for seven Turkish nationals, among them several Islamist commentators. The Turkish Constitution forbids the extradition of citizens outside of its obligations to the International Criminal Court.

This this may well lead to Baku unwinding Turkish cultural and religious influence and promoting secular “Azerbaijanism” as an alternative to Islamic conservatism and pan-Turkism. However events unfold, expect more bickering and frustration between these brotherly Turkic states.

Specifically, as learned this morning by the editorial staff of MilanNews, the former Chelsea and Fulham man has suffered a knee problem after suffering a knock to the back of the joint. It is not yet clear whether this will have any consequences on the midfielder’s availability for Sunday.
