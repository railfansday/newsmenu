Foundation, the company behind the Passport hardware wallet, has reported a security incident that happened on the 26th of November.

Here, the attackers sent more than 23,000 phishing email attempts using emails that appeared to come from official Foundation domains.

These emails were designed to trick users into revealing their wallet recovery phrases, which gave attackers access to the cryptocurrency stored in their wallets.

These numbers do not tell us how many people had personal information exposed. We are still determining that number and will share it once we can confirm it.

This is because some emails were sent multiple times or blocked before delivery.

The investigation further revealed that the attackers may have accessed personal information stored in Foundation’s old WooCommerce store and its current Shopify store.

For context, Foundation previously used WooCommerce before migrating to Shopify in May. Although the company had configured its system to redact customer information 60 days after an order was shipped, older copies of the records remained in legacy database tables following migrations.

Though the admin dashboard showed the information as redacted, the underlying data had not been completely removed. In fact, there is a possibility that other records, including refunds, abandoned carts, and contact forms, may also have retained personal information.

Additionally, in its second investigation update, Foundation disclosed that records containing approximately 5,600 mailing addresses and 5,200 phone numbers, along with names and email addresses, were stored in systems accessible to the attackers.

However, the company cannot confirm whether every record was accessed, so it is treating all potentially accessible information as exposed.

Now what’s shocking here is that more than 5,000 email addresses that received phishing messages were not found in its WooCommerce or Shopify customer records. This means that attackers may also have obtained information from external sources.

As of now, Foundation has revoked compromised API keys and affected email credentials. Moreover, it has also removed the malicious domain, cleared queued phishing messages, and taken affected legacy services offline.

At the same time, it is also rotating keys and secrets across its systems as a precaution. But what’s interesting here is that Foundation has found no evidence that Passport hardware wallets or the private keys stored on them were compromised.

#3238. Oracle integrates ChatGPT, Codex into apps and expands cloud chip lineup

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Oracle on Tuesday announced an expanded collaboration with OpenAI that brings the artificial intelligence lab's ChatGPT and Codex into the software maker's applications to make databases more actionable.

Mahesh Thiagarajan, Oracle's president of Cloud Infrastructure, told CNBC at Oracle AI World in Las Vegas that using ChatGPT "allows anybody to build an application, deploy it, manage it."

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman is set to join Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk on stage for an Oracle AI World keynote on Tuesday at 12:40 p.m. ET.

For OpenAI, the announcement represents another example of the frontier lab's push into the enterprise market, just one day after its key competitor, Anthropic, filed its S-1 as it heads toward an initial public offering.

Oracle's database plays a crucial role in underpinning businesses across healthcare and finance in logging and securing data.

Thiagarajan said his team observed OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra model get trained at Oracle's Stargate data center campus in Abilene, Texas, and could see the real-time benefits.

"More businesses are choosing OpenAI as we make it easier to put AI to work through the tools and systems they already use," said Greg Brockman, president and co-founder of OpenAI, in the press release.

Oracle also said Tuesday that it is making different hardware accessible on its cloud infrastructure, including Google's tensor processing unit AI chips and OpenAI's Jalapeño chip.

"Now customers can actually have infinite possibilities in terms of how they can drive AI," Thiagarajan said.

It is the first time Google's custom chips will run on another hyperscaler's cloud, and illustrates how the companies are increasingly looking for opportunities to get their hardware in the hands of the frontier labs.

Thiagarajan added that the scarcity of chips remains an ongoing issue and that customers such as OpenAI can benefit from having different accelerators to choose from. When asked whether this marks a pivot away from Nvidia GPUs, Thiagarajan said no, adding that Nvidia chips are "the best, most fungible," and that the AI market remains incredibly large.

OpenAI's Jalapeño chip and Cerebras will also run on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure. The Cerebras deal is material, but the AI chipmaker won't be disclosing the scale because it's in a quiet period, a Cerebras spokesperson said.

Cerebras stock climbed about 8% following the news. Oracle shares were up nearly 5% on Tuesday.

The announcement comes seven months after market-leading cloud Amazon Web Services announced plans to bring Cerebras hardware into its data centers, a major point of validation.

Thiagarajan also addressed Oracle's multibillion New Mexico data center dubbed Project Jupiter and the ongoing pushback it's receiving from the local community. He said Jupiter represents just one of the 30-40 different data center projects in development, per quarter, for the company.

"We're actually not too worried about what we're doing because I think we're building global infrastructure," he said.

A hearing to address air quality permitting is scheduled for Dec. 7 in Sunland Park, where the controversial data center has already broken ground. The political pushback tied to the project prompted Oracle to deliver a force majeure notice to its backer Blue Owl Capital last month. Right now, Oracle maintains that the project is on schedule.

Investors are now counting down to Oracle's investor day, where investors will want to see if the company can back its fiscal year 2030 revenue targets and if the margin profile of its cloud deals has changed due to the rising cost of capital.

Bank of America analysts wrote to clients that updates on Oracle's major data center projects are among the topics they expect clarity on at the meeting.

Oracle shares are down more than 30% this year due in part to concerns about how the company plans to finance its ambitious digital infrastructure pipeline.

In such arrangements, the fund’s portfolio management and legal entitlements remain governed by conventional fund documentation while the token layer can change how units are recorded, transferred or made available through approved distribution channels. Eligibility, investor verification and custody do not disappear simply because a ledger is public.

Money-market products are a natural test case for this technology because investors value short-duration instruments, dependable records and efficient movement of liquidity. A tokenised fund unit, however, should not be confused with a stablecoin. It is an interest in an investment fund whose value and redemption rights depend on its structure, and it may be subject to dealing deadlines, investment risks and restrictions. Token movement on a blockchain does not automatically equal cash redemption or bank settlement.

For cryptocurrency investors, the central point is to distinguish an announced milestone from an immediate change in asset value. The underlying development can strengthen a company’s product, legal position or operating capacity without determining what a token will trade for tomorrow. The linked source establishes what was disclosed; the valuation question requires a separate assessment of customers, costs, execution and market conditions. That gap between a confirmed fact and a forecast is where much of the investment risk sits.

The project broadens the set of sterling-denominated tokenised instruments at a time when asset managers are comparing public-chain settlement with familiar custody and transfer-agent arrangements. The potential business case rests on operational efficiency and distribution rather than novelty: fewer reconciliations, clearer ownership records and easier connectivity with digital-asset platforms. Each proposed benefit requires integration with real institutions and must survive both compliance reviews and ordinary market conditions.

Readers should watch for verified subscriptions, holdings in the tokenised class, investor eligibility disclosures and evidence of secondary uses such as collateral management. The underlying £22 billion headline does not establish adoption of the class on its own. Aviva’s announcement confirms the launch; the next stage is finding out whether customers actually use the new format often enough to justify its additional technology, legal work and operational controls.

Market watchers should also avoid treating a company’s or regulator’s headline as a substitute for terms and conditions. Tokens, shares and regulated service agreements create different rights, even when they coexist in one wallet or platform. A reliable follow-up should ask which entity is responsible, what a holder can actually redeem or transfer, and whether the claimed advantages appear in live activity. Those questions are more informative than an unsupported price target or an assumption that adoption is inevitable.

#3957. Virtue and a Sledgehammer review – a haunting sci-fi drama about breaking walls, AI faces, and your heart

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Outwardly about hitting things for stress relief, Virtue and a Sledgehammer is really an affecting family drama that confronts pain but doesn't give in to despair.

It’s been well over a week since my final swing of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s righteous whacking stick, and I’m still finding it difficult to shift the game from my thoughtspace. Having tempted me in with promises of cathartic, base-instinct vengeance against a town of roboticised arseholes, it’s since had me picking through the rubble of my own demolition spree, not so much looking for meaning as trying to parse the abundance of it. I’d gladly invite you to join me in the wreckage.

Virtue’s developers, Selkie Harbour and the apparent nominative determinists at Deconstructeam – previously of The Cosmic Wheel Sisterhood – say it’s a "narrative exploration" game. Which is accurate, on both counts. As pictured sledgewielder Pratelle, your most urgent interactions involve breaking shit, be it the walls of your childhood hometown or the android copies of its former inhabitants. Really though, you’re bashing with purpose beyond your (or Pratelle’s) own satisfaction: hammering away masonry will open up new nooks and paths to explore, which in turn leads to more flashback story scenes, better contextualising a rampage that might otherwise seem lacking in justification.

This is not a swift process. Pratelle’s swings are slow and deliberate, each one needing at least some charging-up, and end on a similarly lengthy recovery animation. While this imbues encounters against the more aggressive bots with some timing-based challenge, the game’s sluggishness also serves to lend some emotional weight – a means of holding your head towards the crumbling homes and the clattering humanoids. Nothing dies here unless you absolutely, intentionally will it, and once you commit, there’s scant chance to do anything but watch as it hits the dirt.

On an initial playthrough, the techniques of fellow low-polyist Mouthwashing may come to mind: that game conveyed the cruelties of its main playable character through similarly slow, purposeful interactions. As Virtue gives up more of its secrets, though, it becomes clear that while it wants you to feel Pratelle’s rage, it also considers her crashout entirely legitimate. To explain fully would mean practising in spoilers, but those narrative shards seriously are worth tracking down, each scene laying on depth and tragic complexity to Pratelle and her family – the latter being the reason she’s back in town to begin with.

Non-chronological, but coherently written and arranged flashbacks run an impressively wide gamut of wholesomeness, unease, and horror. And they wisely take place before the sci-fi element becomes prevalent in the present-day storyline, keeping the darker moments more compellingly, uncomfortably grounded. Even with a tendency towards quip-speak, especially from the mouth of Nina, Pratelle’s smug genius of a sister, the mixing of fond memories with traumatic ones heightens the impact of both. It’s naturally awful to watch Nina coldly kick Pratelle out of their mother’s hospital room, or to see that mother inflict physical abuse on the sisters, but the hurt hits different when a later scene shows all three warmly chatting at home. Based on the chatter of their robot avatars, many of the family’s neighbours are/were variously racist, homophobic, or outright murderous, but it’s the pain and betrayal seeded in those past moments that drive Pratelle’s stomping, bludgeoning campaign of violence.

Crucially, Virtue does not introduce these humanising moments to manipulate you into manufactured, Far Cry 3-ass, you-are-the-real-monster guilt, and in-story attempts to play this trick on Pratelle herself are quickly called out as such. The wrongheaded people are wrong, the game knows it, and there’s no ulterior motive beyond the suggestion that those we love (and are loved by) can be corrupted by ego, ambition, or inherited trauma. Still, that central, lamentable family dynamic, and how skillfully its members are built up and broken apart in such short vignettes, are largely why I’m still pondering the game so much. I also confess to having replayed the entire thing, in far less brutal fashion, in the hope – proved vain – that it might help change some of their fates.

For better or worse, fury is the only language this town understands, and Pratelle is a fluent speaker. The long windup of attacks is at least met by an equal and opposite reaction of object-mangling: walls blast open with an appreciably exaggerated shower of debris, and taking out a building’s corners will cause a thunderous collapse of the entire structure. Thwacking robots isn’t quite as satisfying, in part because of the underwhelmingy dull 'thunk' of the impact sound, but a solid hit will still send them tumbling backwards with a deserved lack of grace. In some delightfully fortuitous melees, a clobbered bot would ragdoll hard enough into an adjacent wall to crack it open, revealing a memory shared I could have otherwise missed.

I briefly wondered if having me collect all these was necessary, given the game has no problem auto-triggering regular cutscenes at points. Except, it kind of is. Pratelle’s journey is cathartic, but it’s also a labour, the weight and sorrow of what she must do mirrored in her leaden footsteps and determined but joyless sledgehammering. It’s entirely appropriate, then, that uncovering her full story takes some extra effort, just as that effort is entirely rewarded.

Virtue and a Sledgehammer stands against much. It’s anti-prejudice, anti-ambivalence, and in not very subtle terms, anti-AI: its depiction of a soulless (albeit human-driven) machine takeover, complete with whining about how hard life was before, throws up gladly smashable parallels to the chatbot infestation of creative and public spaces.

For all its violent opposition, though, it’s also earnestly, maybe even optimistically pro-human. While drawing a line at intentional and misguided evils, it’s accepting of our imperfections and the role they have in shaping us – remember you’re fighting about 600 identical automatons – and only plumbs the depths of true despair when humanity isn’t adequately defended. Rage is vented and steam is let off, but not in a purely spiteful, fuck-the-world manner, and even Pratelle is only tearing her past down so she can move on from it. Maybe one day I will too, if I can ever get her stupid, fascinating game out of my head.

#5543. Capacity, clarity, cash and compliance: the 4 C’s of electric co-op funding success

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Compliance is where cooperative utilities tend to struggle the most, and the latest federal funding programs have introduced even tougher terms, writes Fedsprout CEO Aalap Shah.

Aalap Shah is president and CEO of Fedsprout, which helps electric cooperatives and utilities manage federal and state awards.

If you ask a lineman what it takes for a storm restoration to go well, the answer is usually not about having the right tools or truck. Quick restoration relies on good training, a plan before getting to the first pole and adequate back-office support.

Federal funding works the same way and the latest federal programs are demanding even more.

I’ve worked with electric cooperatives across 14 states and I’ve come to realize there is a pattern. Cooperative utilities do not have an eligibility problem, and most qualify for federal and state funding programs. Yet some co-ops never apply for funding. Others win an award and lose part of it because of disallowed costs.

In its October outlook, the U.S. Energy Information Administration expects record electricity demand this year and next, and projects the average U.S. residential electricity price at 18.2 cents/kWh this year, up from 16.5 in cents/kWh in 2024. Co-ops need funding to support load growth and reliability improvements while keeping rates affordable for members.

In my experience, co-ops that win federal and state funding stand apart in four areas, before and after the award: capacity, clarity, cash and compliance.

The capacity gap is clearly visible across most rural and small co-ops. At a typical co-op, the person who writes the funding application is the same person who runs finance and human resources. They often manage the award, too.

Clarity is not as apparent. Hundreds of federal and state programs are published each year and knowing which one fits the co-op’s needs and what sways the funder needs a dedicated effort. Identifying which federal and state regulations to follow is an arduous task.

I worked with one co-op that planned to reconductor aging lines for reliability. But the approach was not competitive. Once we framed it around economic development and new local hydropower that the added line capacity would allow, the project was one of 19 selected out of 659.

Cash is a constraint for most government-funded projects, which require cost share and pay on reimbursement. Cost share can mean taking on debt, which needs board approval. For reimbursements, the co-op must first spend, sometimes using its line of credit, and then wait weeks or months to be paid back.

Compliance is where co-ops struggle the most after the award. All federal and state money comes with regular reporting, requests for information, procurement rules and written procedures, and audits. Some co-ops learn only after the award what it really requires.

The latest federal funding programs make each of these four problems harder because applicants now have to plan for more cost and higher risk.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture recently announced the Affordable Rural Cooperative program, which offers $175 million in loans and grants for nuclear power supply and transmission. On a hypothetical $100 million transmission project, the grant is capped at $18.75 million, and the co-op would need to bring in at least $25 million of its own. The remainder can be borrowed from USDA.

The final rule for the Rural Energy for America Program, published Oct. 1, compounds the cash flow problem. REAP grants for renewable energy and efficiency projects will be awarded only to completed projects that have operated for at least 12 months.

A co-op now has to finance the work and ensure compliance with federal procurement standards even before knowing whether it will receive a grant. According to the new rules, an applicant is allowed one submission during an annual window and all applications will be ranked nationally, which raises the bar. USDA staff expect the first window to open in January 2027. Co-ops that miss this window will need to wait an entire year to apply.

Federal dollars are flowing again to co-ops, but on much tougher terms.

Even so, I expect that federal programs will be limited over the next few years because of many other competing budget priorities. With less funding available, more applicants will compete for each dollar.

On energy, the current administration has made its priorities clear. These are grid reliability and resilience, more generation, affordability and reducing reliance on foreign imports. I expect programs outside those priorities to be cut back. The new REAP rule, for example, does not allow for ground-mounted solar on certified cropland and restricts systems built with Chinese-made materials.

ARC is a new USDA program aligned with the administration’s focus on nuclear power. Geothermal should also draw more support as the technology matures. In February, the U.S. Department of Energy announced $171.5 million for next-generation geothermal field tests and drilling. In October, Fervo Energy’s Cape Station in Utah became the first enhanced geothermal project of its kind to reach commercial operation.

Hydropower is worth considering for co-ops looking for increased capacity. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission approved two orders cutting environmental review for low-impact hydropower actions and DOE is releasing nearly $430 million to upgrade aging facilities.

Even with midterm elections, I don’t expect the direction to change much. Both Democrats and Republicans support affordability and reliability. A proposal built around these two pillars will have a better chance of success.

This administration has also made data centers a priority and federal support is likely to follow. But before pursuing federal or state funding, co-ops need to answer two questions: Does the load growth from a data center actually serve members? Could water scarcity or community opposition derail the project?

I have been working with a group of Texas co-ops that are first-time recipients of state grants administered by the Public Utility Commission of Texas. Three lessons stood out.

First, the co-ops struggled to understand how much detail the funding agency needed for reporting and responding to requests for information. Different agency coordinators also asked for different details. The result was a lot of back-and-forth with the agency and delays in starting the project and receiving approvals. Having plans, reports, templates and RFI responses from other projects can reduce delays because the co-op knows what to expect.

Second, not every project can start on day one of the award. Explaining schedule delays to the awarding agency can be difficult. Waiver approvals can take weeks or months and significantly delay the project. Co-ops need to start early with waiver requests and prepare detailed budgets and task schedules before the award.

Third, purchases above state or federal thresholds must be competitively bid. A trusted contractor used over the years isn’t automatically approved for a project. Justifying a sole-source or noncompetitive award takes someone well-versed in procurement policy.

The co-ops that win get ahead of the four C’s. Co-ops that prepare a short list of projects and talk to board members about cost share in advance of the funding notice are in a better position to respond with stronger proposals.

Co-ops already work with outside engineers, auditors and attorneys for specialized work. When a big storm hits, they bring in outside crews because they don’t have year-round staff to manage these worst-case events.

Similarly, grant-writing and compliance experts can better prepare co-ops for writing proposals and managing compliance. Bringing in help before the funding notice is released, and again before the project starts, is a smart move.

Storm restoration relies on good planning, a ready crew and back-office support in advance of the storm’s arrival. Federal funding rewards the same preparation because today’s programs can be a bigger storm than co-ops have faced before.
