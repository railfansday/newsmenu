#5311. Colare raises $2.5M to help firms vet engineering candidates with advanced simulations

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Engineering talent vetting startup Colare Inc. says its simulated candidate testing platform is ready for prime time today after closing on a $2.5 million pre-seed funding round backed by Afore Capital, Garage Capital and Acadian Ventures.

The company has created a simulation-based technical screening platform that companies can use to assess an engineering candidate’s ability to do the actual job they’re being hired for, before they proceed to the interview stage.

Colare co-founder and co-Chief Executive Esther Thomas (pictured, left, alongside co-founder and co-CEO Nain Abdi) told SiliconANGLE that the startup is specifically targeting the aerospace, defense, energy and advanced manufacturing industries, where companies are desperately seeking highly specialized engineers. She said that many firms in these industries are struggling to hire new staff fast enough, because their hiring processes are painstakingly slow.

Hiring engineers is especially difficult because in most cases, the only way to assess a candidate is to have them screened by an expert engineer who’s already on the company’s books. But for most companies, that means pulling that employee away from actual engineering tasks to spend hours of their time looking through resumes and conducting physical assessments of the candidate’s work.

“Hiring runs through multiple technical rounds, including multihour onsites, presentations and so on, and only engineers can conduct them,” Thomas said. “So each hiring round comes out of engineering time, often senior engineering time, which is one of the scarcest and most expensive resources at these companies.”

The challenge is made more difficult by the fact that many candidates these days are using artificial intelligence tools to polish their resumes and get past the initial remote interview stage that companies normally rely on to weed out weaker prospects. As a result, numerous unqualified candidates can slip through the net and land an in-person interview, leading to a serious drain on hirer’s engineering resources.

“Every unqualified candidate who reaches an engineering panel burns through senior engineering hours,” Thomas explained. “The high weekly interview volume, coupled with low pass rates and the high cost of engineering talent compounds into a large annual dollar cost. And a bad hire that slips through is worse still.”

Thomas said Colare’s “simulation-based engineering assessments” are designed to show how a candidate actually thinks, builds and solves problems, before they get called to an interview. This is not an easy thing to do. The process of evaluating engineering skills is especially difficult because for each problem, there can be an infinite number of solutions.

“Something like a structural bracket or a power board or a propulsion feed system has thousands of valid designs and thousands of subtly fatal ones,” Thomas said. “The difference lives in tradeoffs that machines can’t check, such as mass against stiffness, thermal margin against packaging, manufacturability against performance and cost against reliability. There is no simple answer key.”

So how do you assess these complex design choices at large scale? Thomas says the answer is to put candidates inside real engineering environments, giving them tasks to solve that are drawn from real-world flight and manufacturing constraints. “[Hirers] upload their candidate list and they can send it [the assessment] out to their entire candidate pool,” Thomas said. “Then our model will analyze every one on their process and output to present HR teams and engineers with a detailed brief of each candidate’s performance.”

Colare uses AI to map the role to hands-on, AI-generated assessments that span areas such as computer-aided design, printed circuit layouts, thermal analysis and finite element analysis. “We score the candidates’ decisions against rubrics built by senior engineers who have shipped real hardware,” Thomas said. “Every discipline demands its own domain depth. A guidance, navigation and control problem is nothing like a design for manufacturing problem.”

Colare has already been deployed by organizations ranging from startups to multibillion-dollar aerospace manufacturer, chemical engineering firms and advanced manufacturing giants. Around half of its customer base is in the aerospace and defense industries, Thomas said.

One of Colare’s customers is a chemical manufacturing startup that’s working on developing field-induced reactor technology. Initially, it only had two people on its staff, who obviously couldn’t afford to waste time screening dozens of candidates when they were looking to hire their first electrical engineer, because they also had to keep the business up and running. Yet they needed that engineer to join the team fast, Thomas explained. Moreover, the two co-founders weren’t even qualified to properly assess the candidates who applied for the role.

“One has a chemical engineering Ph.D. and the other is a business operations lead, so neither were equipped to evaluate the founding electrical engineer they needed to hire,” Thomas said. “Where they once would have relied on outside networks to vet these applicants, Colare gave them the ability to assess candidates directly.”

In another case, the subsidiary of a publicly traded manufacturing firm was preparing to advance a candidate to a final interview with its CEO, based on their performance during in-person interviews with the human resources team. But that candidate would have wasted the CEO’s precious time, for they failed Colare’s simulated assessment.

Colare will use the money from today’s round to expand its assessment library to cover more niche deep technology disciplines, and also try to build more advanced AI models that can predict how engineers will perform on complex challenges they have never encountered before.

The company’s mission is to transform the engineering hiring process, so that verifiable skills carry more weight than paper-based qualifications. “Resumes are no longer indicators of how capable someone is,” Thomas insisted. “We want to replace evaluation to make it meritocratic, so that people are hired on skill, not pedigree.”

“We’ve been talking about [leaving] for a long time,” Amir said. “Most people I know are waiting for the results of the elections to have that discussion on a more practical and serious level.”

Nearly one-fifth of Israeli voters are considering emigrating because of the country’s security and social situation, according to an Oct. 4 survey from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Other polls from this past summer showed similar results. The recent survey found that Israelis are deeply uncertain about the country’s future, with fewer than one-quarter of residents believing their children will live in a better reality.

Israelis have expressed feeling increasingly unsafe since the Oct. 7, 2023, Hamas attacks and amid Israel’s wars with Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran. The cost of living has skyrocketed, with food prices increasing by 20 percent in the last four years. Israeli society is also deeply polarized, with the secular liberal and religious-nationalist factions at odds. Netanyahu’s critics fear that his coalition, if victorious, will advance policies that favor the religious nationalists at the expense of other Israelis.

The latest polls show that it is highly possible that neither Netanyahu’s coalition nor the main opposition bloc will secure a parliamentary majority in the elections, which would yield a hung parliament and set the stage for coalition negotiations. If those efforts to secure a majority ultimately failed, Netanyahu—Israel’s longest-serving prime minister—would stay on as caretaker until a new vote can be held.

If thousands of people decide to emigrate based on the election’s outcome and aftermath, Israel could lose highly skilled workers in key sectors who are sustaining the economy and tend to belong to the country’s more secular factions. Such departures would unsettle the political balance among the remaining population, which would in turn lead to weaker internal opposition.

Many Israelis consider the upcoming parliamentary election—the first held since the Oct. 7 attacks, in which around 1,200 people were killed and 251 were taken hostage—the most important in the country’s history. Israel faces growing international isolation over its war in Gaza and rising settler violence in the West Bank. More than 74,000 people have been killed in Gaza, and much of the enclave has been destroyed since the war began.

Israeli society was polarized before the Hamas attacks in 2023. Earlier that year, Netanyahu’s government proposed a judicial reform bill that would have limited Supreme Court oversight of lawmaking and policy and, according to critics, destabilized Israeli democracy by undermining the balance of power. Thousands of Israelis took to the streets to protest the measure, creating deep rifts within the population. Ultimately, the court struck down the bill.

Such fissures only deepened after Oct. 7. Current and former Israeli military and defense officials accused Netanyahu of pursuing flawed strategies to address the hostage crisis, while the families of the hostages accused the government of inaction. Some pro-war Israelis supported extreme military pressure in Gaza, even as swaths of the population regularly protested the war.

Thousands of Israel Defense Forces (IDF) reservists have served hundreds of days in Gaza and Lebanon. Meanwhile, members of the ultra-Orthodox Jewish population—the country’s fastest-growing population group—have rarely completed military service in any capacity. In 2024, the Supreme Court ruled that Haredi men could no longer be exempted from conscription and ordered the military to begin drafting them. Yet ultra-Orthodox military service and workforce participation remain low, fueling resentment among other Israelis.

The cost of living has continued to rise, in part due to unaddressed structural issues such as high market concentration. Increased tax and utility fees have also led to cost increases, while other wartime costs have deepened the country’s debt. Israel’s tourism and construction sectors have been hit especially hard by the ongoing conflict and labor shortages resulting from conscription, as well as the suspension of work permits for Palestinians from the West Bank.

Taken together, these trends have worsened divisions between the nationalist and religious factions that support Netanyahu and his far-right allies and the more liberal, secular factions that want to see that coalition out.

The country’s coming election has become focused on the status of the country’s ultra-Orthodox at wartime.

Netanyahu’s opponents are ahead but will struggle to form a coalition.

The real action starts after polls close and the coalition wrangling begins.

Netanyahu has served as Israel’s prime minister for nearly 19 years. His Likud party is polling neck-and-neck with Yashar, the party founded last year by Gadi Eisenkot, his former military chief of staff. Eisenkot has formed an alliance with three smaller parties, but this opposition bloc is likely to fall short of an outright majority. After the election, he could seek an agreement with Israel’s two predominantly Arab opposition parties to form a minority government, though the political viability of such a deal remains uncertain.

The opposition parties blame Netanyahu for the security failures that led to the Oct. 7 attacks and for failing to eliminate threats from Hamas, Hezbollah, and Iran. They agree on the need for an independent commission to investigate the Israeli leadership’s responsibility for the attacks and have pledged to enforce mandatory conscription for ultra-Orthodox men.

Israeli journalist Amir Tibon recently wrote in The Atlantic that a victory for Netanyahu could prompt an immediate “exodus of educated, highly skilled Israelis.” Tibon moved back to his home in the Nahal Oz kibbutz this month, three years after surviving the Hamas attacks. But he said that he and his wife “struggle to imagine any kind of future here if this government stays in power” and that many of his friends and relatives have already left Israel.

Indeed, after years of relatively stable migration patterns, nearly 270,000 Israelis left the country for at least three consecutive months between 2023 and 2025, according to an August study by Tel Aviv University. That amounts to roughly 90,000 departures annually—around 50 percent above the annual average for the preceding decade.

Researchers identified increased emigration among physicians, Ph.D. holders, engineers, and individuals with relatively high incomes. In the last three years, Israel experienced a “medical exodus,” with almost 1,400 doctors leaving for a period longer than 12 months—a 94 percent surge compared with a similar period between 2013 and 2015.

Lilach Lev Ari, a professor of sociology at Oranim College, surveyed Israelis who emigrated between 2023 and 2025. She said that 20 percent of respondents reported they would never move back to Israel, roughly double what Lev Ari found in studies from previous years. “What is unique about this cohort are the security considerations, the war, and particularly the socio-political situation in Israel,” Lev Ari told Foreign Policy.

The emigration of highly skilled workers isn’t unique to Israel, but its economy may be particularly sensitive to such trends because it relies heavily on high-quality human capital, especially in tech, health care, academia, and other knowledge-intensive sectors. Further, just 20 percent of Israel’s workers fund 93 percent of all income-tax revenue, according to the Shoresh Institution; half of the country’s earners are so poor that they do not pay any income tax.

“Risk is particularly acute in Israel, where a relatively small group of a few hundred thousand highly skilled people plays a disproportionate role in sustaining the country’s economy and essential institutions,” said Itai Ater, a business economics professor at Tel Aviv University. “The departure of even a fraction of this group could trigger a self-reinforcing dynamic: As more people leave, the country’s economic prospects and essential services deteriorate, giving others stronger incentives to leave and potentially setting off a much broader wave of emigration.”

Mass emigration among this largely liberal and secular cohort could shift Israel’s demographic and political balance toward religious-nationalist factions. Over time, that shift could influence policy on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, civil liberties, judicial authority, and the relationship between religion and state. The erosion of a counterweight to hard-line forces could induce a perpetual feedback loop of polarization and further emigration.

Israeli activist Hagai Agmon-Snir and his wife left Israel for Italy this summer after decades of promoting inclusivity and equality in his home country. As a co-founder of the Jerusalem Intercultural Center, Agmon-Snir helped initiate talks between the LGBTQ and ultra-Orthodox communities, train thousands of police officers in nonviolent methods, and assist an initiative that taught tens of thousands of Israelis to speak Arabic.

Even though his quality of life in Israel was “excellent,” Agmon-Snir said he couldn’t ignore politics. “We left because Israel has become hopeless, and we felt the need to distance ourselves from that evil,” he said. He described his decision as “ideological emigration,” which he said is relatively rare compared with departures driven by fears “that Israel is going to collapse or … that their privileges are going to be lost.”

After Agmon-Snir’s story became public in an interview with Haaretz, he said that dozens of people reached out to learn more about his decision and ask for advice. He and his wife are returning to Israel to vote on Oct. 27, believing they can still help mitigate local injustices from abroad. “We chose to use our freedom and our capabilities to keep on fighting from outside the country with integrity,” Agmon-Snir said.

Giovanni Legorano is a journalist based in Italy.

As AI continues to get smarter, the schemes using it have often become more successful. However, one South Korean cyberhacking gang, reportedly led by a self-taught hacker using AI tools, doesn't seem to have made off with a king's ransom.

Despite allegedly obtaining 8.2 million records and demanding 360,000,000 Won (around $271.77K) from victims, the gang is said to have ended up receiving 300,000 Won (around $210). This is according to Yonhap News Agency, Seoul Economic Daily, and The Chosun Daily, which corroborate reports.

Altogether, the gang reportedly accessed files from over 90 firms, but 60 successful hits came from hacks using AI tools (a mixture of AI coding and an autonomous penetration testing system). The stolen data is said to have included customer information like names, registration numbers, phone numbers, and addresses.

The cyberhacking gang is also said to have sent threatening emails to those affected, saying, "If the data breach becomes public, the company’s reputation will be damaged, and government fines may be imposed." Payment info was not caught up in these breaches, and South Korean police say stolen data has not yet made it onto the dark web.

As noted by The Chosun Daily, police got to the ring fast, which might explain why only one small business appears to have paid the ransom fee. Police received a complaint from an affected company in June of this year, but the investigation officially started in August, leading to the arrest of seven individuals between July and September, including the 32-year-old alleged ringleader.

Reportedly, the ringleader majored in arts and physical education with no IT background, but learned about hacking via YouTube videos and other unnamed sources. The police believe the individuals worked on simulated hacking at a security firm between 2019 and 2022.

38 of the affected firms reportedly suffered losses, though no total monetary figure has been declared for how big those losses might be.

#1474. Carrick's gamble on Bruno may prove his last act as Man United boss but his message is clear: the lunatics have run the asylum for far too long: KIERAN GILL on My Premier League Weekend

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 12:00 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 15:05 GMT, 26 October 2026

It carried shades of Ruud Gullit dropping Alan Shearer for Newcastle United against Sunderland in August 1999. Newcastle led 1-0 at half-time. Then Shearer was introduced at 1-1. Then Sunderland won 2-1. Gullit departed the club after that Tyne–Wear derby defeat at St James’ Park.

Michael Carrick may not last much longer at Manchester United either now that he has produced his own famous axeing. One difference between Shearer and Bruno Fernandes is that the latter did not appear at all in their 3-0 loss to Bournemouth at Old Trafford - an unused substitute to the end.

United have Chelsea next, and those at Stamford Bridge are used to facing a United caretaker in his first league game in charge. Carrick in 2021: a 1-1 draw. Ruud van Nistelrooy in 2024: another 1-1 draw. Darren Fletcher in 2026? Quite possibly. All that remains is the picture of the corner flag popping up on United’s social media feed to confirm Carrick’s departure.

Yet in what was potentially his last act as United manager, Carrick took a stand. He refused to placate Fernandes, who is a fabulous footballer, if also the master of moaning. His ego may be dented for being made an example of, but in truth, this was about more than the Portuguese and his hissy fits.

His disobedience as club captain afforded Carrick the opportunity to send the message that the lunatics have been running the asylum for too long. It is a team that have chewed and spewed too many managers.

Fernandes publicly criticised his coaches’ tactics after he was substituted during their 4-1 Champions League loss at Como. He could have been dropped for poor performances sooner, but his outburst forced the issue, and he was deservedly punished for his midweek act of mutiny.

It was a risk by Carrick, and one which did not deliver him the win he would have hoped for. He is not the first, nor will he be the last, manager to potentially use his dying breath to send such a message, however.

Bruno Fernandes's axeing could prove Michael Carrick's final act... 27 years after Ruud Gullit left Newcastle after leaving Alan Shearer on the bench against Sunderland

Michael Carrick acknowledges United fans at Old Trafford after the final whistle - but insisted it was not a wave goodbye and he does not know whether he will keep his job

One of the candidates to replace him, Antonio Conte, did so at Tottenham Hotspur. His speech after their 3-3 draw at Southampton was stunning. He essentially called on the club to end its excuse culture. He went down swinging. Conte was gone a week later.

So Carrick took his own swing. He may have missed, what with losing 3-0 without Fernandes, but it was needed as a reminder to United’s players that they are not bigger than their club.

And for that, good on Carrick, who may now lose his job as a consequence. At least he will have gone out with the supporters at Old Trafford singing his name before they showed the players what they thought of them.

But if United fans want to feel a little better, Newcastle replaced Gullit with the wonderful Sir Bobby Robson, and Shearer finished that same season scoring 30 goals.

You can think Mikel Arteta is slightly strange, but in this world of managers giving mundane answers to the media we should be thankful that every so often, the man in charge of the champions is willing to provide us with new insight.

This time, after being booked during the 2-0 win over Everton, Arteta told us how he has been clipping up footage of other head coaches stepping out of their technical area during matches. He insists he has evidence that another manager did so more than 30 times in 10 minutes without receiving a yellow.

That was gold dust for the World Wide Web. A glance at one online forum threw up a ton of ideas. Set up sensors around the technical area and have managers wear shock collars! Give the fourth official a water spray to teach them a lesson like a misbehaving cat! Set up rakes around the white lines so when they step on them, they get hit like Sideshow Bob from The Simpsons!

Good suggestions, all. It would certainly give Howard Webb’s Pro Ref one fewer issue to think about when they should have bigger concerns while trying to officiate the Premier League fairly.

Mikel Arteta clashes with fourth official Ruebyn Ricardo at the Emirates on Saturday after stepping out of his technical area

Jokes aside, from an Arsenal perspective, it is good that Arteta is willing to fight the narrative that he is the only one who behaves badly in his technical area. He is not. We all know he is not, and we should believe him when he claims he can prove it to Webb and Co.

Within that dossier might now be a clip of Roberto De Zerbi celebrating Tottenham scoring by invading the pitch at Chelsea. He had already been booked for a bust-up with the opposition bench.

Had this been Arteta, we might have heard more talk of what a naughty boy he is, and how he gets away with too much. But Arsenal should be glad that their manager is happy to hit back.

United lost, so inevitably, the temptation is to purely discuss them and their troubles. But it would be wrong for us to disregard the role played by Bournemouth, who secured their fourth consecutive win without conceding despite juggling European football for the first time ever.

Thursday: 3-0 win over Sturm Graz, Europa League. Sunday: 2-0 win over Sunderland, Premier League. Thursday: 3-0 win over AC Milan, Europa League. Sunday: 3-0 win over United, Premier League. Their fixture list before the November international break has Marco Rose’s team playing Wednesday, Saturday, Thursday, Sunday in three different competitions.

Which is one hell of a slog for a side not used to all this, and the greatest challenge they face. The travelling. The recovery. The training sessions they then squeeze in. Over the last two weeks, Bournemouth have handled it with impressive ease.

If they can continue to handle this workload, it can only help the club convince their players that they should not be seen as a stepping stone.

That is what Bournemouth, led by their owner, Bill Foley, want.

They do not want to be a temporary home to the Antoine Semenyos and Illia Zabarnyis and Dean Huijsens who find their way to them. They want to get to a place where they keep them.

Chelsea found out the day before playing Tottenham that they would not have Joao Pedro, whose injury is being assessed and with no timeline yet placed on his return.

Xabi Alonso could have simply added Danny Welbeck to his starting line-up as a like-for-like replacement, a striker for a striker. But he tried something different.

He added Geovany Quenda to his line-up. It was his first start for Chelsea, and he acted as another No 10 alongside Cole Palmer while Morgan Rogers was a false nine.

With the power of hindsight, we can say it smacked of Alonso overthinking the problem as Chelsea never looked like beating Spurs with their 11th-hour shake-up. That the tactics then resembled ‘hoof and hope’ only made it more confusing that an actual striker was not used.

As for Tottenham, that first victory at Stamford Bridge since 2018 should be a turning point for De Zerbi, more so than their win at Coventry which was helped by Carl Rushworth.

The performance itself was brimming with positivity, even as they impressively coped while losing James Maddison to a second-half injury. His replacement, Conor Gallagher, was like bait for Chelsea as a former player. Palmer fouled him to receive his second yellow. Rogers was fortunate that the refereeing team did not think he deserved red for giving Gallagher a kicking of his own.

Earth almost always has more than one moon. The others are just very small, and they never stay long — but they could provide accessible and unique insights into the solar system.

Temporary satellites, or mini-moons, are small asteroids that wander into Earth’s neighborhood and get captured briefly by our planet’s gravity. A typical one stays about nine months and circles Earth roughly three times before escaping back into orbit around the sun, Grigori Fedorets of Finland's University of Helsinki said in a presentation at the Europlanet Science Congress (EPSC) 2026 in The Hague in September.

Fedorets' modeling suggests that the largest mini-moon around Earth at any given moment is typically only about 75 centimeters (30 inches) across. An asteroid about 3 meters (10 feet) wide should be captured every 10 to 20 years.

"This is not just a theoretical construct. There is observational evidence," Fedorets said. Only two mini-moons have been confirmed to date, however — 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3, both discovered by the Catalina Sky Survey in Arizona. Two fireballs, seen over the Czech Republic in 2013 and Australia in 2016, were traced back to objects that had been in geocentric orbits. Two more asteroids, 2022 NX1 and 2024 PT5, were captured only briefly, without completing a full orbit.

Fedorets noted a potential third mini-moon — the recently detected Sar2901, which could also be an artificial object. Sar2901 has since faded from view but should be observable again around mid-to-late November, according to the Minor Planet Center.

Mini-moons are so small that scientists know little about them. For objects between 1 and 10 meters (3.3 to 33 feet) across, models of the near-Earth asteroid population disagree with the numbers suggested by records of fireballs in Earth's atmosphere. Mini-moons sit right in that gap, so finding more of them should show which picture is right.

They may also reveal how much of the material in Earth’s neighborhood traces its origin back to our moon, as some small asteroids may be chunks of the lunar surface blasted into space by impacts. During the temporary flyby of asteroid 2024 PT5, for example, scientists found its spectrum to be closer to lunar rock than to typical asteroids, according to a 2025 study led by Theodore Kareta, which Fedorets described as the strongest evidence yet for lunar ejecta among Earth's small neighbors.

Kamo’oalewa, a much larger asteroid and quasi-moon of Earth, at which China’s Tianwen-2 mission arrived in July, has been the subject of debate over possible lunar or asteroidal origins.

The transformative Vera C. Rubin Observatory's 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) should find many more mini-moons, though further work will be needed. On its own, Fedorets estimates, the survey should catch a mini-moon about once every two years.

Fedorets and colleagues are setting up a dedicated system running on a virtual server at Finland's Center for Scientific Computing, to sift Rubin's alerts through the ANTARES real-time astronomical data broker to flag potential mini-moons for follow-up observations, which could raise detections to two or three per year.

Making regular discoveries could open doors for future missions, providing targets for relatively easy, low-cost asteroid sample-return missions. Mini-moons’ proximity to Earth, and their low relative speeds, means less rocket propellant is needed to get out to these diminutive targets and back to Earth.

Initially, Fedorets envisions cubesat tech demo missions, which would pave the way for mid-sized missions that could collect samples. He sees the Tianwen-2 low-energy sample mission as a potential stepping stone to resource utilization, while also noting that, at the higher end of the scale, there have been programs such as NASA’s cancelled Asteroid Redirect Mission (ARM) and plans by, for example, TransAstra to put a 100-ton asteroid into a stable orbit around Earth.

"This is not sci-fi," Fedorets said. "These people are actually thinking about this kind of stuff."

The short warning times and instability of the orbits of mini-moons dictates potential mission profiles. A spacecraft could wait in Earth-moon space, ready to head for a newly found mini-moon, similar to how ESA's Comet Interceptor will launch and then wait for a suitable comet to study. Asteroids could also be nudged into more stable orbits, allowing more extensive future missions.

"LSST is now online, and over the next 10 years, we are expected to find many more of these temporary moons," Fedorets said. Regular discoveries could pave the way for exploration in the early 2030s and sampling missions later in the decade.

#4601. Earth’s first fully-marine amphibian stalked the seafloor with a chunky, bony body

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 12:00 PM EDT

Nearly 237 million years ago, a spiky and toothy bottom dweller skulked the Late Triassic oceans that once covered present-day China. What sets this newly discovered giant salamander-sized prehistoric amphibian apart is not its impressive body–it’s that it lived in the ocean. The newly described Gerrothorax maritimus is the first example of a fully marine amphibian in the fossil record, according to a study published today in the journal Nature Ecology & Evolution.

There are currently over 8,000 species of living amphibians, and none have been known to live in fully marine (salt water) environments. Less than two percent like crab-eating frogs (Fejervarya cancrivora) and some spotted salamandars have any degree of salt tolerance. Other four-limbed animals, including birds, reptiles, and mammals, all have species who can survive without any food or water from land. There was also no evidence of a fully marine amphibian in the fossil record. Until now.

In this study, a team from the University of California, Davis in the United States and Peking University, and the Geological Museum of Guizhou in China analyzed two nearly complete skeletons found in southern China’s Guizhou Province. The fossils were found in an area once covered by ocean and hundreds of miles from any large land mass.

“This area is part of an ecosystem that we’ve been looking at and trying to reconstruct,” Ryosuke Motani, a study co-author and paleontologist at UC Davis, said in a statement. “We’ve been mostly studying marine reptiles and the fish from this place, but now we also have this amphibian and it’s a sort of very unusual member of the lineage.”

The well-preserved skeletons helped the team reconstruct what Gerrothorax maritimus looked like and offer clues to its marine life. Notably, it had a flattened body covered in bony armor. This armor likely defended it against predators like the 10-foot-long ichthyosaur that stalked these seas millions of years ago. Gerrothorax maritimus itself was roughly three feet long with a disc-shaped body.

“If you think about today’s amphibians, this animal is unusual because it’s big,” Motani said. “It’s probably, body mass-wise, about the same as the giant salamander, but what’s different is that giant salamanders are elongated whereas this animal is really chunky.”

Based on Gerrothorax maritimus’  bony armor and the shape of its skull, the team believes that it was a bottom-dwelling suction feeder, similar to the wobbegong, a family of 12 bottom-dwelling carpet shark species alive today.

“They would probably just wait at the bottom and then when prey comes around, they would just suck it in,” said Motanti.

Modern amphibians can’t live in marine environments because their skin does not tolerate salt. The skin is permeable, and allows them to use it for multiple purposes like respiration. Amphibians don’t drink or breathe through the same mouth opening since they can do both just using their skin.

Due to this salt-intolerant biology, they must live in or near freshwater. While some modern species like the crab-eating frog have some degree of salt tolerance, they will lose weight in brackish waters and must recover in freshwater.

The researchers propose that bony armor and a more fish-like body helped Gerrothorax maritimus circumvent this limitation. The armor would have kept its skin from absorbing water, meaning that it needed to drink salt water with its mouth, and then absorb it through its digestive tract. The creature’s gills would have allowed it to filter out the salt. Other species in the Gerrothorax also have gills.

According to the team, the fossils’ location within deep sea sediments and the fact that they were nearly complete led them to believe that Gerrothorax maritimus was an ocean-dweller.

“Carcasses at sea, unless they sink to the bottom right away, tend to dry out and lose limbs prior to sinking to the bottom,” said Motani. “But these animals had their fingers preserved, so that means they probably died near where they lived.”

Range Energy, a company working to electrify trucking through the use of a trailer with its own battery and motors, has published an analysis showing how much fleets can save through electrification – and just at the right time, with diesel at record highs.

You may have heard a little something about diesel prices lately, which have just about doubled in less than a year due to Donald Trump’s war of choice in Iran.

Diesel is now at record highs, and it’s devastating fleets and truck drivers and anyone who uses any products that are delivered on trucks (so, everyone). It’s an urgent problem that needs an urgent solution, and solutions are not forthcoming from the responsible entities.

So, fleets have to seek another solution, and Range Energy would like to be the one that provides it.

Range is a startup based in California, founded and staffed by former Tesla employees and others from around the EV industry. It’s building an electrified trailer which we demoed at ACT Expo in 2024 (really, watch it, it’s a very cool demo) which promises to cut fleet fuel use significantly.

It does this by having its own battery and motors inside the trailer, meaning the tractor doesn’t have to work nearly as hard as the trailer can effectively pull its own weight. Even though the tractor still runs on diesel, it uses a whole lot less of it.

This differs from the other popular option for fleets: full electrification. There are a number of electric semi tractors available, and fleets have been moving to install them, getting their depots ready for charging. But Range lets you bring your own tractor, and electrifies the trailer instead.

Range previously published some early findings, showing a 36% reduction in fuel use in a testing scenario. It also did a pilot program with an egg farm in California, which improved the fuel efficiency of the farm’s diesel fleet by 50-70%.

Now Range has published its own white paper showing comparative savings that fleets can achieve with either its own solution, the eTrailer, or Tesla’s solution, the fully electric Tesla Semi. The paper does not cover the various other electric semi tractors available on the market today, just a face-off between Range and Tesla.

The analysis goes through several scenarios, mostly focusing on refrigerated trailer units (“reefers”), which is the first product Range is focusing on.

Trailer electrification is a great idea for reefers, because they usually fill up on volume rather than weight (called “cubing out”), meaning battery weight doesn’t eat into maximum trailer payload. Batteries also offer power to run refrigeration, meaning you don’t need to have a diesel generator on the trailer.

The upshot of the whole thing is that: fleets can save money with the Range eTrailer, and can theoretically save more money with the Tesla Semi, but the numbers are relatively close in the most common scenario, and the Tesla scenarios have a much wider spread depending on what implementation you consider.

For example, if you only use public chargers rather than your own depot, the Tesla Semi will be more costly because energy will be more expensive to your fleet than if you bought it at commercial utility rates. So, even though installing chargers in your depot sounds expensive, it will pencil out better in the end.

The best money savings came from having two Semis share the same charger, but that requires tight timings and overnight driving.

Another important feature Range points out is build time for a project. The nice thing about the eTrailer is it can slot into fleets immediately with little operational change required, whereas the larger charging needs of an electric tractor) tend to require bigger projects to get a depot ready with chargers (but despite the higher upfront costs, you get better long-term savings, since you avoid diesel entirely with an electric tractor).

In the end, Range found that its solution saves a little more money than the Tesla Semi does in most scenarios, but the difference is quite narrow, with each option having its strengths and weaknesses. Both solutions are better than diesel in most scenarios, but not all of them.

You don’t necessarily need to choose between the two, as an electrified trailer can still be used with an electric tractor. It works the same way in either case – using its own motors to take some of the effort off the tractor. This means extended range, for longer hauls with light loads that couldn’t quite make it on the Tesla Semi’s inherent range. And the benefit of being able to run the reefer while disconnected from the tractor or shore power remains as well.

The analysis has plenty of limitations. It has to estimate future diesel costs (it used a number below current prices, but still historically high), didn’t cover financing costs, it doesn’t have real-world data for the Tesla Semi, didn’t cover electrified tractor or trailer options from other companies, can’t analyze individual charging costs site-to-site since each is specific to a utility company, didn’t cover the myriad incentives available in various regions, and most of all it didn’t consider diesel emissions, which are a huge societal cost to everyone that electrification reduces (and full electrification reduces more).

So in the end, every fleet has a different use case, and should run the numbers themselves to see how either solution would fit in. But in the generalized case, it looks like there are plenty of savings available out there, whether through tractor or trailer electrification.

Bruno Fernandes’ mutinous post-match comments at Como were widely and correctly condemned, most pertinently by an Old Trafford crowd who showed, once again, that their loyalty towards whoever is currently Manchester United manager is perhaps their most unique and commendable trait.

But just because Bruno shouldn’t have said it doesn’t mean the point itself was incorrect. Ultimately Fernandes was right – and the way Carrick deployed Mason Mount on Sunday afternoon tells us that, deep down, he knows it.

“Sometimes when you give too much of the ball to these teams, they get confidence,” Fernandes said on Tuesday. “I think sometimes we have to be more brave under pressure, take the risk of pressing high and making them uncomfortable.”

Sitting back rather than pressing high: here is the simplest tactical summation of modern football’s great divide between the possession-heavy, hard-pressing super-clubs and the more low-block, counter-attacking teams who take them on. As Bruno suggests, Manchester United really ought to be in the former, not the latter.

Carrick, like Ole Gunnar Solskjaer before him, has coached Manchester United with the broad tactical brushstrokes of Sir Alex Ferguson, who, genius adapter that he was, would be the first to acknowledge that these old tactical ideas (midblock 4-4-2; counter-attack quickly down the flanks) are no longer suitable for a club of United’s stature.

The data confirms that Carrick’s Manchester United have played like a Fergie team. But it’s 2026, and that makes Carrick a man out of time.

Clearly, the 3-0 defeat on Saturday had little to do with tactics. Nevertheless, Carrick’s plan was revealing, because by deploying Mount on the left – but instructing him to invert into the centre – Carrick created narrowness where usually he would want width on both flanks, preferring Giggs/Ronaldo/Nani types to explode on the break.

As we inch closer to 2027, more details are surfacing about the 2027 Chevrolet Silverado and GMC Sierra, and the latest development in the models’ spec sheets is an improved ride.

General Motors will roll out an upgraded version of its Adaptive Ride Control (ARC) suspension. While the technology is already present in previous model years, the next-generation pickup trucks will feature an upgraded version that improves ride quality and handling.

This is great news on top of new powertrains, new features, and other new things for the 2027 models of GM’s full-sized twins. Some will be skeptical, some may expect a recall, but the news looks good for now.

Otherwise known as ARC, for short, is GM’s play on Continuous Damping Control (CDC). The system can change suspension damping on the fly, making it slower or faster and improving ride quality across road conditions. The system relies on wheel position sensors, a dedicated control module, and an actuator that opens and closes a valve in the dampers to continuously adjust suspension damping while driving.

It’s a cheaper, less complex version of Magnetic Ride Control (MRC) technology, which trickled down from supercars and found its way into more “normal” cars. While MRC is the top of the heap in the adaptive suspension space, it’s more expensive and requires magnetorheological fluid. ARC, meanwhile, uses traditional dampers with a valve and an electronic actuator.

An additional wheel position sensor has been added, bringing the total from three to four in the entire system. The new sensor will be positioned at the rear of the Silverado and Sierra and will provide another data stream to the computer, giving it an even better picture of the damping the suspension needs to deliver.

With more “resolution,” so to speak, more data streams will enable more precise adaptability. While the current system is already good enough, an extra layer of information would enable further optimizations.

GM’s Executive Chief Engineer for the 2027 Silverado and Sierra weighs in. Mark Dickens said in an interview with GM Authority that “you really have to drive it. The tuning improvements with the extra sensor deliver substantial improvements in ride and handling.” Hopefully, the Autoblog team can feel those improvements when GM unleashes its new full-sized twin trucks soon.
