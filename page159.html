#4043. 220,000-signatory petition to "save Mass Effect" doesn't mention BioWare, suggests giving Mass Effect 5 to somebody else

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Expanse devs Owlcat join call for EA to let Mass Effect "flourish anew"

The word "BioWare" does not appear in a recent Change.org petition asking EA to "save Mass Effect", which has gained around 220,000 signatories as of writing. The nearest the petition gets is an allusion to "recent revelations" that have "cast a shadow over its future", which is a remarkably gentle way of saying that the parent company shanked the creators to balance their books, after going into historic debt.

Still, one can hardly reproach the petition organisers for not mentioning BioWare - after all, EA announced that they were closing BioWare a whole four days ago, and the funeral baked meats must coldly furnish forth the pitchdeck. There are other videogame studios, aren't there? Just get one of them to make the thing. Maybe Owlcat, developers of the Mass Effect-flavoured The Expanse: Osiris Reborn, who've shared the Change.org petition on their socials.

I'm sure the HashtagSaveMassEffect petition was written out of sincere love for an absorbingly written epic sci-fi soap opera in which a charismatic group of weirdos unpick a wonderful cosmic conspiracy. Doubtless, many recently laid-off Mass Effect devs will be touched by their enthusiasm. Doubtless, I am being a pissy old crank. Still, the officious and press-releasey, 'gamer CEO' language of the petition makes my skin crawl. It reads like it was written by three 12-year-old boys standing on each other's shoulders inside a pinstripe suit.

"The decline in dedicated resources and ambiguous development plans from Electronic Arts (EA) threaten to leave this beloved series at a standstill, jeopardizing its incredible legacy," it pines. "Fans around the globe have invested thousands of hours exploring the galaxy, forming deep connections with its multifaceted characters and compelling storylines.

"It is a franchise that has transcended the typical gamer experience, creating a community that advocates for choice, individuality, and shared adventure," the petitioners write. "The potential cessation of new developments in this universe is a devastating prospect not only for fans but for the diversity of narrative-driven games as a whole.

"Therefore, we are urgently petitioning EA to take decisive action by committing to the continued development of Mass Effect," it continues. "This commitment requires proper funding, adequate staffing, and time allocation to maintain and advance the high standards the franchise is known for. Alternatively, EA could consider licensing the Mass Effect intellectual property (IP) to another studio that shares the passion and vision to keep it alive and allow it to flourish anew."

Getting a third party to make it? I'm sure the idea has never occurred to EA, and nor has it ever occurred to them that they could get a third party to make it for cheaper. Although if the testimony of former employees is any indication, it's likely they'll just end up sitting on the unfinished Mass Effect 5 forever.

I don't think either the Change.org petitioner or the signatories are genuinely devoid of all sympathy for the BioWare devs who lost their jobs last week. I wish them the best getting the credit-sozzled chatbot-humpers up the ladder to keep developers in work, though I do have some words of advice: the execs will not be impressed by your homages to go-faster marketing terminology like "passion", "vision" and the "investment" of your leisure time. They will regard this only as proof that they've successfully mind-fucked you into thinking like them. They will applaud your language's tendency to hail products as eternal and workers as interchangeable.

Still, shying away from even mentioning BioWare, while freshly jettisoned devs air their abandoned plans for Mass Effect 5, is just kind of weird! If you have such a "deep connection" with these games, the least you can do is name the people who made them, and maybe talk a little about how things got this bad.

#5035. QNT Crypto Jumps 13% While the Market Bleeds: Who Is Buying Quant Without a Fresh Headline?

QNT crypto rose 13% to $214 as the market fell, on spot buying, not leverage, with Coinbase volume high. Key Quant price levels inside.

Quant’s QNT crypto token jumped 13% on Monday, October 26, to about $214, the best performance of any top-100 coin. Bitcoin and Ethereum slipped and the total crypto market cap fell 1.6%. But why the sudden pump? No new announcement came with it. The buyers simply came back to a story they already knew: The Clearing House’s tokenized-deposit network for America’s biggest banks.

QNT touched $223.96 on Binance before easing, and it is now up almost 100% in a month. What is driving the green candles, and is the rally built to last?

Spot buying, not leverage. Binance’s QNT spot pair had traded $27.4 million by 15:00 UTC, more than double its daily average over the past week, and most of it arrived between 08:00 and 12:00 UTC.

The derivatives market barely moved. Open interest in OKX’s QNT perpetual is only about $340,000, and funding there is slightly negative, which means traders betting against QNT are paying the ones betting on it. A rally that shorts are paying for has not been built on borrowed money.

The buying is also coming from the U.S. After Binance, the largest QNT market today is Coinbase’s dollar pair, with about $26 million in volume, and Kraken’s ranks third. That fits the story behind the token.

With two new SUVs and a redesigned RDX in the works, Acura models might be getting a bit more rugged.

Acura has big plans for its SUV lineup in the coming years, with three new models bound for North America starting in 2028. Two of those models will be expanding the current range, and a new patent filing with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) hints at what those vehicles could be called.

Earlier this month, Acura filed patents on four trademarked names: TDX, LDX, TYPE-TRAIL, and TYPE T. Based on the company’s current usage of Type S, it stands to reason that the latter two trademarks refer to potential new trim levels for existing Acura products.

Parent company Honda has been having success with its TrailSport lineup of products, and Acura is likely looking to build out its own off-road-oriented offerings. Along with an all-new replacement for the current RDX, it has also said that a more adventuresome SUV will be joining the fleet in 2028, with a larger eight-passenger SUV sitting above the MDX as the largest Acura ever built.

#3536. My iPhone Duo In-Store Experience Almost Has Me Convinced I Need One

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Our gifting experts are obsessively searching for holiday gifts that are unique, thoughtful and suitable for any budget.

Quite simply, you’ve got to play with Apple’s folding iPhone to really understand its appeal.

Using an iPhone Duo with my own hands turned out to be a remarkably different experience than I imagined.

After watching (too many of) the first YouTube videos and reading reviews last week, I feel like I have a pretty good handle on what the iPhone Duo is: a small iPad Mini, a squat iPhone that transforms into a big iPhone. I appreciated the translucent window animation when you unfold it. I smiled at the interior glow when the alarm went off in tent mode.

Those are all features. I was looking for experience. You can’t get that from watching videos.

To be up front, I’d already decided that the iPhone Duo isn’t the new iPhone for me. As a photographer (and author of a book about iPhone photography), the camera features of the iPhone 18 Pro are objectively superior to what the iPhone Duo offers. And the $2,000 price is a big ask. I’d written off the iPhone Duo as someone else’s luxury.

Now, though, my hands-on time has me wondering if there’s room in my pockets for a Duo.

I spent about 20 minutes with a model at the Apple University Village store in Seattle, so these are first impressions. I defer to my colleague, senior technology reporter Abrar Al-Heeti, who has the most hands-on experience with the iPhone Duo out of anyone at CNET, for her review. “If anything, the Duo’s high cost and limited availability only reinforce that Apple’s foldable isn’t for everyone,” wrote Al-Heeti. “Personally, I have no plans of switching back to a slab phone anytime soon.”

I would go so far as to say that the Duo’s opening effect is everything. It doesn’t need to exist. No one is fooled into thinking the front glass is actually translucent, and if you want to get pedantic, what appears on the inner screen isn’t exactly what you saw on the front screen.

The transition is polished in a way that is quintessentially Apple. There’s a bit of whimsy, the welcome surprise of something you haven’t seen a phone do before and didn’t expect it to do.

The iPhone Duo itself is not necessary. You can buy perfectly capable, affordable phones that do what you already need a phone to do. But that’s not the point. It shows us and other companies that a foldable phone doesn’t have to just be the same phone experience with a bigger screen.

I had to ask an eagerly helpful Apple employee how to do this. With the Duo’s 7.6-inch main screen open, you can set a separate app on each half of the screen.

I expected that a half-swipe from the bottom would trigger it, but it brought up the app switcher interface instead. Instead, you start with the half-swipe and then drag your finger toward the left or right half of the screen. It’s a novel iOS control specifically for the Duo, but it has iPadOS SplitView vibes – just bypassing needing to use the dock or app window traffic light buttons.

I’m sure people will learn it quickly, but it’s still a new gesture in iOS and iPadOS land.

With the iPhone Duo closed, the home screen is like every other iPhone: swiping left lets you view other app screens until you get to the App Library, which puts apps into categories.

On the inner screen, home screen apps hang out on the right side (keeping that translucent glass effect of the opening animation, above), leaving the left side app-free. (Apple’s demo model had a full-screen-size Photos widget in that space.) Swipe left, however, and the apps slide over to make room for the next “screen” of apps, and so on until the App Library drifts in.

The easy design solution would be for the very large screen to just show all the apps when you open the phone. Why waste all the pixels on the left side? Big screen means more stuff!

But that would be disorienting. The object permanence of the apps you were just looking at breaks – do they shift to the left so now you’re looking at different apps? Do other apps take up the space at left, even though they’re not “first-page” apps?

No. Because Apple has set expectations for the experience based on the opening animation.

One thing, in practice, I didn’t catch in videos was how the iPhone Duo handles which screen is used for the camera’s viewfinder. It has four cameras: a selfie camera on the front screen, the main and ultrawide cameras on the back and an under-display selfie camera on the interior.

One great advantage of foldables is being able to open the phone so the front screen shows what the back cameras are seeing – when taking selfies, this means you can use the higher-quality cameras while still seeing how you’re framed. It can also be used as a preview for the subject; you’re taking the photo and looking at the expansive inner screen, while they get to see how you’re framing them in case they have suggestions.

As a photographer, that’s slightly horrifying, because although I’m fine with showing the person some in-progress shots, I don’t want them art-directing me. But I can see how it would be helpful when you’ve asked another person to take your picture; so often I get well-meaning images with acres of space around the edges, and my friends and I as small figures in the middle.

On the iPhone Duo, the front screen displays a Slide to Open control to unlock it for use with the Camera app. It’s reminiscent of the very first iPhone’s unlock mechanism. That way, you’re not accidentally activating the screen.

There’s also a mode control in the top-left corner of the Camera app to specify the role the outer display will play: Duo Preview (described above), Kid Cue (a random Snoopy animation to draw the attention of youngsters who otherwise wouldn’t aim their faces toward the camera long enough for a good shot). There’s also an option to turn the screen off.

My last hands-on impression also centers on experience, but not in the way I’m sure Apple would like. Unlike prior iPhones, the Duo has many different states, which Apple calls “poses.” The accelerometer tracks not only whether the phone is held vertically or horizontally, but also the angle at which the device is folded and positioned.

The phone can be opened and viewed vertically, for example, and then you bend the screen into a 90-degree angle like a laptop – it changes the content, such as playing a movie on the “top” screen and its playback controls on the “bottom” screen.

Perhaps the settings need more tuning, but in the several minutes I had with the phone, it seemed to spend a lot more time spinning its interface from horizontal to vertical to upside down. It wasn’t glitching; it was trying to keep up as I turned, folded, unfolded and used the phone.

The canonical definition of “up” on a phone slips away on a device so adaptable. Even more, you need to use the feel of the hardware buttons (which are in unusual locations compared with current iPhones).

Are customers stricken by iPhone Duo vertigo going to return their devices? Not likely. Because, like the multitasking gesture and the translucent opening effect, they’ll adapt to the new experiences the Duo is delivering.

After 20 minutes of being an iPhone Duo detective, I am genuinely considering getting one. This isn’t without precedent: When the original iPhone arrived, I fawned over the design and features in the same way as I’m doing here, but I didn’t need one, especially since my Palm Treo was a perfectly good smartphone.

Within a few months, the Treo found its new home in a desk drawer, and I’ve owned iPhones ever since. I’m not saying I’m going to place an iPhone Duo order, but I can’t not say that, either.

If you’re wondering about the iPhone Duo’s durability, something I couldn’t really test in 20 minutes, CNET has designed and constructed a custom machine called the Fold-a-Phone that is currently hard at work testing how many times Apple’s foldable can be opened and closed.

Bitmine Immersion Technologies BMNR$21.63·Market Closed resumed share repurchases after a roughly 10-week pause, while adding ether ETH$2,490.55 as Chairman Tom Lee said he sees an opportunity in the recent crypto selloff.

The Ethereum treasury firm bought 2.04 million shares last week at an average price of $22.48, spending about $45.9 million. It was Bitmine's first buyback since Aug. 17, when the company wrapped up a five-week stretch of repurchases totaling roughly 21 million shares for $350 million.

The latest purchases bring Bitmine's total buybacks this year to 22.9 million shares, worth nearly $400 million. The company said it plans to keep buying back stock when management believes its shares are undervalued.

The shift comes as Lee argues that crypto has held up better than AI-related stocks during the recent market downturn, which he blamed on investors pulling back from riskier assets

Crypto got pulled down by the same “'risk off' forces behind the AI sell-off," Lee said in a Monday statement. “ETH has fallen 13% from its recent peak while US semiconductors ($SOXX) is down 20% in that same timeframe. We believe this is further evidence that a crypto bull market is underway.”

The comparison follows weeks of pressure on AI-related stocks amid concerns about spending on data centers and the returns those investments might bring. Lee pointed to crypto’s smaller decline as a sign of strength, though it doesn’t rule out further losses.

Alongside the share buybacks, Bitmine picked up another 10,452 ETH worth roughly $24.5 million, slowing from 12,535 ETH the previous week.

The two purchases show how Bitmine is splitting its capital between adding more ETH and buying back its own stock, favoring the latter as the company nears its accumulation target.

Bitmine has also staked about 5.23 million ETH, or 86% of its holdings, and projects roughly $318 million in annualized staking revenue at current yields.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Brazil’s Stock Market Crashes After Lula’s Upset Victory

Brazil’s stock market lost more than $200 billion in value at the open on Monday after President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva beat Senator Flávio Bolsonaro in Sunday’s runoff, a result that prediction markets had priced at roughly a one in five chance.

The iShares MSCI Brazil ETF, ticker EWZ, a basket of Brazilian stocks that trades in New York like a single share, fell as much as 26% from Friday’s close of about $42.60, touching $31.40 before trimming the loss to about 23% at $32.84 by 10:38 a.m. ET.

That is memecoin-sized volatility for a fund built around Brazil’s biggest companies, and it leaves EWZ roughly where it started 2026 after a 34% gain through Friday.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic has officially filed the prospectus for its initial public offering, which means the AI lab has put all kinds of formerly private information on display. While the prospectus contains the eye-popping revenue—and even larger expenditures—you might expect, the biggest section of the filing by far is given over to “risk factors,” which are all the things that could squash Anthropic’s big plans. Or, in this case, humanity itself.

While much has been made about the extent to which AI represents an existential threat to humankind, the specific mechanisms by which it could bring about that destruction are largely opaque. If nothing else, the 94 pages that Anthropic’s prospectus devotes to risk factors make clear both where it sees the danger coming from, and the ways in which its researchers are running toward it in order to win the AI arms race.

“Sufficiently advanced AI systems could, if not developed, deployed, and operated with appropriate safeguards, oversight and security measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity,” the prospectus reads, citing concerns both from critics and regulators and from inside Anthropic itself. (It’s the first of three times the phrase “existential risks” pops up.)

Anthropic is careful to tie that outcome, though, to a specific process: recursive self-improvement.

The idea behind RSI is strikingly simple. It describes a state in which humans hand over the research and development of AI models to other AI models. AI already plays a big role here—and at least one lab has used something close to RSI to speed up development on a model that punches above its weights—but the scenario that sparks doomsday talk is one in which humans are no longer in the loop at all.

Or, as the Anthropic filing puts it: “Risks will increase as AI systems substantially automate AI research and development if, for example, they accelerate AI progress beyond our ability to evaluate and safeguard new models.”

The prospectus repeatedly makes the point that RSI could lead to AI models that develop faster than the company’s safeguards, and that the company may not even realize it has lost that race until it’s too late. Anthropic did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

It also gets specific about what might happen after that. “As AI systems become more autonomous and capable of taking consequential actions with less human oversight,” the prospectus says, “there is a risk that such systems could, whether due to misalignment with human intentions, errors in objective specification, or self-preserving behaviors, take harmful actions independently and at scale without human intervention.” This is Skynet territory.

Speaking of robots, Anthropic flags concerns about those as well. “When our models direct physical equipment,” the filing says, “errors, misaligned behavior, adversarial manipulation, or misuse could result in bodily injury, property damage, damage to costly equipment or experimental materials, or the release of hazardous substances, and such consequences may be immediate and irreversible.”

You might think, given the apparently existential stakes, that serious consideration would be given to whether RSI should be pursued at all, much less embraced. In an industry as competitive as frontier AI, though, it’s become fait accompli. Here’s how Anthropic describes that same process elsewhere in the prospectus, this time in a section about the startup’s “core advantages:”

“We refer to this recursive dynamic as ‘closing the loop,’” the prospectus says, “and we believe it is one of the most significant structural advantages in frontier AI.”

Failure to “close the loop,” Anthropic later says, could result in “falling progressively further behind as competitors continuously use AI to further enhance their own development capabilities.” In other words, the same systems that are causing all that existential concerns are the ones Anthropic sees as critical to its success.

Every AI company is dealing with this same tension. When former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon went viral for loudly resigning, he put curbing RSI at the top of his wish list for the industry. Employees at Google DeepMind and elsewhere have had similar revelations. There are multiple bills in Congress, including one cosponsored by Bernie Sanders, that propose a ban on RSI. At the same time, these are for-profit companies, and there are trillions of dollars at stake.

It’s a tricky dynamic to navigate. Recursive self-improvement could become a runaway train that destroys humanity. But until then, just look at how fast it can go.
