#5207. Microsoft offers voluntary buyouts to 60,000 employees amid AI investments

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft Corp. today issued voluntary buyout offers to about a quarter of its workforce.

The approximately 60,000 workers affected by the move are located in the U.S., Canada, India and the U.K. They reportedly all have a score of 70 or below on the company’s internal experience scale. That means the most senior employees affected by the job cuts are vice presidents.

Voluntary buyout programs give workers advanced notice of layoffs. Staffers who indicate that they wish to leave early usually receive more generous severance terms than in a standard restructuring initiative.

In the case of Microsoft’s buyout program, employees will have until early December to accept the offer. Workers who opt in will leave later that month.

Some marketing, sales and engineering teams have reportedly decided against participating. However, it’s unclear which specific business units are set to see job cuts.

It seems likely that Microsoft’s loss-making Xbox video game business is among the impacted groups. After the company let go 1,600 of the division’s employees in June, GeekWire reported that additional layoffs were expected.

According to Business Insider, workers with a score of 64 or below on Microsoft’s experience scale will receive one week of pay for every six months spent at the company. Staffers at levels 65 to 67 will receive two weeks. Microsoft will also provide stock vesting for six to 12 months, continued healthcare coverage for a limited amount of time and outplacement support.

“The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us. We’ll learn from it,” Microsoft Chief People Officer Amy Coleman wrote in a memo to employees. “At the same time, we’ll keep helping people focus on the work that matters most, build new skills and, where it makes sense, find new opportunities across Microsoft.”

The company is trimming its headcount despite the fact that it delivered strong financial results last quarter. Microsoft’s revenue grew 18% year-over-year in the three months ended June, to $90.01 billion, while its adjusted earnings of $4.74 per share topped the consensus estimate by more than 12%.

The job cuts may be part of an effort to make more funds available for Microsoft’s data center buildout. The company opened 31 new data centers last quarter, more than half the total number of cloud facilities it had at the start of the year. Microsoft expects to end 2026 with $175 million in capital expenditures. That’s down from the $190 billion the company projected by April, but still significantly above its 2025 spending.

Custom chips play an important role in the company’s’ efforts to reduce its data center costs. Earlier this month, Microsoft debuted an inference accelerator called Maia 300 that it says provides 40% more tokens per megawatt than leading graphics cards. The company revealed that it plans to make the Maia chip series its primary source of inference capacity.

Enterprise AI governance is moving into the data, workflows and controls that determine what automated systems can do. As agents take on consequential work in finance, audit, compliance and sustainability, organizations need results that are grounded in business context, traceable to their sources and governed by clear accountability.

TheCUBE’s preview of Workiva’s Amplify event highlighted the controls needed as AI shifts from recommending actions to taking them. Trustworthy automation also depends on connecting information with the business conditions surrounding it, according to theCUBE Research’s Krista Case.

“What I’m looking at as Workiva’s bigger opportunity is creating that context and being that connective tissue across those disparate platforms and bringing in that more tribal knowledge … to make sure that as we are starting to ask AI to actually execute functions, it can be trusted because it does increasingly have that context behind it,” she said. “They are historically very strong in things like compliance and reporting and very regulated and audited workflows. They are beginning to make some steps into areas like sustainability.”

Case and co-host Alison Kosik spoke with executives and practitioners at Workiva’s Amplify event, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. As AI agents move from assisting employees to carrying out regulated work, governance must extend beyond the quality of the underlying data. Companies also need an auditable record that supports accountability after a task is completed, according to Case.

“We need to understand things like: Where did this information and … these insights come from? Who approved a particular action? Can you maybe trace what happened if an AI agent is taking an action on your behalf?” Case said. “When you think about finance and these other regulated processes, it’s not really good enough for the action or the response to just look plausible. We have to really make sure that it can be substantiated.”

The harder design question is how much independence to grant an agent without surrendering meaningful oversight. The answer will depend on the risk and business use case, with monitoring and escalation helping organizations maintain control.

“What’s interesting is that going back to the conversation around speed, if a human has to review and approve every action, then really the whole point or much of the value is moot,” she said. “We need to kind of make decisions around when AI is assisting a human, when it can execute on its own [and] when it needs approval. I do think that some of these boundaries are still being defined, and I think they’re going to evolve and change over time, especially as the business use cases evolve.”

Workiva’s 2026 Midyear Executive Benchmark Survey exposes a central challenge for enterprise AI governance: the gap between confidence and readiness. Eighty-four percent of surveyed executives were at least somewhat confident in AI outputs appearing in annual reports without human review, yet only 11% said their data quality was sufficient for AI use. Established financial controls can help organizations make AI-assisted reporting more accountable by documenting four elements: where data originated, who touched it, what happened to it and how it was reviewed and approved. That accountability remains necessary as AI accelerates reporting, according to Steve Soter, vice president and industry principal at Workiva.

“It actually makes it even more important because accelerating a process, if you don’t have it grounded by … those four things, then speed doesn’t become an asset,” he said. “It really becomes a liability. It becomes a risk.”

#5219. Intel buys quantum software pioneer Classiq for $2.2B

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Intel Corp. said today it has agreed to the terms of a blockbuster acquisition for the quantum computing software startup Classiq Technologies Ltd.

It’ll pay $2.2 billion to buy the Israeli company, making it the second-largest acquisition in its history after its $15.3 billion deal to buy Mobileye back in 2017.

Classiq has likened itself to the Microsoft of the quantum computing world, having developed an operating system that enables the execution of complex algorithms on a range of different quantum computers. Its software is agnostic to the wide range of quantum machines currently being developed by companies such as IBM Corp., Google LLC and IonQ Inc. That includes systems that use trapped ions, superconductors, photons, carbon nanotubes, topological networks and the spin qubit-based system developed by Intel.

The startup’s software is designed to run on top of specialized processors that were developed specifically for quantum algorithms by a company called Quantum Machines Ltd. The operating system serves as a platform for developers to design, build, test and run software designed to run on any kind of quantum computer.

For instance, BMW used Classiq’s platform to develop quantum software that’s used to simulate car aerodynamics, so they can be modeled in different scenarios, such as where various components are built using different materials. The idea is that by stress-testing different ideas in an ultra-realistic simulation, it can avoid the need for more expensive real-world testing.

Another customer is HSBC Holdings Plc, which used Classiq’s platform to develop a quantum algorithm for fraud detection. Meanwhile, the Italian bank Intesa Sanpaolo used Classiq to build an application that allows it to price options over shorter timeframes to increase its profitability per transaction.

One of the most notable things about today’s acquisition is that Intel Chief Executive Lip-Bu Tan was one of the earliest investors in Classiq, participating in its $33 million Series B round of funding in February 2022. He returned to participate in the company’s $110 million Series C in May 2025. All told, Classiq has raised just over $200 million in funding.

Intel said Classiq will become its global development center for quantum computing. It intends to combine Classiq’s platform with its existing quantum spin-qubit manufacturing process to “create a user-friendly full-stack quantum computing platform” that will enable quantum technology to mature as a mainstream computing business.

In a statement, Intel likened Classiq’s technology to electronic design automation and computer-aided design software, saying that it could have the same impact on quantum computing that those technologies had on chip design and advanced engineering. “Providing this high-level abstraction enables quantum computing to scale beyond custom hand-crafted circuits and applications to become a mainstream computing platform,” the chipmaker said.

The deal is not just a bet on the future of quantum computing, but also a major move by Intel into the world of operating systems. In some ways, it’s reminiscent of Google’s acquisition of Android 2005, a move that enabled the search engine giant to gain an entry into the nascent smartphone industry. Android has since become the world’s most popular smartphone operating system by far, giving Google substantial influence over various aspects of the industry, including hardware development.

Classiq isn’t the only company developing operating systems for quantum computers, though. Intel’s biggest rival now will likely be IBM Corp., which is not only seen as a leader in terms of quantum hardware, but also software.

IBM has developed the Qiskit platform, which also enables hardware-agnostic quantum application development. Qiskit hasn’t been around as long as Classiq’s platform, but IBM’s brand recognition and status as a quantum pioneer gives it strong credibility as a solid alternative.

Intel said the acquisition is subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approvals, but if all goes well, it expects the deal to close during the first half of next year.

The NFL rarely announces two weeks' worth of schedule changes at the same time, but that's exactly what the league did Monday.

Let's take a look at the changes, starting with the two moves that got made in Week 9.

With this change, the Raiders-49ers game will now be moved into the prime viewing window for Week 9. #450. 1.9-million-year-old discovery rewrites the story of human evolution

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An archaeological site in the Jordan Valley is rewriting the timeline of early human migration. New research shows that 'Ubeidiya is at least 1.9 million years old, placing humans in the region hundreds of thousands of years earlier than previously believed. The revised dating makes 'Ubeidiya, alongside Dmanisi in Georgia, one of the oldest known sites of early human activity outside Africa. The findings suggest that ancient human populations, using different types of stone tools, had already spread into the Levant during a crucial early period of human expansion beyond Africa.

The research was led by Prof. Ari Matmon of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Prof. Omry Barzilai of the University of Haifa, and Prof. Miriam Belmaker of the University of Tulsa. By combining three advanced dating methods, the team established a more reliable timeline for 'Ubeidiya, a site that has played an important role in research on human evolution.

Their findings indicate that the site is at least 1.9 million years old, making it one of the earliest known locations where ancient humans lived outside their African homeland.

Ancient Stone Tools Reveal Early Human Activity

The 'Ubeidiya Formation has attracted scientific attention for decades because of its early evidence of the Acheulean stone tool tradition. This technology is known for large tools shaped on both sides, including hand axes. The site also contains a rich collection of animal fossils, representing species originally associated with Africa and Asia, including several that are now extinct.

Despite its importance, researchers have struggled to establish exactly when early humans occupied the area. Previous estimates generally placed 'Ubeidiya between 1.2 million and 1.6 million years old, but those dates relied on comparisons with other archaeological and geological evidence rather than direct measurements.

To resolve the uncertainty, the researchers collected new samples from the site and analyzed them using three complementary dating techniques. Each provided an independent source of evidence about the age of the ancient deposits.

The first technique, called cosmogenic isotope burial dating, uses rare isotopes that form when cosmic rays interact with rocks exposed at Earth's surface. After the rocks become buried, these isotopes decay at known rates. Measuring what remains allows scientists to estimate how long the rocks have been underground.

The researchers also investigated evidence of Earth's ancient magnetic field recorded in sediments deposited at the bottom of a lake. As these sediments accumulated, they preserved the orientation of the magnetic field at the time. Comparing these records with known magnetic reversals showed that the deposits formed during the Matuyama Chron, a period that began more than two million years ago.

A third technique involved fossilized shells from Melanopsis, a genus of freshwater snails found within the sediment. Using uranium-lead dating, the team was able to establish a minimum age for the geological layers containing the ancient stone tools.

Together, the three approaches pointed to a much older age than earlier estimates.

A 1.9-Million-Year-Old Discovery Changes the Human Migration Timeline

The combined evidence indicates that 'Ubeidiya dates back at least 1.9 million years, significantly changing the timeline of early human settlement beyond Africa.

This places the Jordan Valley site at approximately the same age as Dmanisi in Georgia, another major location for understanding early human migration. The similarity in age suggests that ancient human populations were expanding into different regions around the same time.

The findings also raise intriguing questions about how these early migrations unfolded. Evidence from the two sites suggests that different groups of ancient humans may have carried distinct stone tool technologies out of Africa during overlapping periods.

These included the Oldowan tradition, associated with relatively simple stone tools, and the more sophisticated Acheulean tradition, known for carefully shaped tools such as hand axes. Rather than spreading in a single technological sequence, these different approaches to toolmaking may have traveled beyond Africa at roughly the same time as separate groups of early humans entered new environments.

Solving a Three-Million-Year-Old Dating Puzzle

The researchers also had to explain an unexpected complication in their results. Initial measurements of cosmogenic isotopes suggested that some rocks were approximately three million years old, considerably older than indicated by the site's magnetic, fossil, geological, and archaeological evidence.

Further investigation revealed why the measurements appeared to conflict.

The sediments had undergone a complicated history of repeated deposition, burial, exposure, and movement within the Dead Sea rift and the surrounding landscape. Some of the material had been buried long before it was eventually transported and deposited again near the ancient lake at 'Ubeidiya.

This recycling meant that the geological history recorded by certain rocks extended much farther into the past than the formation of the archaeological layers themselves.

"The exposure-burial history that emerges from the model implies recycling of sediments previously deposited and buried in the rift valley... and then redeposited along the 'Ubeidiya paleo lake shoreline."

By accounting for this complex sediment history, the team reconciled the conflicting measurements and strengthened the evidence for the site's revised age.

The results establish 'Ubeidiya as one of the oldest known centers of early human activity outside Africa. They also suggest that the movement of our ancient relatives into new territories was already underway 1.9 million years ago, potentially involving multiple populations with different toolmaking traditions.

#5226. Cutting both ways: AI sharpens systems protection while opening new exploits for malicious actors

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The cybersecurity industry is discovering that powerful frontier AI models are a double-edged sword.

Companies can use models to identify vulnerabilities in source code and system configurations faster than ever before. As it turns out, malicious actors can do this as well.

A prime example of how AI models have shifted cybersecurity into a new gear can be seen in recent news involving exploits surrounding the PHP programming language, which powers up to 70% of global websites. Security researchers used OpenAI Group PBC’s GPT-5.6 Sol to scan PHP’s code base for bugs. They discovered critical vulnerabilities, and organizations have been scrambling to patch them.

Meanwhile, Amazon.com Inc. disclosed last week that attackers began targeting PHP vulnerabilities four days before patches were released, and exploit attempts seen by Cloudflare Inc. have since climbed into the millions.

This latest round of digital threats highlights how organizations need to be prepared for the inevitable breach. During the Dell Technologies World gathering in Las Vegas earlier this year, there was a clear message that data protection and cyber resilience have emerged as foundational control layers for enterprise AI. This has set the stage for how enterprises must adapt security controls to the AI era, according to theCUBE Research’s Krista Case.

“This is part of a broader market conversation that ties cyber recovery capabilities to resilience, AI governance, data integrity, and operational trust, in a world where enterprise AI systems are only as reliable as the data pipelines, runtime controls, and recovery processes supporting them,” Case said. “Cyber resilience and AI infrastructure platforms are expanding beyond backup and recovery into metadata visibility, recovery validation and contextual understanding of enterprise data. Organizations should pay close attention to how vendors establish governance and validation for AI environments.”

This feature is part of SiliconANGLE Media’s exploration of key trends and architectural shifts powering continuous, production-grade AI. Behind the focus on cyber resilience is a concern within the cybersecurity community that AI has the potential to open a significant hole in systems protection. AI models have dramatically increased the ability to analyze and potentially exploit weaknesses. A monthly report from Microsoft Corp. listed 1,453 new vulnerabilities in October alone.

“AI changes the whole game,” Steve Kenniston, senior cybersecurity evangelist for portfolio marketing at Dell, recently told theCUBE. “There’s the model inferencing. There’s the model training data. There are systems where people can do things like prompt injection. There’s identity management that needs to be thought about.”

For its part, Dell has been actively employing frontier models to build defenses. The firm is a participant in Anthropic PBC’s Project Glasswing, which gives vetted organizations access to powerful AI tools to secure critical systems. Glasswing’s purpose is to bring together organizations whose software supports critical infrastructure and could lead to catastrophic results if successfully attacked.

The rise of AI agents has also brought renewed scrutiny to identity in enterprise systems. Easier access to AI tools by threat actors has changed the rules of engagement for access management and detection. “Undoubtedly the cost of entry and the barriers to entry have crumbled,” according to John Scimone, president and chief security officer of Dell, who spoke recently with theCUBE. “Said another way, the least sophisticated attack tomorrow is more sophisticated than the world will have ever seen a year ago.”

In a world where the attacks have grown more sophisticated, another dynamic has become clear: there is no warning. Enterprises are confronted with a situation where, unlike natural disasters such as hurricanes or tsunamis, for which warning systems are in place in many parts of the globe, a major attack will often strike with no hint of what’s to come.

This has placed even greater emphasis on the need for recovery and resilience, according to InfoScale Director of Product Management Joseph D’Angelo.

“There’s no prediction here,” said D’Angelo, in a recent conversation with theCUBE. “So, the only way to protect yourself is to ensure that you have what we like to call a defensible recovery posture. And that is understanding the state of your applications, understanding the state of your data, and being able to recover those in a way that is not encumbered or in any way compromised by an attack.”

D’Angelo’s point around data is an important one. The AI systems currently powering numerous enterprise applications today are only as good as the data that fuels them.

Data needs storage, and companies such as Infinidat are focused on security solutions that can protect the operational state of a business as AI increases the speed and complexity of cyber risk. This involves a combination of immutable snapshots, cyber detection and recovery capabilities designed to help organizations identify clean recovery points and avoid restoring compromised data.

“It’s all about the data,” said Infinidat Chief Marketing Officer Eric Herzog, during a recent appearance on theCUBE. “The core is the data, which is why you need next-generation data protection on your primary storage, which is what AI would use. You need next-generation data protection on your backup, as well.”

Another key development on the cybersecurity horizon for many businesses involves post-quantum cryptography, or PQC. The migration from classical encryption to quantum-safe algorithms that can withstand attacks from future quantum computers is an important requirement for any strategy that addresses emerging threats and strengthens long-term cyber resilience.

At the moment, enterprises do not appear ready for the quantum threat. A study published earlier this year by DigiCert Inc. found that only 7% of organizations surveyed have deployed quantum-safe or hybrid cryptography across most of their digital certificates. As the growth of AI agents has significantly boosted the role of data in enterprise platforms, the quantum threat could ultimately cause significant disruption.

“Quantum computing will break the encryption and digital signatures protecting data today, while agentic AI raises the stakes by increasing the value of data and autonomously shares it across teams and organizations,” said John Roese, global CTO and chief AI officer at Dell.

AI tools have provided insight into the scope of the quantum threat. An Anthropic PBC report published in late July detailed how researchers using Claude Mythos Preview discovered improved ways to attack cryptographic algorithms. This included a successful weakening of HAWK, one of nine candidates in the National Institute of Standards and Technology, or NIST’s, process for additional post-quantum digital signature algorithms.

“CISA, NSA, and NIST have warned since 2023 that adversaries could be collecting encrypted data now and holding it until they can decrypt it,” said Garrison Buss, chief technology officer and co-founder of QuSecure Inc., in a briefing prepared for SiliconANGLE. “What the AI results change is the other side of that clock – an attacker no longer needs a quantum computer to make meaningful progress; they only need classical compute and a few days.”

As 2026 begins to wind down, enterprises are facing the need to implement AI solutions as fast as possible, while urgently protecting against the threats that artificial intelligence brings. This will require infrastructure-level security that can mitigate the risks posed by agentic AI, quantum computing and sophisticated models in the hands of threat actors.

“There is no AI without a secure infrastructure, and that means data that is also recoverable, that is secure, and is of course compliant,” said theCUBE Research’s Christophe Bertrand, during an analyst briefing. “Cyber resiliency risk is not just on the data, what you have to go through every day, it’s also starting to appear in the actual AI infrastructure itself. Don’t make backup and recovery an afterthought, because it’s going to hurt your wallet.”

As companies build resilient infrastructures, the unknown factor may be control over AI itself. Highly publicized attacks on Hugging Face and the United Nations by “rogue” AI agents illustrate the security complexities of the new autonomous world. The tech industry has been defined by decades of innovation, and this may be the time for unconventional thinking when it comes to cybersecurity.

“We used to draw the stack, and security was a layer,” said theCUBE Research’s Dave Vellante, during a recent edition of theCUBE Pod. “It’s not a layer anymore. It has to be something that’s orthogonal. I think that the attackers are always going to have an advantage. You know the old saying, they only have to be right once. And there’s always going to be seams.”

According to a blog post from Cohere, the model’s written reasoning explicitly stated it would not use any external tools. Yet in the very next step, it attempted to send a test string (a short piece of placeholder text) to an external server before being blocked by a security sandbox.

Cohere said it could not tell whether the behavior was deliberate or a leftover from its training.

Asked about the debate over the model’s intent, Cohere co-founder Ivan Zhang told TechCrunch that Cohere has seen its own models pick up strange habits in training simulators.

“It’s really hard to assign intention or motivation. We’ve read our own model traces, we’ve seen reward hacks, and sometimes these models get really creative when it comes to trying to break out their sandboxes,” Zhang said. (Reward hacks are shortcuts a model finds to earn credit without doing a task as intended.)

To teach an AI model to act as an agent that takes actions on its own, companies like Alibaba train it in thousands of practice setups, sort of like flight simulators for software. The model is rewarded every time it finishes a task in a simulator, and it picks up habits along the way. If these practice setups routinely stored files on Alibaba’s cloud servers, models could learn to save files there by default.

Critics also argued that the incident could have been caused by the way Cohere investigated the behavior, steering the model toward it instead of measuring how often Qwen does it on its own.

To find what triggered the upload attempt, Cohere supplied the beginning of a network command (an instruction telling one computer to connect to another) and let the model finish it. Because it processed thousands of routine network commands during its training, the model acted like a highly advanced autocomplete — often filling in web addresses, some of them for Alibaba’s servers.

Cohere said the original upload attempt happened without any such help, but that the behavior is rare.

After developers asked for more evidence, Cohere released the raw traces from its tests on Monday evening on the AI developer platform Hugging Face, along with a script so others could try to reproduce the behavior.

One initial skeptic, after going through the traces, rated the evidence as strong that Qwen can invent suspicious web addresses, but found no evidence that Alibaba planted a backdoor or that Qwen was deliberately hiding its intent.

The incident lands as governments are deciding how far to push back on Chinese AI. Last week, the Department of Homeland Security ordered federal civilian agencies to remove the Chinese AI developer DeepSeek’s products from their systems.

In its blog post, Cohere called on governments to clarify whether their restrictions on Chinese AI also apply to models running on government computers.

“I’m not asking for full regulation of these open-weights models,” Zhang told TechCrunch, adding that companies should treat open models like any program downloaded from the internet.

Cohere, which sells its own AI models to governments, stands to benefit if officials grow warier of free Chinese alternatives.

#4164. The surprisingly simple exercise that could lower your blood pressure

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that exercising just once or twice a week may be enough to maintain some of the blood pressure benefits gained from regular physical activity, at least over a short period. However, people who continue exercising three times weekly may experience larger and more sustained improvements.

The findings, published in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, suggest that even a single weekly session of isometric exercise could help people preserve improvements in blood pressure when they cannot commit to a more frequent workout schedule.

Isometric exercise involves tightening muscles while holding the body in a fixed position for several minutes. Common examples include wall squats, planks, and handgrip exercises.

Earlier research has found that isometric training can lower blood pressure more effectively than other types of exercise, sometimes producing reductions comparable to or greater than those achieved with certain medications. However, researchers have not fully understood how often these exercises need to be performed to maintain their cardiovascular benefits.

To investigate, researchers enrolled 100 adults whose average age was 37.4 years. Participants had systolic blood pressure ranging from normal to high-normal levels (120-140 mmHg) and did not regularly participate in structured exercise programs.

Under expert guidelines, systolic blood pressure between 130 and 139 mmHg falls into the "high-normal" category, which warrants specific lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals. None of the participants had been diagnosed with hypertension.

To qualify, volunteers had to be free of injuries and illnesses, have no current or previous use of blood pressure medication, and be nonsmokers. They also had to consume no more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups. One served as a control group, while the other four began the same four-week exercise program at home, focusing on isometric movements involving the lower body.

The training consisted of four wall squat holds per session, with two minutes of seated recovery between each hold. Participants completed this routine three times weekly during the first four weeks.

After this initial training period, the four exercise groups followed different schedules for another four weeks. One continued with three weekly sessions, another reduced its frequency to twice weekly, and a third performed the exercises only once a week. The fourth group stopped training altogether.

Researchers evaluated blood pressure, heart rate, and additional measures of cardiovascular function at the beginning of the experiment and again after four and eight weeks.

Even One Weekly Workout Helped Preserve Blood Pressure Benefits

After the first four weeks, all four exercise groups showed significant improvements compared with the control group. These included reductions in systolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart contracts, and diastolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart relaxes. Mean arterial pressure, a measure of average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, also decreased significantly.

These initial results were consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the blood pressure benefits of isometric exercise. However, after participants switched to their assigned maintenance schedules, the groups no longer showed statistically significant differences in diastolic blood pressure.

By the end of the eight-week study, participants exercising just once or twice weekly had maintained significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure compared with the control group. Neither schedule maintained a statistically significant improvement in diastolic blood pressure.

The greatest benefits appeared among participants who continued performing wall squats three times per week. Their systolic blood pressure fell by an average of 13.73 mmHg (-13.73 mmHg) from the beginning of the study.

By comparison, the twice-weekly group experienced an average reduction of 6.95 mmHg, while participants exercising once a week recorded a decrease of 6.56 mmHg.

Those who maintained the three-session schedule also preserved improvements in other cardiovascular measurements, including total peripheral resistance (an indication that blood vessels have narrowed) and heart rate variability.

Stopping Exercise Caused Blood Pressure Benefits to Fade

The results also revealed what happened when participants stopped exercising completely.

Those who discontinued wall squats after the initial four weeks experienced a reversal of their earlier blood pressure improvements. By the end of the study, their readings were no longer statistically different from those of the control group.

This finding suggests that maintaining some level of regular isometric exercise may be important for preserving the cardiovascular improvements gained during training.

However, the researchers emphasized that the maintenance phase lasted only four weeks. Additional studies following participants for longer periods will be needed to determine whether less frequent training can preserve these benefits over months or years.

"The study demonstrates that while lower frequency isometric exercise training may be sufficient to maintain blood pressure reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations," the researchers write.

"The study offers evidence that lower frequency isometric exercise training can help maintain short-term blood pressure reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges."
