#5419. NHTSA Proposes Cyan Lights For AVs, And Other Conveniently Tesla-Specific Changes

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) has announced more proposed changes to Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 108, which governs vehicle lighting regulations. The latest changes, announced Oct. 23, include establishing equipment specifications for vehicles with automated driving systems, including displaying cyan lights to signal when an automated driving system is operational, and removal of the requirement for autonomous vehicles (AVs) with no driver controls to have manually operated turn signals, hazard lights, and headlight beam switching controls or telltales.

This goes beyond the announcement NHTSA made earlier this month that it would modernize U.S. headlight regulations, which addressed headlight glare on other road users, improving performance, updating testing and materials, and modernizing lighting technology with rules for LEDs, dusk sensors, auto high beams, and replaceable light sources.

The Tesla Cybercab is the only car on American roads that does not have physical driving controls like a steering wheel or pedals, and it's currently the subject of a safety probe because of that lack of traditional controls. These proposed changes to Standard No. 108 almost certainly have absolutely nothing to do with the fact that Tesla CEO Elon Musk has donated over $400 million to Republicans this election cycle.

Mercedes-Benz got approval to use turquoise lights on its cars equipped with Drive Pilot, the company's Level 3 autonomous driving technology, in 2023. Mercedes made this decision to let other drivers and law enforcement know that a person is allowed to be distracted while their car is legally in control.

The new proposal will not require autonomously driving cars to have these cyan lights, but it legally establishes what NHTSA calls "an 'if equipped' standard" that regulates light color, as well as setting mounting and visibility rules for companies that choose to use them. If a vehicle is equipped with Automated Driving Systems marker lamps, at least one should be visible from each of the front, sides, and rear of the vehicle, at a proposed minimum height of 15 inches, and the lights can't be brighter than the parking lights.

Other proposed changes include a removal of the headlight testing requirements that currently require headlights in all vehicles to meet performance parameters when tested at 12.8 volts. It also aims to eliminate the current regulation that forces the uppermost light in a vertically oriented headlights to operate as the standard beam, since NHTSA has tentatively concluded that it's possible to provide adequate illumination for safe driving without requiring the low beam to be placed above the high beams.

The proposal incorporates what NHTSA describes as "a process to remove unnecessary barriers to technological innovation while ensuring motor vehicle safety is not compromised." It seems the primary "barriers to technological innovation" in autonomous vehicles are the current requirement for all cars to have a turn signal operating unit, as well as the requirement for all vehicles to be equipped with a headlamp beam switching device.

Proposed modifications to the regulation would specify that these changes would only apply to vehicles that are not equipped with manually operated driving controls, so there would be no changes in regulations for vehicles with a steering wheel and pedals.

Finally, the proposal changes the requirements for telltales, which are the sounds and little lights on your dashboard that illuminate when your turn signal or hazard lights are activated, or when your low-beam headlights or high-beam headlights are activated. NHTSA now wants to specify that these telltales are only required for cars with a driver, which it defines as "an occupant of a motor vehicle seated immediately behind the steering control system." By this definition, an autonomous vehicle without a steering wheel cannot have a driver, so it shouldn't be required to have these telltales.

As autonomous vehicle technology comes closer to reality, it's likely that many other NHTSA regulations will have to change, so we will keep you updated as those changes roll out. For now, NHTSA wants your input on this crop of changes; there are several ways to do it, outlined in the Federal Register.

#4719. Toyota Recalls 106K Hybrid Land Cruisers for Possible Fuel Leak

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The issue stems from a potentially faulty fuel-line connection, which could waste gas and increase the risk of a fire.

Gas is expensive these days, and filling up your SUV will cost you even more if it’s leaking fuel. That’s a potential concern for nearly 106K Land Cruiser owners, whose vehicles are involved in a new recall that Toyota has reported to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).

The recall documents state that 105,794 hybrid Land Cruisers have a plastic fuel tube on the gas tank that connects to a metal fuel tube that sends the combustible liquid to the turbocharged four-cylinder engine. However, if the retaining clip that holds these two parts together is lifted, it can cause the fuel lines to disconnect and cause a leak. Not only will such a leak lead to wasted fuel and the gas engine eventually shutting off, but it also increases the risk of a fire.

While the recall covers over 105K Land Cruisers from the 2024 to 2027 model years, Toyota says that not all of the affected SUVs were sold in the United States. The automaker also hasn’t yet identified the cause of the issue, according to the NHTSA report. Still, Toyota says that all dealers have been notified about the recall, and all owners will be notified by mail by December 13, 2026. Toyota dealers have been instructed to inspect the fuel lines and install a supplemental clamp to prevent them from disconnecting, or to replace the plastic tube and install a clamp if necessary.

#5622. New Jersey may finally reverse its draconian e-bike law after massive backlash

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

New Jersey lawmakers are already looking to walk back one of the country’s most controversial e-bike laws, just months after the sweeping new regulations took effect and sparked widespread backlash from riders, bicycle shops, and advocacy groups.

A newly introduced bill, S4830, sponsored by State Senator Raj Mukherji, would remove licensing, registration, and insurance requirements for low-speed electric bicycles with motors rated at 750W or less and a maximum motor-powered speed of 20 mph (32 km/h). This includes Class 1 and Class 2 electric bicycles.

The proposal would essentially restore bicycle-like treatment for these two classes of e-bikes, importantly including throttle-equipped models that make up the majority of e-bikes sold in the state and more broadly in the US, while maintaining stricter requirements for faster and more powerful electric bicycles.

The move comes after New Jersey’s previous governor, Phil Murphy, signed a sweeping e-bike law in January following several tragic crashes involving young riders.

That legislation, which took full effect in July, eliminated the state’s previous three-class e-bike framework and introduced licensing and registration requirements for all electric bicycles, including ordinary low-speed commuter models. Throttle-equipped and faster e-bikes also faced insurance requirements.

As we previously reported, New Jersey bicycle shops experienced devastating sales declines after the law took effect, with some reporting drops of 20% to 90%. Several businesses were forced to lay off employees, downsize, or even close entirely as e-bike sales and usage plummeted.

Meanwhile, riders faced confusing new requirements that made e-bikes considerably less accessible, which was particularly impactful for lower-income residents, people with disabilities, and those who rely on electric bicycles for daily transportation.

The original law was intended to improve safety, but critics argued that it largely punished responsible riders instead of addressing the growing problem of high-powered electric motorcycles masquerading as bicycles. Only responsible riders actually registered their bikes as required by law, while others were deterred from cycling, and the hooligan riders who often gave e-bikes a bad name continued to ride illegally.

After all, requiring someone riding a 20 mph (32 km/h) commuter e-bike to obtain a driver’s license and register their bicycle does little to address reckless riding on machines capable of highway speeds.

The newly proposed legislation appears to acknowledge that distinction, offering a more targeted approach that could preserve reasonable safety regulations without making ordinary e-bike ownership unnecessarily complicated.

S4830 still needs to work its way through the legislature before becoming law, but for New Jersey’s struggling e-bike industry, it’s a promising sign that lawmakers may finally be listening. It is still widely argued that requiring electric bicycles from 21-28 mph (32-45 km/h) to require a driver’s license and registration is overly burdensome, but at least the state appears to be moving back toward the right direction.

Volkswagen issued a recall affecting nearly 73,000 EVs globally. Until a remedy is available, VW will limit charging on impacted vehicles to 80% of their maximum battery capacity.

After issuing a recall with the NHTSA earlier this month that impacts 22,524 ID.4 electric SUVs in the US, official information from the German Federal Motor Transport Authority (KBA) reveals several thousand more EVs are affected worldwide.

In three separate recalls now under investigation by the KBA, a total of 72,668 EVs are impacted worldwide, including 9,881 in Germany.

The recall affects certain batches of the ID. Buzz, ID.3, ID.4, and ID.7 models produced between 2022 and 2025, as well as Cupra Born models from 2022 to 2024.

Volkswagen issued the recall after an internal investigation found that some battery cells produced during that period do not meet specifications. The recall stems from a supplier defect.

In some instances, “certain charging profiles combined with a state of charge over 80% may trigger thermal propagation,” or a battery fire, Volkswagen said.

Until a remedy is available, Volkswagen is urging owners to limit charging to 80% of its max capacity. If technically possible, VW said: “the maximum available charging capacity will be temporarily limited to 80% by the system.”

In the US, Volkswagen emailed owners of vehicles included in the recall, saying that unless they opt out by October 21, it “intends to apply a temporary vehicle setting that limits charging to 80%.”

The update will be done remotely and reflected in the mobile app on or about October 27, 2026. Some owners have already received the update.

Those in the US can also contact the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration Vehicle Safety Hotline at 1-888-327-4236 or visit NHTSA.gov for more information. The NHTSA campaign number is 26V630.
