#4811. Crypto wallet maker Keystone to destroy almost 19,000 devices after security breach

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hardware wallet maker Keystone said it will destroy almost 19,000 unsold devices and suspend shipments after a website breach exposed customer information and a key that could let counterfeit wallets pass authenticity checks.

An attacker gained access to the company’s website on Oct. 20 through a flaw in a third-party data monitoring tool, Keystone said Monday in a post on X. The attack exposed 25,964 buyer records from 2024 through 2026, including names, contact details, shipping addresses and order information. Another 12,873 unpaid checkout records may have been affected.

The breach adds to a string of attacks on hardware wallet users this year. At Ledger, criminals fitted devices sold through resellers in Southeast Asia, with hidden components capturing recovery phrases during setup. Similarly, a flaw in Coldcard’s seed-generation software let attackers reconstruct private keys and drain wallets remotely.

The stolen Keystone key is built into devices during production and cannot be changed. It allows wallets to pass online authenticity checks, raising the risk that fake devices could appear genuine and put users' funds at risk, according to the firm. The company said it will destroy 18,835 unsold units.

Keystone said the breach did not expose recovery phrases, private keys or wallet credentials.

Some customer records should have been deleted under Keystone's 180-day retention policy, but an automated deletion tool failed without the company detecting the issue, it said.

“We failed to fulfill our commitment to erase customer data on time, and we accept responsibility for this failure,” Keystone wrote.

Keystone’s update came a day after hardware wallet maker Foundation disclosed a separate breach in which attackers sent phishing emails that appeared to come from its official domains.

Foundation said it had identified more than 23,000 phishing email attempts. It found no evidence that Passport devices or private keys were compromised.

Foundation also found older database copies that its 60-day data deletion process had missed. In an update, it said attackers could access records containing about 5,600 mailing addresses and 5,200 phone numbers, though it could not confirm how many were accessed.

Keystone said devices delivered before Oct. 20 remain safe to use if they pass authentication, while customers who received devices using the old key after that date should seek further checks before use.

The company said users do not need to move funds due to the breach alone and warned against fake recall or asset migration messages.

Keystone will replace the authentication key in new devices, shift website sales to preorders and cut customer data retention to 90 days, it added.

#4535. A three-legged cougar named Crimson is thriving in Arkansas

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 11:06 AM EDT

At first, the video seems to feature an empty habitat. There’s a crate filled with hay, platforms of various heights, and a ball hanging from a rope. Then a furry head peaks out from behind the crate. A few seconds later, a large wild cat bounds into view, rushing toward whoever is holding the camera. The young mountain lion (Puma concolor) appears so happy to see the videographer that you almost can’t tell he has only three legs.

“Crimson was just three weeks old when National Park Service biologists and then California Department of Fish and Wildlife determined he had been abandoned,” Emily McCormack, an animal curator at Turpentine Creek Wildlife Refuge in northwest Arkansas, tells Popular Science. “Using GPS collar data from his mother and camera monitoring, it was confirmed that she had moved to a different den site and was no longer returning to care for him.”

As if the situation could get any worse for the young mountain lion, he also had an injury to his hind foot. Without treatment, the injury ultimately could have killed him. He was rescued and transported to the Los Angeles Zoo, where he received acute veterinary care. Then he was taken to Oakland Zoo by Flying Tails animal air ambulance service. The Oakland Zoo veterinary hospital team eventually amputated the injured leg.

Thankfully, Crimson made an exceptional recovery, but he was too young to be considered for rehabilitation and release. The California Department of Fish and Wildlife decided Crimson would live permanently at Turpentine Creek Wildlife Refuge, a safe haven accredited by the Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries. And now it seems safe to say that Crimson has settled well into his forever home.

“He is thriving in his new habitat at our sanctuary. We chose a long habitat enclosure with relatively flat ground, where he can explore but also has the ability to fully run a good distance,” says McCormack. “Crimson loves his neighbors, especially a female lion named Lucy. He loves his meal times and can be heard chirping all day to the staff, interns, and guests.”

Mountain lions, also known as cougars or pumas, are native to parts of the Americas and represent the world’s most wide-ranging cat species.

A new stack of Starlink satellites is now in orbit following an early morning SpaceX launch from California.

A Falcon 9 rocket lifted off at 3:25 a.m. EDT (0725 GMT) on Oct. 27, from from Pad 4E at Vandenberg Space Force Base. The rocket delivered the 27 satellites of Starlink Group 15-16 to low Earth orbit (LEO), and successfully executed its first stage booster landing.

The mission's booster, B1082, cut its engines and disengaged from Falcon 9's upper stage approximately 2.5 minutes into flight, and adjusted its trajectory for a touchdown on SpaceX's Of Course I Still Love You (OCISLY) droneship in the Pacific Ocean.

B1082 successfullly landed on OCISLY at T+8:23, seconds before Falcon 9's second stage completed its first engine burn. The return of B1082 marks the booster's 21st Starlink mission, and 26th overall flight, the others of which include USSF-62, OneWeb Launch 20, NROL-145, Transporter-18.

Falcon 9's upper stage ignited its engine for a second time about 53 minutes after liftoff, circularizing its orbit with a burn that lasted a very short 3 seconds. That was followed several minutes later by the deployment of the rocket's Starlink payloads.

USSF-62 | OneWeb Launch 20 | NROL-145 | Transporter-18 | 26 Starlink missions

SpaceX's Starlink network consists of more than 11,000 active spacecraft. The speed with which the company is delivering them to orbit in bulk, however, has slowed somewhat.

As SpaceX shifts focus to its larger, more powerful Starship rocket under development, plans to retire Falcon 9 have already begun. Part of that process included ending Starlink launches from Florida earlier this year.

Today's mission was the 117th Falcon 9 launch of 2026, so far. About three-quarters of those were dedicated to Starlink.

Even with the importance of physical media ownership in the current era of digital product ephemera, it's often unlikely your PC will actually be able to play the CDs, DVDs or Blu-Rays you have around the house. Internal disc drives aren't common amongst desktops or even laptops anymore, so your only recourse is often an external drive.

One Steam Frame user was hoping to connect his own detachable disc drive to watch movies and play Need For Speed Carbon (valid!) using the headset, which can run both VR and 'flat' games without a separate PC, but found that the Frame lacked the drivers to read a disc. In a very earnest community post, user Aiodensghost lamented that this fact "KILLED MY ABILITY TO INSTALL MY ABANDONWARE FROM 2006. You don't sell NFS Carbon anymore (fuck you very much EA) [...] May I PLEASE have the ability to use an optical drive?" So polite!

A Valve employee by the username gaberowe would respond a day later, simply saying "we will look into this thanks for alerting us". Around ten minutes pass, and the 'deckard-linux-no-oopsies' GitHub repository (yes, it's really called that), which is the Linux kernel sources used in SteamOS for the Steam Frame, sees a commit from Rowe that effectively turns on CD-ROM support. More savvy Steam Frame users can hypothetically install the updated kernel themselves, as this commit enabled an existing Linux kernel feature, but it's likely that this support will simply be added in an upcoming software update.

In the early 1990s, conditions in parts of Peru were brutal. Poverty was widespread, and the Shining Path insurgency was terrorizing communities. The authoritarian government tried to crush the far-left militant group, at times committing human rights abuses. Hundreds of thousands of people fled the violence.

After the August 1991 murders of three Catholic priests in the Diocese of Chimbote, many missionaries were among them. Augustinian missionaries who stayed in Peru were told by their superiors in the United States to develop an exit plan.

Robert Prevost, an American priest in his mid-30s who had arrived in 1985, was among those who refused to leave. He ultimately spent 20 years in Peru across four decades, ministering to the poor, distributing food and clothing to displaced migrants, helping parishioners through personal difficulties, and naturalizing as a Peruvian citizen. Last year, he became Pope Leo XIV.

On Nov. 6, Leo is scheduled to begin an 11-day visit to Latin America that includes a return to Peru along with stops in Uruguay and Argentina. The pope was diagnosed with lung cancer this month and had part of his right lung removed. But the Vatican confirmed on Monday that Leo will proceed with the trip—the longest of his pontificate so far, covering more than 19,000 miles, 12 plane flights, two helicopter rides, and significant changes in altitude.

Dr. Stefano Margaritora, the surgeon who operated on the pope, said on Oct. 17 that Leo’s recovery was proceeding well and that “there are no clinical reasons why [the trip] should not take place.” And Luigi Carbone, the physician responsible for coordinating Leo’s medical care, said in a statement on Monday that “there are no contraindications that would prevent him from undertaking his upcoming Apostolic Journey to Latin America as planned.”

Latin America is a traditional stronghold of the Catholic Church, with around 40 percent of the world’s 1.42 billion Catholics in the region. But the share of people who consider themselves Catholic has fallen by nine percentage points in six of Latin America’s most populous countries in the last decade, according to a 2024 Pew Research Center survey.

The rise of Protestant and evangelical churches has meant a decline in Catholic affiliation in the region, particularly since the 1970s. But over the last 10 years, the Pew study showed a sharper increase in the percentage of people who describe themselves as atheist, agnostic, or “nothing in particular.” Sexual abuse scandals involving Catholic clergy have also alienated members.

The declining percentages of Catholics in Latin America mirror a worldwide trend. A global Pew survey conducted between 2023 and 2024 showed that more adults left the Catholic Church since childhood than joined it in 21 of 24 countries surveyed across all six inhabited continents.

The church’s leadership is aware of this decline. When fellow cardinals elevated Leo to the papacy, they selected a U.S.-born prelate who was known for ministering to communities in Peru, including far-flung ones where transportation was difficult. Leo would take crucifixes and ceremonial wine with him, sometimes traveling by foot or on horseback. He once said that he intended to live in the country for the rest of his life.

“It will be a massive homecoming,” said Rev. John Lydon, a fellow Augustinian who attended Villanova University with Leo in the 1970s and lived and worked alongside him in Peru. The Augustinian order emphasizes the values of truth, unity, and charity. “The Peruvian people are very warm and welcoming. They loved [Leo] because he treated them with dignity and respect,” Lydon added.

Peru is where many of Leo’s social priorities solidified. Central to his outlook are love for the poor, treating migrants with dignity, and addressing income inequality.

Living among the poor “deeply affected him—on a personal, human level,” Lydon said. Leo “would go out to where the people were … They really loved him for that. They were poor, and society treated them badly, and here was someone who was conscious of their human dignity.” Lydon spent nearly 40 years as a missionary in Peru and is now the director of the Mother Cabrini Institute on Immigration at Villanova.

Five soup kitchens that Leo established in Peru are still operating, according to Lydon. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the now-pope helped secure oxygen supplies for hospitals. Leo was so beloved by his parishioners that his birthday celebrations each September couldn’t be confined to a single day and instead were stretched out over a week.

His fellow missionaries and church leaders saw him as someone who listened thoughtfully to parishioners and fellow clergy, including those he had differences with, to get things done.

When Leo’s predecessor, Pope Francis, sent him to lead the Diocese of Chiclayo in 2014, it was heavily influenced by the controversial Opus Dei organization, which Francis did not see eye to eye with. (Opus Dei has faced accusations of secrecy and coercive recruitment.) Francis was confident that Leo would be able to engage in dialogue and be accepted, Lydon said: “I think he was picked because of his personality of working well with people who think differently.”

The cardinals who selected Prevost as pope may have seen him as an American counterweight to U.S. President Donald Trump. Leo has characterized the treatment of immigrants in the United States as “inhuman” and called Trump’s threats against the Iranian people “truly unacceptable.” Trump later lashed out at Leo on Truth Social, calling him “WEAK on Crime” and “terrible” for foreign policy.

Though Leo has avoided attacking Trump personally, the pope has said that he has “no fear of the Trump administration or speaking out loudly.” The public tension between Trump and Leo has done little to dent the pontiff’s popularity in the United States, where around eight in 10 Catholics had a favorable view of Leo in a June Pew survey. Just over half of those polled said that Trump has been too critical of the pope.

Leo’s ability to shape U.S. policy toward Latin America is limited. However, his credibility among U.S. Catholics, moral authority, Latin American background, and access to top U.S. officials—last May, he met with U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who is Catholic—give him an unusual platform from which to challenge Washington’s approach to issues including migration, poverty, and the use of force.

Though popes make public pronouncements on issues important to them, one of their main sources of influence comes through their power to appoint bishops in dioceses around the world. Just as Francis had goals in mind when he sent Leo to Chiclayo, Leo’s bishop appointments reflect messages that he wants local congregations to hear. From 2023 to 2025, Leo became well acquainted with potential appointees as he headed the Vatican’s Dicastery for Bishops, which reviews bishop candidates and recommends them to the pope.

Since becoming pope, Leo named Evelio Menjivar-Ayala, a native of El Salvador who was once an undocumented immigrant, as West Virginia’s bishop. He tapped Michael Pham, who fled communist Vietnam as a child, as bishop of San Diego. In Latin America, he named Luciano Maza Huamán, known for having deep local roots and serving his community, to lead the Archdiocese of Piura in Peru.

While Leo relies on bishops and other local clergy to connect with their congregations from week to week, his trip to Latin America will energize Catholics who get to hear from him directly, said Archbishop Gustavo García-Siller of San Antonio, a native of Mexico. “I don’t have any doubt that he will empower the laypeople,” he said.

Leo’s itinerary starts with a visit to Uruguay, where more than half of the population is religiously unaffiliated, making it the only country in the region without a Christian majority. No pope has visited Uruguay since 1988. Leo’s visit to Uruguay gives him a chance to generate interest in the faith and connect with non-Catholics who may share his views on social issues.

Uruguay has among the region’s most developed welfare states. Its government defines poverty reduction, income redistribution, development of human capabilities, and protection of social rights as major social spending goals. Leo has spoken about reducing poverty and removing its “social and structural causes,” the hope of a “different distribution of wealth,” and protecting vulnerable people during hardships. Such statements will find a receptive audience in Uruguay.

From Uruguay, Leo will head to Argentina, Francis’s home country. Francis never traveled back to Argentina after becoming pope. (No pontiff has visited the country since 1987.) Seven months before he died in April 2025, he told journalists that he wanted to return but there were “several things that need to be resolved first.”

Francis likely wanted to avoid being seen as taking a side in Argentina’s polarized political climate, Jimmy Burns, the author of a 2015 biography of Francis, told Reuters last year. During his stop in Argentina, Leo won’t bring the same complicated history as Francis. Leo will lead a tribute to his predecessor at the Metropolitan Cathedral of Buenos Aires, where Francis regularly celebrated mass as archbishop before becoming pope.

Leo’s final stop will be Peru, where he will spend as much time as in Uruguay and Argentina combined. He will travel to the capital, Lima, as well as the cities of Chiclayo, Cusco, and Pucallpa.

The visit promises to be emotion-filled for the pontiff, who served as Chiclayo’s bishop from 2015 to 2023. He is remembered in Peru for hiring basketball, swimming, and karate coaches to keep young people away from crime and taking them on weekend trips to the beach, as well as for being close to his parishioners and calmly staying in Peru despite the threats in the 1980s and 1990s.

“It’s going to be like a pastor coming home,” said Rev. Matthew Carnes, a Jesuit priest and vice president for mission and ministry at Santa Clara University in California. “People will just flock to see him and really savor the sense that they’re being seen, as well.”

#5077. Solana’s Firedancer Now Charges 1 SOL Per Bug Report

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Solana’s Firedancer team now requires researchers to burn 1 SOL per bug report to combat AI-generated submissions flooding its security program.

The team behind Solana’s Firedancer software has introduced a paywall for bug hunters. Researchers must now burn 1 SOL, roughly $100, with every submission. The goal is to cut down on AI-generated spam and give engineers more time to investigate real security flaws.

Under terms updated on Monday, the burned tokens are permanently removed from circulation. The cost is nonrefundable whether a report turns out to be invalid, outside the program’s scope, or a duplicate. Reports submitted without a burn are not eligible for rewards.

Jump Crypto built Firedancer as an alternative to Agave, the main software validators use to check and process Solana transactions. A serious bug in this software can stop validators from processing payments or cause them to disagree about account balances. That could disrupt trades and transfers across the network. Every hour spent sorting through duplicate or unworkable reports is an hour not spent fixing vulnerabilities like these.

The decision follows a $1 million audit competition that ran from April 9 to May 9. It attracted 809 reports from 162 researchers, yet produced only 127 unique security findings once non-exploitable observations were excluded. Nearly half of the valid submissions were duplicates.

Jump believes AI agents helped find or write almost every submission. In an Oct. 16 review, Jump’s Ishan Bhatt argued that AI has made finding bugs cheap while judging them remains expensive. Engineers still had to reproduce each problem and check whether the proposed attack could actually work. Some reports flagged apparent weaknesses while overlooking protections elsewhere in the code.

The review backlog peaked at 299 open reports. As checks slowed, other researchers rediscovered bugs that had been submitted but not yet fixed or disclosed. About 80% of duplicates arrived more than three days after the original report.

The contest had already charged $100 per submission and capped researchers at one report a day. Jump says charging a fee discourages people from sending large volumes of speculative findings. The new platform also limits each main report section to 3,000 characters.

Alongside the new fee, Jump is raising payouts. Eligible findings receive double the standard reward through Nov. 20, and triple for bugs specific to Alpenglow, Solana’s new voting system. Critical Alpenglow findings can qualify for up to $300,000.

The move shows how AI is changing security work in crypto. Automated tools can generate reports faster than humans can review them, so teams are now using economic costs to filter the noise.
