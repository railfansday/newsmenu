Rural hospitals in South Carolina will be the beneficiaries of a six-month cybersecurity pilot program conducted by the state, federal government and private sector tech companies.

Project Lifeline 250 was announced Monday by Governor Henry McMaster and White House National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross.

It aims to strengthen rural hospitals’ network defenses, identifying devices and technologies being used in critical care settings and implementing backup technologies to keep care running in the event of a cyberattack.

“Cyber threats are a rising challenge to our nation’s security, and we must remain vigilant to protect the systems our communities depend on,” he said in the announcement. “Project Lifeline 250 is another important step toward ensuring South Carolina’s rural hospitals have the resources they need to defend against cyberattacks and continue providing critical care.”

Of note, the cybersecurity boosts will come at no cost to rural hospitals participating in the pilot, according to an announcement from the governor’s office.

Outside of helping protect the participating hospitals, officials said they intend for the project to help evaluate which cybersecurity technologies can be scaled nationwide. They pointed to Project Watershed 250, a similar public-private pilot partnership in Texas launched over the summer to similarly explore cybersecurity protections for critical infrastructure.

“Project Lifeline 250 connects the world-leading American industry with rural hospitals and uses the greatest American tools and capabilities to defend hospital networks; deploy physical back-up devices after an initial incident to ensure an impacted hospital can still provide critical care; and identify high-risk and legacy devices and replace them,” National Cyber Director Cairncross said in the announcement. “President Trump and his entire Administration remain committed to strengthening and modernizing facilities and technology, and deploying innovative care models that bring high-quality health services to rural communities nationwide.”

The announcement did not specify which or how many rural hospitals would be participating in the pilot, though the announcement ceremony was held at Newberry Health Hospital, an independent, 90-bed acute care facility.

On the tech vendor side, the governor’s office listed Microsoft, Cisco, Cyera, Trellix and Zscaler as participating industry partners.

Microsoft President of U.S. Public Sector Industries Chris Barry, in a statement, noted that the tech giant is "proud to bring the experience we’ve gained supporting more than 870 rural hospitals across the country." As of early 2025, over 550 rural hospitals nationwide had received free or low-cost cybersecurity support from Microsoft through a rural health program run in partnership with the Biden administration and national provider groups.

Bill Thorn, public sector chief technology officer at cybersecurity firm Trellix, said that it would bring agentic AI capabilities to the program that will help automate detection and response. Another vendor, Abnormal AI, said it also plans to lend hospitals its automated Behavioral Security Platform, which protects against phishing attempts, as part of the program.

ChatGPT is cracking down on photorealistic, political deepfakes ahead of next week’s US midterm elections.

One of the biggest problems of the AI age is that AI deepfakes are now so realistic that they’re practically indistinguishable from real photos. That matters a lot during election season, when voters are looking for reliable, truthful information on their candidates and polling sites. OpenAI is implementing a new safeguard to prevent people from making political deepfakes.

The US midterm election is only a week away on Nov. 3. Tech issues are taking center stage in many races, especially as AI and data center concerns rise for folks across the country. (Check out CNET’s guide to spotting political scams.)

If you were to go into ChatGPT today and ask it to generate an image “depicting election interference or that may discourage voting,” as OpenAI put it in an Oct. 20 update, the chatbot would create the image, given it doesn’t violate any other content guidelines. But it will be in an illustrated or cartoon style — all requests to have the image appear photorealistic should be ignored.

For example, CNET asked ChatGPT to create an image of Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers standing outside a polling location. Every such prompt generated an image in a cartoon style. A photorealistic version of this kind of image would likely spark anxiety, if not outright panic, for voters.

The idea is to make it harder for bad actors to create realistic political deepfakes that could interfere with or question the integrity of an election without stymying people’s ability to create political content in general. In another example, CNET Managing Editor Jon Reed couldn’t trick ChatGPT into creating a photojournalist-style image that showed a voting machine recording the wrong choice.

“The problem with deepfakes isn’t social commentary, it’s deception. So our aim is to degrade the evidentiary value of an image, while preserving room for illustrations and social commentary,” Josh Lawson, OpenAI’s head of elections, told CNET. The goal is to “make our platform less useful to bad actors by adding another layer of friction,” he said.

This cartoonification of AI images is a practical way to make political deepfakes a little harder to create. Improving AI image models, like ChatGPT Images 2.5, is a double-edged sword. Newer models make fewer errors or hallucinations, making better content for creators. But removing those flubs makes it even harder for everyone else to identify AI-created content with the naked eye. Verification or AI labeling methods have tried to help, but those methods aren’t industry-wide nor foolproof.

When I asked Gemini maker Google about any precautions it is taking specifically for AI images, a spokesperson pointed me to its midterm election policy, which says the company is “rolling out additional protections to limit the creation of misleading election-related images” but doesn’t include any details on how.

Anthropic doesn’t have a traditional image or video generator, and a spokesperson told CNET that any attempts to use Claude to deceive voters or disrupt elections violate its policies. The company also posted this general election guidance earlier this month.

There’s no guarantee that OpenAI will keep this system in place once the election cycle is over. But it’s a solid sign from one of the biggest AI labs that it is taking its role in safeguarding American elections’ integrity at least somewhat seriously.

#2232. Next Man United manager: How Eddie Howe compares to Michael Carrick amid sack talk

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Adeyeye Oluwapelumi | 26 Oct 2026 16:37

Michael Carrick’s reign at Manchester United has never been under greater uncertainty, with the 45-year-old facing mounting scrutiny after a torrid start to his first full season at the helm.

Winless in their last eight games across all competitions, United suffered a humiliating 3-0 defeat to Bournemouth on Sunday, coming four days after a 4-1 Champions League thrashing at Como and leaving the 20-time English champions 16th in the Premier League table.

The Red Devils are a far cry from the side Carrick led to third place in 2025-26, having taken over as interim boss in January, almost five years after his unbeaten three-game caretaker stint, with his permanent tenure marked by turbulence this term.

Eddie Howe has been reported as a likely replacement, with the former Newcastle United boss without a club since leaving St James’ Park this summer.

With that in mind, Sports Mole compares both managers in terms of experience, playing styles, Premier League record, all-competition statistics and honours.

Howe is the far more seasoned of the two, having first taken charge of Bournemouth as caretaker manager in December 2008 at the age of 31 and spent the best part of 18 years in management since.

Across spells with Bournemouth, Burnley and Newcastle United, the 48-year-old had taken charge of 776 matches by the end of last season.

That includes 369 Premier League games across 10 top-flight campaigns with the Cherries and Magpies, plus a Champions League run in 2023-24 in which Newcastle beat PSG 4-1 but finished bottom of their group.

Carrick, by contrast, took his first permanent managerial job at Middlesbrough in October 2022, almost a year after a three-game caretaker spell at United and following time on the coaching staffs of José Mourinho and Ole Gunnar Solskjaer.

The former midfielder has managed 168 matches across his entire career, 136 at Middlesbrough and 32 across two spells at United, meaning Howe has taken charge of roughly 4.6 times as many games.

Howe’s average spell as a coach lasts 4.08 years compared with Carrick’s 1.28, highlighting the gulf in longevity despite an age gap of less than four years.

Howe is regarded as a tactically flexible coach who prefers a 4-3-3 in possession, shifting to a 4-5-1 out of possession, with a heavy emphasis on pressing and quick turnovers.

The 48-year-old’s approach has been shaped by Jurgen Klopp, Johan Cruyff and Diego Simeone, while his man-management is influenced by American basketball coach John Wooden.

Howe has also shown adaptability, using 3-4-2-1 and 4-4-2 shapes through injuries in 2023-24, a season that still brought Newcastle’s highest-scoring Premier League campaign with 85 goals.

Carrick, a composed midfield controller during his playing days, prefers a 4-2-3-1 system, with his Middlesbrough side scoring 13 goals in four matches in March 2023 on their way to a fourth-placed finish.

At United, his approach relied on quick, free-flowing counter-attacks, supported by a compact defensive structure that could absorb pressure without the ball, while restoring Bruno Fernandes to his natural number 10 role also played a key part in the team's resurgence in 2025-26.

Carrick has collected 50 points from 27 Premier League matches as a manager, winning 14, drawing eight and losing five for an average of 1.85 points per game.

The 45-year-old’s 2025-26 spell was the high point, with United taking 39 points from 17 league games, winning 12, drawing three and losing two to finish third and earn a Manager of the Season nomination.

This season has been the opposite, with one win, four draws and three defeats from eight league games, leaving United with an average of 0.88 per game.

Howe has overseen 369 Premier League matches, winning 140, drawing 81 and losing 148 for 501 points and an average of 1.36 per game.

The 48-year-old’s peak came at Newcastle, where he finished fourth with 71 points in 2022-23 and secured Champions League football again in 2024-25, before slipping to 12th with 49 points in his final campaign.

Carrick’s win rate of 51.9% comfortably beats Howe’s 37.9%, though the sample size is small compared to the former Magpies boss’ 369 games, which include relegation with Bournemouth in 2019-20.

Both managers' only previous meeting also came in the Premier League in March, when William Osula struck late to secure victory for 10-man Newcastle at St James' Park, marking Carrick's first defeat as United's interim boss.

Across his entire managerial career, which includes Middlesbrough and two spells at United, Carrick has won 79 of his 168 matches in all competitions, drawing 32 and losing 57, for an overall win rate of 47.0%.

Howe has won 340 of his 776 matches across his career, drawing 162 and losing 274, which equates to a win rate of 43.8% by the end of last season.

At Middlesbrough, Carrick won 46.3% of his 136 games, while his time at United since returning in January, both as interim and permanent boss, stands at 14 wins, seven draws and eight defeats from 29 matches, a 48.3% win rate.

Howe’s best returns came at Newcastle, where he won 112 of 231 games for a 48.5% win rate, marginally ahead of Carrick’s overall mark, while his first Bournemouth spell yielded exactly 50.0% from 102 matches.

The 48-year-old’s overall figure is dragged down by a 39.1% win rate in 87 games at Burnley and 40.2% across 356 matches in his second spell at Bournemouth.

Howe’s biggest honour came with Newcastle in 2024-25, when he led the Magpies to EFL Cup glory by beating Liverpool in the final, ending a 56-year wait for a major trophy, with the triumph coming two years after losing the final to Man United.

At Bournemouth, he won promotion from League Two and League One as runner-up before lifting the Championship title in 2014-15, taking the Cherries into the Premier League for the first time.

Howe was named LMA Manager of the Year in 2015, received the Football League Manager of the Decade award, and has won five Premier League Manager of the Month awards.

Carrick has no major silverware as a manager, with his individual accolades limited to EFL Championship Manager of the Month in March 2023 and Premier League Manager of the Month in January 2026.

The 45-year-old’s best results include a play-off semi-final and an EFL Cup semi-final at Middlesbrough and a third-placed finish at United, though his five Premier League titles and Champions League win as a player carry the biggest weight.

It was just like old times as José Mourinho ended a night at Camp Nou with a knee slide and three points

José Mourinho had already done the por qué?, the printout and the press conference no-show in under 100 days. Now he’s done a knee slide too, and in the perfect place: back where it all began and where it can all begin again. When Kylian Mbappé scored the first in the clásico on Sunday night, Real Madrid’s manager hardly moved, sitting in the shadow of the bench – but when the striker got the second to win it with time running out, that was different. Mourinho leapt into the light, throwing himself to the grass, the knees of a 63-year-old’s trousers muddy as a schoolboy’s, stuck his tongue out and pumped his fists.

He does so love it when a plan comes together. To the right of him, the bench was emptying, subs joining the players in sprinting towards the south-west corner. As they leapt about, dodging the bottles thrown towards them from the end, way, way up in the furthest corner and stuck inside a glass box like a bunch of David Blaines, the small group of Madrid fans who had come were going mad. “We have the best goalkeeper in the world, but none of us cried because Thibaut couldn’t play,” Mourinho said. Plenty might have felt like it, more reason to be fearful. Barcelona, the team who had won nine in a row, banging in 4.3 goals per game, had been beaten by Madrid, the team who had lost twice already and, truth be told, were mostly just not very good. The night it could have been all over, the title race done when it’s not even in November, was instead the night it finally started. “If you leave here nine points behind, it’s very heavy psychologically; if it’s six, it’s more balanced; if it’s three, that’s different,” Mourinho said.

This was different, and so is the season now. Counting the clásicos all those years ago, Mourinho racked up his sixth consecutive meeting with Barcelona without defeat, and there had been no parked bus, nor many lumps kicked. They didn’t just desperately hang on, although there was a moment when Dean Huijsen saved a shot with his face. Nor had they just waited for a moment, resisting and then robbing it, even if João Cancelo did insist: “That’s Madrid; they don’t have many chances but they score them.” The final shot count said 18-10 to Barcelona, and possession was 62% Barcelona’s.

“The way we won is important: you can win luckily or by defending a lot. But we won with calmness and totally deservedly. We did it by being organised, concentrated and having the courage to come out from the back,” Mourinho said. And if that might have been pushing it a little – and, let’s face it, the analyses might be different had Lunin not made an astonishing save from Gerard Martín or Lamine Yamal’s second-half shot not faded just past the post – he had a point.

Barcelona were not at their best. They had been hurried and imprecise, Rodri telling his teammates to lower their “pulse rate” as they came out for the second half. Lamine Yamal somehow missed an extraordinary chance just before the break. And when they did tighten the screw in the second half, they couldn’t break the wall. Xavi Espart was forced off, yet another injury at the back, the failure to sign a centre-back in the spotlight again. Yet this was still a faster, more aggressive, tighter Madrid than anyone expected. Asked to define it, Lunin went for “discipline and attitude”. And if that is partly about occasion, the least you can ask for, it’s more than you sometimes get.

Besides, it was also more than that. More than Marc Cucurella getting under Lamine Yamal’s skin, although he certainly succeeded. More than the wonderful winning goal, a flash of inspiration with Jude Bellingham going all Nureyev, the ball flitting between his feet and Gabriel Jesus’s legs, pirouetting to release Eduardo Camavinga. Before the game, Rodri said that one of Madrid’s tricks is to convince you that they’re not that good, but insisted he wouldn’t fall into that trap. Still he felt that plan particularly. Pedri did too. Bellingham dealt with Rodri, Bernardo Silva with Pedri. Pressed and tired too, according to Hansi Flick, neither truly took control. The inclusion of Arda Güler and Silva – the surprise in the middle, starting his first league game in six weeks – underlined an intention for Madrid to keep the ball when they could. They actually had a midfield, which was new. From the back they tried to build, draw Barcelona in. And if a time came when they had to wait, to put bodies in before the ball, well, they would do that too. Six times Barcelona shots were blocked. At least twice more, shots were taken from their toes. At the other end, meanwhile, Madrid attacked with an intent and an incision that probably drew the clearer chances: the xG showed 1.19 v 1.94.

They’ve won every game since Vinícius was suspended, facile and perhaps even cruel though that may be to say. And since Mourinho grew a beard, if you believe in that kind of thing.
