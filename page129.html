Welcome to your guide to Pips, the latest game in the New York Times catalog.

Released in August 2025, Pips puts a unique spin on dominoes, creating a fun single-player experience that could become your next daily gaming habit.

Currently, if you're stuck, the game only offers to reveal the entire puzzle, forcing you to move on to the next difficulty level and start over. However, we have you covered! Below are piecemeal answers that will serve as hints so that you can find your way through each difficulty level.

If you've ever played dominoes, you'll have a passing familiarity with how Pips is played. As we've shared in our previous hints stories for Pips, the tiles, like dominoes, are placed vertically or horizontally and connect with each other. The main difference between a traditional game of dominoes and Pips is the color-coded conditions you have to address. The touching tiles don't necessarily have to match.

The conditions you have to meet are specific to the color-coded spaces. For example, if it provides a single number, every side of a tile in that space must add up to the number provided. It is possible — and common — for only half a tile to be within a color-coded space.

Here are common examples you'll run into across the difficulty levels:

Equal: Every domino half in this space must be the same number of pips.

Not Equal: Every domino half in this space must have a completely different number of pips.

Less than: Every domino half in this space must add up to less than the number.

Greater than: Every domino half in this space must add up to more than the number.

If an area does not have any color coding, it means there are no conditions on the portions of dominoes within those spaces.

#5603. Trump campaigns in Mississippi, highlighting Republicans' challenges

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump arrives at a campaign rally at Crossroads Arena, in Corinth, Miss., Monday, Oct. 26, 2026. Jacquelyn Martin/AP hide caption

CORINTH, Miss. — President Donald Trump campaigned Monday in Mississippi, as he continued a closing midterm rally blitz that has highlighted Republicans' challenges ahead of an election that could drastically shift power in Washington and statehouses across the country.

"I'm here because Cindy's doing great, but I just got to make sure. I can't take a chance," Trump told the crowd, his voice audibly hoarse, as he worked to boost Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith, who is facing a surprisingly stiff challenge from Democrat Scott Colom.

Colom was spotted at the rally site, too, creating the rare spectacle of Trump's staunchest supporters interacting directly with a figure the president would soon go on to denigrate as a "Marxist radical" and "fake" and "shameless phony who pretends to be a moderate" from the stage.

Mississippi, among the nation's most reliably Republican states, is not where the Republicans envisioned needing a late-October stop from the president. But Colom is one of many Democratic underdogs who have managed to expand the House and Senate battleground maps because of widespread voter discontent over inflation, the Iran war and other aspects of Trump's second presidency.

He said he had planned to go to the rally site to listen to what was on Trump supporters' minds.

"They actually deserve a senator that will listen to them, that actually cares about what they think, actually cares about what they're going through," Colom said ahead of the president's arrival.

Trump's event kicks off a week of aggressive campaigning that will include more stops in states he won comfortably in 2024, including Texas, South Carolina and Iowa. Trump also plans to hold a rally Saturday in Salina, Kansas, officials announced late Monday, marking his second visit to the state in less than two weeks.

Former Vice President Kamala Harris speaks at Women for Crowley event Monday, Oct. 26, 2026, in Milwaukee. Nam Y. Huh/AP hide caption

Top Democratic candidates and their surrogates were also greeting voters — more than 30 million of whom have already cast ballots, according to data collected by The Associated Press from election administrators across the country — as they try to end Republicans' threadbare majority in the House, reclaim control of the Senate and claw back power in state capitals.

Former Vice President Kamala Harris, Democrats' 2024 presidential candidate, campaigned in Wisconsin with Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley, who faces Rep. Tom Tiffany, a Trump ally, in the governor's race. If elected, Crowley would be the first Black governor to lead the battleground state.

"This is a tight race because the stakes are so high," Harris said at an event billed as a "Women for Crowley" rally.

Meanwhile, in Michigan, Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed distanced himself from an ad created by a group called Rebellion PAC that blames high grocery prices on U.S. support for Israel.

"This ad is not affiliated in any way with the Abdul for Senate campaign," a spokesperson for El-Sayed, Roxie Richner, said in a statement about the spot, which features a couple discussing U.S. support for Israel as they shop for groceries.

"Apparently it's costing us a lot of money to drop bombs on Gaza," the woman says.

The ad had been condemned by U.S. Rep. Haley Stevens, who narrowly lost to El-Sayed in the Democratic primary.

The way to win Michigan "is not by blaming the high costs of our goods on the only Jewish homeland in the world," Stevens wrote on X.

El-Sayed, who would be the country's first Muslim senator, has denounced antisemitism while fiercely criticizing Israel for its military operations in Gaza — a position that has been a frequent point of attack for both Republicans and supporters of Israel in his party.

Vice President JD Vance reacts after speaking at a campaign rally at Bozeman Yellowstone International Airport, Monday, Oct. 26, 2026, in Belgrade, Mont. Finn Gomez//POOL Getty Images North America hide caption

Vice President JD Vance returned to Kansas to stump for Sen. Roger Marshall, who finds himself in a tight contest with Democratic nominee and pastor Adam Hamilton. Hundreds of people were gathered inside the warehouse of a steel-fabricating company in north Topeka in a state where Democrats haven't won a Senate race since 1932.

Marshall, who has raised far less money than Hamilton, told the crowd that Trump is on the ballot, even if his name doesn't appear, because of how Democrats will thwart his agenda if they win control of Congress.

"Democrats are coming out like it's a presidential year," he said, calling on the GOP to match Democrats' energy.

Jace Sullivan, a 50-year-old electrician from Topeka who describes himself as "85% Republican," said Trump supporters need to "circle the wagons" and get out to vote.

Wearing a red "JD Vance for President 2028" shirt, he said he thinks Trump is rightly working to keep Iran from getting nuclear weapons.

"Gas prices are high and it does suck, but it's a means to an end," Sullivan said.

Trump, at the Mississippi rally, said he had, in fact, expected gas prices to rise even higher than they had.

"It worked out fine and it'll be over soon," he said of the war with Iran.

Earlier, Vance was in Belgrade, Montana, to campaign with Republican Aaron Flint, who is facing Democrat Sam Forstag in the race for the state's 1st Congressional District, and Republican Senate candidate Kurt Alme.

Despite the visits to ruby red states, Sen. Tim Scott, who serves as chairman of the National Republican Senatorial Committee, told The Associated Press Monday that GOP candidates are "closing very strongly" in races across the country, including South Carolina, which Trump is set to visit for the second time in as many weeks to stump for GOP Sen. Darline Graham.

"One of the reasons why we will be in the majority is because he has invested the time and the energy on the campaign trail," Scott said on the sidelines of U.S. Rep. Russell Fry's 3rd Annual Freedom Fry event.

But it's Trump's visit to Mississippi that perhaps brings the midterm landscape into the sharpest relief with just a week of campaigning left.

To be sure, Hyde-Smith, who is vying for her second full term, remains the favorite in a state Trump won three times by an average of nearly 20 percentage points.

But Colom, a local prosecutor who would make history as Mississippi's first Black senator since post-Civil War Reconstruction, is looking to parlay inflation, Trump's lagging popularity and Hyde-Smith's mistakes into an upset.

Senate Democrats' super PAC has been a late entry in the contest, with a $1.2 million investment. Mississippians have not chosen a new Democratic senator since 1947, and Democrats last won a Senate race there when that senator, the onetime segregationist John Stennis, won his last reelection in 1982.

In New York, meanwhile, one of the party's most vulnerable House members pulled from Trump's campaign playbook. Rep. Mike Lawler, who is in a tight race with Democrat Cait Conley in New York City's northern suburbs, manned a McDonald's drive-thru — mimicking something Trump did two years ago, even as Lawler has tried to emphasize his independence from the president.

Die Werkself are aiming to bounce back from defeat last time out, while Die Legende are looking for a first win in four games despite arriving as underdogs.

Since making their false start in the Bundesliga, Leverkusen have established themselves as a force to be reckoned with under Carles Martinez Novell.

However, Martinez Novell's side followed that up with an eight-game undefeated run across all competitions, including a six-match winning streak that featured four clean sheets.

That impressive stretch was brought to an end on Sunday, when Leverkusen were surprisingly beaten 3-0 by Hoffenheim on the road, failing to respond to a flurry of late goals from Die Kraichgauer.

Keen to get back on track in midweek, Die Werkself will be hoping for a repeat of their first-round DFB-Pokal triumph over Wehen Wiesbaden, which saw the top-flight club run out deserving 4-0 winners on August 22.

Meanwhile, Miroslav Klose's Nuremberg have shown a similar level of resilience to their opponents this season, and will certainly pose a challenge for their hosts on Wednesday.

Die Legende began their campaign with a nine-game unbeaten run across all competitions, winning six times in the process.

Much like Leverkusen, Klose's side saw their streak come crashing down this past weekend, when they fell to a narrow 1-0 defeat to Kaiserslautern.

Regardless, Nuremberg remain second in the 2. Bundesliga and firmly in the hunt for promotion, and as one of the strongest clubs in the division, they will be excited for the chance to show their strength.

That being said, the visitors only scraped through 5-4 on penalties against Viktoria Koln after a 1-1 stalemate in the DFB-Pokal's first round, and without a win in three matches - a spell that goes back to mid-September - fans will be harbouring doubts.

Elsewhere, 17-year-old defensive midfielder Kennet Eichhorn is working his way back to full fitness after his metabolic disorder stabilised, so Ezequiel Fernandez and Aleix Garcia may continue in the hosts' double pivot.

Right-back options Montrell Culbreath and Guela Doue are also sidelined with foot and calf injuries, though Jarell Quansah will be ready to start.

As for Nuremberg, they are lighter in defence than ideal, missing centre-back Mikayil Faye and left-back Kristian Mandic, while Finn Otto was forced off against Kaiserslautern, so Alexios Kalogeropoulos and Styopa Mkrtchyan could be joined by Tim Janisch and Eric Porstner in Klose's backline.

Elsewhere, midfielder Finn Becker is unavailable and left winger Mohamed Ali Zoma is recovering from a dislocated shoulder, though Adam Markhiev and Javier Fernandez should be on hand to start in the centre of the park, with Rayan Ghrieb and Rafael Lubach out wide.

#3627. Dead Man’s Bones Unveil First New Song in 17 Years

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After a short teaser clip lit up the internet with news of their return, Dead Man’s Bones—the duo of Ryan Gosling and Zach Shields—are officially back with their first new music in 17 years. “Lost in the Night” was produced with longtime collaborator Tim Anderson, and it gets a black-and-white music video starring Hamnet’s Noah Jupe and The Summer I Turned Pretty’s Rain Spencer that draws on its lovestruck lyrics.

Dead Man’s Bones set out to write new music in recent years, but couldn‘t settle on where to make it. The duo tried a number of Los Angeles studios before driving to a farm outside of the city with some gear in tow—two guitars, two drum machines, and two synthesizers—where they proceeded to record “Lost in the Night” in one day.

The first and only Dead Man’s Bones album came out in 2009. Gosling and Shields worked closely with the Silverlake Conservatory Children’s Choir, a California education organization founded by Flea, on the self-titled debut to achieve its cross of indie rock, gothic folk, and doo-wop. The duo retreated from the public eye after promoting their first album. However, they found renewed interest over the years through covers (like Cat Power tackling “Pa Pa Power” for her 2022 album Covers), various film and TV syncs, and a seasonal bump courtesy of social media users playing their music around Halloween.

#294. Here’s the latest.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s far-right coalition faces an opposition loosely led by a former military chief of staff. Election officials reported record turnout but a clear winner might not emerge immediately.

Aaron BoxermanDavid M. HalbfingerLeo Sands and Max Bearak

Polls close soon in an election that has seen Israelis vote in record numbers. In what is widely seen as the most consequential election in the country’s history, voters were deciding whether to keep Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his far-right coalition in power or chart a new course.

By 8 p.m. local time (2 p.m. Eastern), two hours before polls were scheduled to close, 74.3 percent of eligible voters had cast ballots, the highest rate for that point in the day since 1988.

Turnout appeared to be particularly high among Arab citizens, who make up about a fifth of Israel’s population. By late afternoon, hours before the polls were set to close, turnout in some Arab-majority towns had exceeded the totals from the last election, in 2022.

Responding to the high turnout in those towns, Mr. Netanyahu accused Arab citizens of “trying to steal the elections,” repeatedly using brazen and overtly racial appeals to push his right-wing base to vote. An attempt by his Likud party on Tuesday to have voting hours extended was rejected by the Central Election Committee.

The election is Israel’s first since the Hamas-led attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, which was the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust and ignited three years of war that left Gaza largely in ruins and nearly all its population displaced.

Voters have a choice between two blocs offering starkly different visions. Mr. Netanyahu, the leader of the most far-right government in Israeli history, built a coalition of nationalists and ultra-Orthodox Jews, which faces an ideologically diverse group of Jewish opposition parties loosely led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff whose son was killed in the Gaza war. Parties representing Arab citizens of Israel also staunchly oppose Mr. Netanyahu’s government.

Both sides are framing the election as a make-or-break moment for the country. Mr. Netanyahu’s coalition offers the promise of a more religious Israel with an ever-tighter grip over the West Bank and Gaza. His opponents are casting the vote as the last chance to preserve democracy and pull Israel back from the brink of becoming a pariah state under international sanctions.

Voting will close at 10 p.m. (4 p.m. Eastern time), but there is unlikely to be an immediate determination of who will be the next prime minister. Opinion polls suggest that Mr. Netanyahu’s allies and rivals are roughly tied, and assembling a governing coalition in Israel’s parliamentary system could take weeks or months.

Opposition leaders led by Mr. Eisenkot have called for a reckoning over what they call Mr. Netanyahu’s failure to prevent the catastrophic Oct. 7 attacks. They also have pledged to repair Israel’s shattered image abroad, including by cracking down on Israeli extremist violence against Palestinians in the occupied West Bank.

Mr. Netanyahu has resisted the establishment of an independent commission to investigate the Oct. 7 attacks. He has vowed to move ahead with overhauling Israel’s judicial system, a move that critics say could undermine the country’s liberal democracy. And his allies say they hope to permanently entrench Israeli rule in the West Bank and Gaza despite international condemnation.

The 77-year-old leader has dominated Israeli politics for decades. In recent days, he repeatedly suggested that the country could face annihilation without him in charge.

Early indicator: Exit polls in Israel can be useful to get a sense of each party’s relative strength, but they are not considered a reliable gauge of precise results. And in a tight race like this one, placing too much stock in them can be unwise.

Vote-counting: Votes are tallied in Israel the old-fashioned way, with ballot slips counted by hand at more than 12,000 polling places across the country. They are supervised in that count by camera-toting election observers.

First results: After the hand count is completed, paper tallies are taken to regional hubs where the results are recorded electronically. The whole process takes hours, and the authorities begin to release the results once they are completed.

Possible delay: Israel is a parliamentary democracy, and no party is expected to win anywhere near an outright majority of the votes. Once the results are in, the process of forming a government will begin. That could take weeks or even months.

Next government: The party with the most votes and the most seats in the Parliament may not be the one to form a government. That happened after the 2021 election, when Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud Party won the most seats, but could not assemble a majority coalition.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s governing coalition has the distinction of surviving for a full four-year term in office, something no government in Israel has achieved for more than 30 years. Since first taking office in 1996, he has tallied 19 years in power and become Israel’s longest-serving prime minister by far.

On Tuesday, he is seeking a seventh term, in Israel’s first parliamentary election since the deadly Hamas-led attack in 2023 that prompted its devastating war in Gaza.

The longevity of his most recent governing coalition followed three years during which a cycle of collapsed coalitions and five snap elections had left the country without a functional government or national budget.

In 2021, Israelis ousted Mr. Netanyahu from power and then returned him to office 18 months later. He had staged a previous comeback, in 2009, becoming prime minister a decade after his party was voted out of power.

In 2021, Mr. Netanyahu’s government collapsed after failing to pass a budget, setting off legislative elections. At the time, he had been indicted on charges of fraud, bribery and breach of trust, and the coalition he was leading was shaky. According to analysts, heading into an election was a gambit by Mr. Netanyahu to strengthen his grip on power before his criminal trial.

But the electorate that voted that March didn’t give him the seats he needed, ending a period of 12 straight years in office for him. It took months of wrangling for an eclectic coalition of anti-Netanyahu forces to cobble together a single-seat majority in the Parliament. Naftali Bennett, a right-wing former ally of Mr. Netanyahu’s, led the coalition as prime minister, hammering out a deal with Yair Lapid, a centrist and former broadcaster who was appointed foreign minister.

The eight-party coalition proved too unwieldy to endure. After two right-wing lawmakers defected and several on the left rebelled in critical votes, that government collapsed in June 2022.

After the resulting elections, held in November 2022, Mr. Netanyahu returned to power as right-wing voters moved to more extreme parties, diminishing the center left.

Mr. Netanyahu’s Likud party allied with far-right and ultra-Orthodox lawmakers to secure a clear majority in the Parliament, forming the most hard-line and religious conservative government in Israel’s history.

The coalition accounted for 48.4 percent of the vote but had a parliamentary majority because two anti-Netanyahu parties — one leftist, one Arab nationalist — fell just short of the share of votes needed to obtain seats.

It was a reminder of how tiny swings among voters can have a big effect under Israel’s parliamentary system.

Mr. Netanyahu has since overseen an acceleration of the country’s rightward shift, including an effort to overhaul the judiciary that prompted huge street protests. Questions about whether his government could have better pre-empted the Oct. 7 attack have also become hot-button issues.

The election in Israel on Tuesday is unlikely to yield quick answers about who might lead the next government.

Voters don’t directly pick a prime minister in Israel’s proportional parliamentary system. They choose a party or slate, the first step in determining who may be selected to try to form a government. The process is not guaranteed to succeed and can lead to another election if it does not. Here are four things to watch for.

This is considered one of the most consequential elections in Israel’s history, and voters turned out in record numbers.

Recent polls had suggested that voter turnout could be at least as high or higher than in the last general election, in 2022. That year, slightly more than 70 percent of eligible voters — nearly 4.8 million out of nearly 6.8 million people — participated. That election followed four other votes in quick succession from 2019 to 2021 in which turnout hovered near 70 percent.

This year, there are 7.3 million eligible voters, about 540,000 more than in 2022.

Parties must clear a threshold of 3.25 percent of the total vote in order to get a seat in the Knesset, or Parliament.

But no party is expected to gain a majority of 61 parliamentary seats, according to the polls. That means there will likely be lots of post-election negotiations among those parties who pass the threshold.

Parties or lists that do not reach the threshold lose those ballots. As a result, parties and candidates lobby voters not to waste their votes on a list that seems unlikely to meet this criterion.

She said she would vote for the first time after previously boycotting the political system, which she views as discriminatory toward Arabs. “I don’t want to be the kind of person who clings to rigid principles when reality changes,” she added.

Many others have also stayed home on Election Day in the past, alienated by political leaders, convinced their ballots hold no sway or unwilling to validate a system that they say treats them as second-class citizens.

Arab politicians and local leaders are warning of voter-suppression efforts, through either disinformation campaigns or the presence of armed right-wing activists near polling stations in Arab villages.

Last week, Mr. Netanyahu’s Likud party announced a joint effort with another right-wing party to send 2,000 activists to oversee “voting integrity” in Arab cities and towns. The move was widely seen as an attempt to suppress Arab voters.

In the 2015 election, Mr. Netanyahu sought to prod supporters by saying that Arab voters were “heading to the polls in huge quantities.” After critics accused him of race-baiting, he apologized for any offense caused by the remark.

But on Tuesday, he tried a similar tactic, pointing to apparently high turnout among Arab voters and suggesting that they were “trying to steal the elections.” (Election authorities said there had been no indication of widespread irregularities.) Mr. Netanyahu’s Likud party released a poster showing a crowd of women in hijab, urging right-wing Israelis “not to stay home.”

Yousef Makladeh, a leading pollster focusing on Arab society in Israel, said recent surveys indicated that 65 percent to 70 percent of Arab voters planned to vote on Tuesday. That would be an increase of 12 to 18 percentage points over turnout in the 2022 election.

For the very first time, activists say, efforts to turn out the vote feel like pushing on an open door.

Omar Abu Siyam, a social media personality from Mu’awiya, an Arab village near Haifa, Israel, said that when he told his nearly 90,000 Instagram followers that he had changed his mind and decided to vote, hundreds of commenters responded that they felt the same way.

The most critical issue for Arab voters by a wide margin, Mr. Makladeh said, is the surge in violent crime within their communities, a crisis many say the police have deliberately neglected under Itamar Ben-Gvir, the national security minister.

“Arabs are terrified of what four more years with Ben-Gvir in office will do to us,” said Dr. Thabet Abu Ras, a political geographer who is advising voter-turnout initiatives across Arab municipalities. He added that discriminatory housing policies, resulting in a record number of home demolitions in Arab communities, were also driving voters.

Higher Arab turnout could threaten smaller parties that are more likely to support Mr. Netanyahu’s coalition. By increasing the total number of votes cast, it would raise the threshold for smaller parties to win seats in the Knesset.

“This time,” Dr. Abu Ras said, “the Arab vote is worth gold.”
