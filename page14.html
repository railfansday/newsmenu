Mazda and Seiko are two very important Japanese companies; both exhibit true heritage and precision when it comes to car making and watchmaking. Between the two of them is an expansive back catalog that features some of the most iconic Japanese creations of all time.

So it wouldn’t come as a shock to anyone that these 2 companies would work together to create more icons, but this time in the form of a timekeeper. The latest collaboration model has just been launched in Japan, leaving the rest of the world in envy.

Mazda just dropped its newest collaboration timepiece with Seiko (BF9504), a chronograph that embodies what Mazda calls the “aesthetics of subtraction and condensed elegance.” Mazda says the BF9504 draws on its award-winning design language while keeping the functionality of a chronograph. Both brands wanted a minimalist design, free of unnecessary colors and decorations on the dial, while the red stopwatch second hand and crown serve as an accent.

One thing that makes this collaboration stand out from the others is that it’s the first to use curved Hardlex glass. The curved glass gives the design a 3D impression, and the glass itself has been further strengthened to resist scratches. The 3D glass also offers a unique viewing experience depending on the angle, which the designers were also aiming for.

The dial itself was inspired by Mazda’s clusters and meters, boasting excellent legibility. Its design contrasts black metal with red accents, and the font is called Mazda Type, an original to the brand. Another key detail is embedded on the watch: “ESTD 1920 HIROSHIMA, JAPAN.” This small detail shows just how long the Mazda story has been and how far it’s come.

The complete package includes two interchangeable straps in leather or steel. Even the watchbox is special, featuring a tri-color design that echoes the watch it houses. For the very lucky who can purchase the watch in Japan, it’ll cost 69,300 Japanese yen ($439 converted), and it will be available for order through dealers starting October 29.

As a fun fact, this model is the third collaboration between Mazda and Seiko; the first was in 2001, a vintage rotary-themed model. The most recent was a watch commemorating Mazda’s race team, the Mazda Spirit Racing model, which was sold last year.

Crypto trading revenue excluding Robinhood Chain fell 6% from Q2, while prediction markets revenue climbed to $173 million.

Robinhood's crypto revenue jumped 20% from the previous quarter to $120 million, offsetting a decline in crypto trading revenue, the company reported Tuesday.

In its third-quarter earnings, Robinhood Chain brought in $26 million in its first quarter on mainnet after launching on July 1.

Excluding this, crypto transaction revenue fell 6% from the second quarter to $94 million, while total crypto revenue remained down 55% from a year earlier when crypto markets were nearing bull market highs.

Crypto trading volumes also ramped up from last quarter, rising 23% to $49.8 billion — $20.6 billion coming from the Robinhood app itself and $29.2 billion on Bitstamp, the exchange it acquired last year.

The company said Robinhood Chain, its layer 2 blockchain built on Arbitrum, processed more than $80 billion in DEX volume and made over $50 million in fees during the quarter.

Prediction markets also continued to add more revenue than crypto. Event contracts generated $173 million, up 11% from the previous quarter and more than 6x from a year earlier. That includes $28 million from Rothera, its joint exchange with Susquehanna.

Robinhood's total net revenue rose 12% year over year to a record $1.42 billion while net income fell 14% to $476 million.

Robinhood shares Robinhood Markets, Inc. (HOOD) fell 5.5% in after-hours trading to $89.22, leaving the stock down roughly 22% year-to-date.

#5142. Dell sees AI agent guardrails as a path to greater autonomy

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As enterprises put AI agents to work, they face a challenge: How can they give those systems more autonomy while keeping AI agent guardrails in place?

Security and resilience are becoming central considerations as enterprises decide which tasks to delegate to AI agents. Those decisions extend beyond individual actions to the data protection and cyber resilience capabilities that support the business, according to John Scimone (pictured), president and chief security officer of Dell Technologies Inc.

“The good news is, this is the best time to get it right,” Scimone said. “Organizations are today designing agents that are going to be working for years ahead. At that point of design is always the best time to get security done well, to get resilience done well. Security resilience by design for agentic is a huge opportunity.”

Scimone spoke with theCUBE’s Dave Vellante for the AI Cybersecurity and Resilience Summit, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how cyber resilience has replaced traditional disaster recovery, how security leaders brief boards and how much autonomy enterprises should give AI agents. (* Disclosure below.)

Business resilience once meant disaster recovery built around wildfires, hurricanes and grid outages. Today, a widespread cyberattack across an entire digital ecosystem is the more likely scenario, according to Scimone. Agents add a new dependency because work handed to software stops when the software does.

“What happens if these agents aren’t available?” Scimone said. “What happens if the data that the agents are deciding on is not available? A core part of your resilience posture has to contend with the notion that if our agents go down, our work is not happening.”

Companies are still cautious about letting agents act alone. A survey of 25 enterprise technology and security leaders  led by theCUBE Research’s Krista Case found that 19 had autonomous agents in production. None allowed those agents to perform high-risk actions without human approval.

“It doesn’t mean doing it willy-nilly and just throwing caution to the wind,” he said. “It means being very thoughtful around where can’t you fail, putting deterministic guardrails in place … and letting the business run. Right now, I’m seeing too much caution in the space on that front.”

Dell applies that discipline internally, giving every agent tight identity and access control, extensive logging and traceability of decisions and actions, Scimone explained. That groundwork matters because only four of the survey’s 25 respondents had tested an AI agent incident scenario. Dell has also expanded its cyber resilience tools and joined OpenAI Group PBC’s call for collective action on cyber defense.

Attackers have used AI for years but haven’t yet deployed it at full scale, according to Scimone. He expects that to change soon.

“We haven’t hit that curve that I do believe is coming, and in the coming quarters, not in the coming years,” he said.

Vellante pointed to one encouraging finding from the survey: Organizations granting agents bounded autonomy showed stronger recovery readiness than those relying solely on human approval. In Scimone’s view, AI agent guardrails designed up front let companies take more risk, not less.

“Organizations that design those governance systems upfront are going to be the ones, ironically, that can move the quickest and can take the most risk because they can do it confidently with the right insights and the right data to know that they’re not overstepping,” Scimone said. “At the same time, they’re not under-leaning.”

U.S. President Donald Trump is facing yet another court challenge to his tariff-driven war on trade, after an unbroken string of prior defeats that leave the future of his mercurial trade policy increasingly uncertain.

Last week, the same company that successfully challenged Trump’s global tariffs under the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act filed suit against his latest attempt to reach back in time. At issue is Trump’s use of an obscure and never-used statute, Section 338 of the 1930 Trade Act, to punish Canada for its allegedly discriminatory behavior toward U.S. commerce.

This new trade fight may not be as clear-cut as some of the prior Trump defeats in court, though.

The central thrust of the lawsuit challenging Sec. 338—part of the notorious Smoot-Hawley law that ushered in the Great Depression—is that the law was explicitly designed for a different world.

The statute, which had never been used and in fact was unknown even to many trade experts until Trump resurrected it, was designed to give the United States a way to push back against countries such as France and Great Britain that were discriminating against U.S. exports as part of “imperial preference”—a circling of the wagons that gave preferential treatment to colonies at the expense of global trade in the dawning days of the depression.

The statute was “all about imperial preferences,” said Sara Albrecht, the chairman and chief executive of the Liberty Justice Center, which filed the lawsuit on behalf of a U.S. business harmed by Trump’s latest trade spat.

And even more clearly, the statute was meant to give the White House a tool to deter other countries from discriminating against U.S. exports—not a cudgel to wield against countries (such as Canada) whose tariffs on U.S. goods were dollar-for-dollar retaliation for Trump’s first round of trade restrictions.

“Retaliation is not discrimination,” Albrecht told Foreign Policy.

But even if the narrowly tailored 1930 statute were valid for modern-day trade fights, that doesn’t matter, the suit (and many scholars) argue, because Sec. 338 is—legally speaking—dead and buried.

That 1930 law has been updated and superseded not once but twice in later legislation, first through the 1962 Trade Act and later in Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act. Everything that was in the old statute was rehashed in more modern law—and, according to established legal doctrine, that Hoover-era law has arguably been implicitly repealed. What’s more, the updated version of the statute includes a lot more limits and safeguards, including formal investigation of alleged wrongdoing by other countries. The tool that Trump has turned to for his fourth bite at the apple would dodge all those limits.

“Congress did not intend to leave in place a parallel authority that would allow the President to impose the same remedies, for the same conduct, free of every one of those safeguards,” the plaintiffs argued in their lawsuit before the U.S. Court of International Trade.

The Trump administration has leaned heavily on that updated version of the statute—Sec. 301 of the 1974 Act—for many of its tariffs, from the first-term duties on Chinese exports to the second-term attempt to slap tariffs on every U.S. trading partner. (Those tariffs are also currently facing an uphill battle in court.)

But if Sec. 338 is still valid, scholars such as Philip Zelikow argue, then Sec. 301 cannot be. That would mean that the main weapon the Trump administration is relying on to continue global tariffs in the wake of this year’s Supreme Court setback is itself invalid: Trump cannot have his cake and eat it, too.

The whole battle over Trump’s tariffs comes at a time when the United States continues to pursue elusive trade deals with major partners, including Canada. Trump administration officials have said that a deal with Canada—with whom the United States has had a free-trade agreement since the 1990s—is close, but Canadian officials say a final agreement remains far off.

This latest clash over what seems to be an arcane relic of U.S. trade legislation is important because of the way that the Trump administration has sought to weaponize trade to a much greater degree in the second term than in the first. During Trump’s first term, his administration relied on relatively targeted and legally defensible authorities to slap tariffs on big trading partners.

But starting early in his second term, Trump sought to hike tariffs on every country in the world, starting with a novel use of a Carter administration-era law that had never been used to impose tariffs—and with good reason, as the Supreme Court later ruled. That 1977 law doesn’t give the president the power to levy tariffs, even for a self-proclaimed national-security “emergency,” such as a half-century of persistent trade deficits.

Undeterred, the Trump administration replaced the outlawed first batch of tariffs with a new tranche under a provision of the 1974 Trade Act. That, too, was struck down by courts, but it had expired by then anyway.

So the administration reached for a third option, also under the 1974 law, which hit 99 percent of U.S. trade with tariffs on the grounds that every U.S. trading partner was lax on combating forced labor in supply chains. That attempt, too, is under legal challenge, and it’s likely to be struck down.

And then Trump reached even further back in the history books to find a way to punish Canada for retaliating against his previous rounds of tariffs by invoking, for the first time, a statute that even protectionist President Herbert Hoover was loath to sign into law.

The irony is that all of Trump’s efforts to hike import duties paid by U.S. businesses and consumers by every means possible is that they have conspicuously failed to achieve any of their stated goals.

Exports are falling and imports are rising, widening the very trade deficit that Trump said was a national emergency. Tariffs have failed to drive a manufacturing renaissance, either in employment or the construction of new factories. Nor have the tariffs been a reliable source of additional revenue for a government saddled with a huge budget deficit and $40 trillion in debt: Net tariff revenues as a share of overall revenues are actually lower than at any time in the past decade, in part because courts have made the U.S. government refund tariffs collected illegally.

And increasing the price of nearly everything from nearly every country in the world has exacerbated inflation, which is a problem for the Trump administration as it tries to grapple with an affordability crisis ahead of the midterm elections. U.S. inflation is now running at 3.7 percent year-on-year, higher than when Trump took office, fueled by higher energy prices (fruit of the Iran war), tariffs, and the artificial intelligence build-out. That self-inflicted higher inflation makes a serious case for the Federal Reserve to hike interest rates this week—the very opposite course of what Trump has advocated for years.

The through line of all of Trump’s second-term tariff fights, from the Rose Garden “Liberation Day” tariff announcement that slapped duties on penguins to his efforts to resuscitate moribund trade relics to punish Canada, is that Congress is missing in action. Despite a handful of tepid attempts early in Trump’s second term to wrest back the constitutional powers of taxation from the White House, there have been no serious legislative efforts to reverse nearly a century of congressional delegation of trade authority to the executive branch.

“If Congress were doing its job, we wouldn’t be in this place,” said Albrecht of the Liberty Justice Center.

That may soon change. U.S. midterm elections are only a week away, and Democrats are widely predicted to win back the House of Representatives and perhaps even control of the Senate. Trump’s one-man war on trade (and trade law) may soon have an expiration date.
