I've been singing the praises of merino wool in these pages for over five years. Most people have come around to the fact that merino wool is a super fiber; it's both sustainable and quite remarkable in its capabilities. Merino wool somehow manages to be comfortable in 95-degree heat, and it'll insulate you against below-freezing temperatures. Unlike synthetic fibers derived from petroleum, merino wool resists odors and is naturally breathable. What's not to love? The price.

Merino wool isn't cheap. That's why I love these merino T-shirts from Quince: They're just $42.

The Quince All-Season T-shirt is 100 percent extra-fine merino wool with a 165-gsm weave, which is perfect even on the warmest summer days where I live. (Admittedly, I think the hottest it got this summer was 92 Fahrenheit). I like the relaxed fit here as well. It's neither skintight technical nor overly loose and baggy. It fits like a T-shirt is supposed to. That's what Quince does well in a number of categories: offer a solid, no-nonsense, competitively priced merino wool T-shirt that fits well and feels great. As an added bonus, Quince offers free shipping and free returns for 365 days. I haven't actually tested either—I haven't needed to, my Quince T-shirt is still going strong after two years of use. But there are plenty of customer reviews on Reddit suggesting the company does honor that offer.

Not sold on the idea of a wool T-shirt? I understand. When I started testing merino, I too had visions of lumberjacks in plaid and itchy sweaters someone's grandma had knitted. None of that is merino wool. Merino sheep have thinner, softer wool, which has evolved to keep them comfortable across a wide range of temperatures and is comfortable to wear next to your skin.

One wool myth that's not just a myth is care. Merino isn't cotton. Quince's care instructions for the All Season T-shirt say to wash it in cold water and lay flat to dry. The latter is important, as hanging merino wool to dry will stretch it out (because of the water weight). Can you throw it in the dryer? Yes. I have, for testing purposes only. It will shrink, and it will wear it out much, much faster, but you can do it.

The charging-speed war is fully on in the United States, with ChargePoint announcing on Tuesday that its latest EV charger can send a whopping 750 kilowatts to one plug. But the Express Solo isn't just about power—it promises to make high-powered fast-charging stations easier to install, expand, and operate thanks to some clever engineering.

Developed with Eaton, the Express Solo combines the charger and power conversion equipment in a single enclosure. It eliminates the need for a large, separate power cabinet, reducing the wiring and groundwork needed to get everything connected up. ChargePoint calls it the most powerful standalone charger in the U.S.

The Solo comes in 200-, 400-, and 600-kW configurations that can deliver up to 800 amps. In order to achieve the headline charging power figure of 750 kW delivered to a single port, you need at least two connected stations. They will share available power across up to four connected vehicles, meaning that all four won't get 750 kW simultaneously. But if a vehicle shows up that can accept 750 kW and there aren't a bunch of other vehicles plugged in, then it may pull that peak amount. That wouldn't be a passenger vehicle, of course, but rather an electric big rig like the Tesla Semi, which can pull over a megawatt.

ChargePoint pitches the Solo as the basis of a charging system that can distribute the available power to make the best use of it. So if one car can only accept a modest charging rate, the system can direct more of the available output to another that can use it.

ChargePoint and Eaton’s earlier microgrid announcement outlined how chargers could draw power directly from on-site batteries and solar panels, promising to reduce conversion losses and reliance on grid electricity when it’s most expensive.

The Solo also offers practical improvements for drivers, such as an Omni Port system of built-in adapters in order to offer both CCS and NACS connectors. The cables are suspended on moving arms, allowing the plug to reach any car's plug location, ChargePoint says. It also touts cut-resistant cables and an alarm system help deter vandalism by using the station’s speakers, screen, and lights to draw attention when it detects tampering.

For drivers, finding a more powerful charger won't shorten your stop if your EV can't take the extra power. But the ability to more easily shift power between plugs at a station could be a big convenience upgrade. If you've ever pulled up to a charging station only to find a Chevy Bolt occupying the last available 350-kW stall, you know that charging capacity isn't always put to its best use.

#112. Nanotyrannus skeleton sells for $47.3m in New York auction

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The mounted skeleton of a newly-identified dinosaur once mistaken for a young Tyrannosaurus rex has sold at auction for a colossal $47.3m (Â£35.5m).

Nicknamed Jodi, the roughly 10-foot-tall (3m) skeleton was auctioned by Sotheby's in New York. The sale comes a year after researchers confirmed that the skeleton belongs to a distinct genus.

The Late Cretaceous carnivore was expected to fetch at least $9m when it went under the hammer on Tuesday. The auction comes after Sotheby's sold a Tyrannosaurus rex, dubbed Gus, for a record $50.1m in July.

The sale has reignited debate in the scientific community over whether dinosaur remains should be sold privately or displayed publicly.

Sotheby's described Jodi as an "excellent exhibition-ready" skeleton with about 191 fossil-bone elements and a "beautifully preserved, virtually complete skull".

The auction house added that the Nanotyrannus is "one of only three such skeletons known to science". The specimen is the first to come to market since the existence of the genus was confirmed by US researchers last October, Sotheby's said.

The identify of the buyer, who won after a thrilling 12 minutes of auction bidding between six interested parties, has not been disclosed.

It becomes the second-most valuable dinosaur fossil ever sold, behind Gus. The third-most valuable was a Stegosaurus named Apex, which was sold by Sotheby's in July 2024 for $44.6m.

Megan Jacobs, a palaeontologist at the University of Portsmouth, told BBC Radio 4's Today programme on Tuesday that Nanotyrannus "was for a very long time believed to be sort of a teenager of T. rex".

"But it's a lot smaller and its a lot more gracile. So it's very sort-of sleek, slender, [with] blade-like teeth for slicing through meat," Jacobs said.

Tyrannosaurus rex and Nanotyrannus lived alongside one another during the same geological period approximately 67 million years ago.

Jodi was initially exhibited as a juvenile T. rex at London's Frieze Masters international art fair in October 2023, before the Nanotyrannus genus was classified.

The skeleton was discovered in the US state of Montana by rancher Jodi Pierson on her land in the Hell Creek Formation, a heavily studied geological site. It was later excavated by commercial palaeontologist Clayton Phipps.

Phipps also discovered the famous "Dueling Dinosaurs" fossil. That specimen, which shows the remains of a Nanotyrannus and Triceratops apparently in combat, was found eight miles (13km) away from Jodi in 2006.

The "Dueling Dinosaurs" discovery became crucial to the research published by Lindsay Zanno and James Napoli that classified Nanotyrannus as separate from T. rex. Their paper ended decades of debate over whether the dinosaurs were distinct from one another.

The auction of Jodi comes as some scientists warn that auctions could herald a new era in fossil collecting by the ultra-rich.

Researchers told the BBC after the sale of Gus that such remains should be exhibited in public spaces where they can be more freely studied.

Prof Steve Brusatte, a palaeontologist at the University of Edinburgh and consultant on the Jurassic Park films, told BBC World Service's What in the World programme in July that the T. rex's auction was "concerning".

"I don't know of any museum or university that can afford a price tag like that," he said. "Imagine if the Mona Lisa was just owned by a super-wealthy person and it wasn't on display and people couldn't see it, couldn't enjoy it."

He said at the time that he hoped such a sale didn't become a trend, adding it could adversely affect scientific research and public education.

Responding to Tuesday's auction, Brusatte called it "harrowing" that Jodi could disappear from public view.

"We've really just found out that Nanotyrannus is real, and now one of the best skeletons is about to be sold to the highest bidder, and it's harrowing to think it could disappear into the vaults of a collector rather than displayed on a museum where it can wow and inspire kids and families and the public at large," he told the BBC.

But Jacobs told the Today programme on Tuesday there were two sides to the debate, and that a lot of time, effort and money went into excavating the specimens.

"I don't begrudge the people involved for wanting to sell it for such a large amount of money," she said. "Although its a scary prospect, actually a lot of them have ended up in the public eye... these things without a doubt will end end up in a museum at some point."

#5920. Review: Emirates A380 First Class, Still Spectacular After All These Years

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Emirates’ A380 first class offered a fabulous experience on my flight from Mumbai to Dubai, with a comfortable suite, a delicious breakfast, an onboard shower, and outstanding service. The only problem was that the flight was far too short. Even with my stomach still recovering from India, I would happily have stayed onboard all the way to Los Angeles.

This flight was one of the reasons I planned my trip to India in the first place. I wanted to revisit Emirates’ A380 first class and then try its newer Boeing 777-300ER first class suite on my connection to Brussels.

It had been several years since my last Emirates A380 first class flight. The suite is no longer cutting edge, but I was looking forward to the experience very much and I was not disappointed.

As I explained in the introduction to this trip report, I booked Mumbai–Dubai–Brussels for 102,000 Emirates Skywards miles and approximately $700 in taxes and fees.

That included this A380 flight and the following day’s flight to Brussels on a Boeing 777-300ER featuring Emirates’ “Game Changer” first class suites.

I booked before Emirates introduced its restrictions on first class award bookings. Having about 100,000 miles already in my Skywards account, this was an attractive way to use them and compare two different first class products.

The departure time from Mumbai was hardly ideal, but I was determined to make the most of the flight.

I left the Four Seasons Mumbai around 8:00 pm and arrived at the airport well ahead of departure. Emirates’ check-in counter was not ready to accept me and I was told to return at midnight. Thankfully, I had already checked in online and had my digital boarding pass. That allowed me to proceed through security and immigration rather than wait landside for a paper copy.

I spent the time before boarding in the first class section of the Adani Lounge.

About 70 minutes before departure, I headed over to the gate where I found a gaggle of people waiting to board, with a different line for every class of service. I had the first class line to myself. We boarded a bit late at 4:24 am (the exact reason for the delay was not given).

I boarded directly onto the upper deck, turning left into the first class cabin.

First class is located at the front of the upper deck, with 14 suites arranged in a 1-2-1 configuration. The window seats are the obvious choice when traveling alone, while the center pairs make more sense for those traveling together.

Each seat is 23 inches wide, expanding to 28 inches in bed mode, and reclines into a fully flat bed measuring 78 inches long. All suites have direct aisle access and sliding privacy doors, though the doors do not extend to the ceiling.

Storage included a large area underneath the screen/table, two smaller compartments, and a shelf with a flip-up vanity mirror and a basket of snacks. There are no overhead bins in first class, which makes the cabin feel more open, but means your carry-on bag stays on the floor inside your suite.

Next to the seat is a personal minibar stocked with soft drinks and a tablet that controls the seat functions. Having drinks within reach is convenient, though with such attentive service on this flight, I did not avail myself to anything.

Each suite also has two adjustable air vents, though the cabin never got warm.

I had selected 2A, but moved back to 3A when it became clear that no one was seated in the rear half of the cabin. There were only four first class passengers on this flight, which made an already spacious cabin feel even more private (moving also made taking pictures easier without someone sitting directly across the aisle).

The woodgrain and gold trim are certainly not subtle and it does looks dated now, but the suite was well-maintained and extremely comfortable. I don’t mind a little gaudy decor when everything works and the seat is this pleasant to sit in, plus I loved the mood lighting.

After breakfast and my shower, I returned to find that the crew had prepared the suite across the aisle as a bed.

That was a lovely touch. With so few passengers onboard, I could keep my own suite for sitting and dining and use another for resting. A 4:25 am departure would normally make sleep my first priority. On this flight, though, I wanted to enjoy the seat, breakfast, bar, and shower before closing my eyes.

There simply was not enough time for everything and there wasn’t time for anything more than a quick cat nap.

Slippers and pajamas were proactively provided, though I did not change into them on this short flight (a friend had requested them so I just put them in my bag). I also forgot to request an amenity kit (silly me), which are available via request. I’ll review the amenity kit when I review my next flight.

Breakfast was served on this early-morning flight. My stomach was still recovering from Delhi Belly, so this was not going to be the occasion to sample everything Emirates had loaded onboard.

I skipped the Champagne and caviar, hoping I would feel more adventurous on my flight to Brussels the following day. The menus are now magazine-style:

The crew began mothering me almost immediately when I explained my somatic issues, I was offered herbal tea as my pre-departure beverage, served with some biscuits.

Later on before pushback, I was offered Arabic coffee and dates.

For breakfast, I ordered a cheese omelet, which seemed like a reasonably safe choice.

(While it was being prepared, I walked back to the onboard lounge. Upon returning to my suite, I enjoyed a very good breakfast).

The omelet was good, but the accompaniments stole the show. A cube-shaped cinnamon roll was rich and delicious, as was the rhubarb yogurt…delicious.

A cappuccino completed the meal, plus more freshly cut fruit after my shower.

I would have liked to try more, but I was thankful to enjoy breakfast at all after the preceding day in Mumbai. The crew was so considerate about my condition, which I will discuss below.

One of my favorite features of the Emirates A380 is the lounge at the rear of the upper deck.

I love this unique feature to the A380 and although it takes up a lot of real estate, it is one of the primary things that gives Emirates an outsized reputation of quality and luxury. When I arrived, passengers and crew were watching a Belgium–USA World Cup match live.

Belgium was leading 1-0 when I reached the bar. The USA tied in, then Belgium scored again…and then two more times. The game was moving considerably faster than I expected, though my main interest was the lounge itself.

I was offered a glass of Krug (limited only to first class passengers), but skipped that due to my stomach issues.

On a longer flight, I could see myself spending quite a bit of time here. On this flight, breakfast was waiting and I still had a shower to take.

The A380’s shower spas remain a fabulous feature. At the front of the first class cabin, the large lavatories include space to change and an actual shower. This wasn’t my first shower onboard a flight, but being able to shower at 40,000 feet still makes me smile.

The bathroom was well-stocked with shower amenities:

You receive five minutes of running water, which can be stopped and started. That does require a little attention, but it is more than enough and the pressure was acceptable.

I was the only passenger who showered so I suppose I could have asked for a second one, haha.

I emerged refreshed and returned to the cabin, where my bed was waiting. Breakfast, a shower, and then a nap…quite a way to travel between Mumbai and Dubai.

I’ll write a separate post on the shower experience.

Emirates calls its entertainment system “ICE” and the large screen in the suite offers considerably more entertainment than anyone could use on a flight this short including hundreds of movies, TV shows, games, live TV, and music. A tablet in the side console makes it easy to scroll through content.

Noise-cancelling headphones were from Bowers & Wilkins and very nice.

I just enjoyed some classical music while watching the tailcam.

Emirates offers complimentary inflight Wi-Fi to Skywards members traveling in first class, provided your membership number is attached to the reservation. While it wasn’t Starlink (that’s coming), the OnAir system worked fairly well.

The purser, Miss Donna from the Philippines, was proactive, gracious, and welcoming. Miss Hee from South Korea was also fabulous. They were concerned about my stomach troubles and rather mothered me throughout the flight, which I appreciated very much.

A crew can make or break a flight, even with an excellent seat and this crew could not have been better.

There were only four passengers in first class, which certainly helped. But a lightly booked cabin does not automatically produce attentive service. It still requires people who care about their work and pay attention to the passengers in front of them.

The thoughtful preparation of a separate bed was one example. Their concern about how I was feeling and constant attention really made me feel special.

This was an early-morning flight of only about three hours, yet they made it a first class experience and I was very grateful.

Despite the late departure from Mumbai, we landed on time on Dubai, with the sun having just risen and an army of Emirates jets present on the ground.

My Emirates A380 first class flight from Mumbai to Dubai was excellent. The suite was comfortable and well-maintained, breakfast was delicious, and I enjoyed both the onboard lounge and shower.

But the crew made the strongest impression. Donna and Hee were wonderful, particularly while I was not feeling my best, and made the short flight very special. Emirates’ A380 first class is no longer the newest or necessarily the best suite in the sky, but that did not diminish how much I enjoyed this flight.

Next time, I would like a longer flight…and a stomach ready for the Champagne and caviar.

#205. Slovenia mourns loss of glacier on its highest peak after scorching summer

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Extreme heat accelerated disappearance of Triglav glacier from mountain revered as a symbol of Slovenian identity

The final ice patch has vanished from Slovenia’s Triglav glacier, the latest Alpine casualty of Europe’s scorching summer.

The once mighty ice mass lost 93% of its surface area between 1983 and 2003, shrinking to cover less than a hectare of overheated rock, but went on to surprise researchers who feared its imminent demise, clinging on to the mountain for two more decades.

In the last five years, which includes Europe’s three hottest years on record, the remaining ice has melted to a handful of isolated pockets, with the final remnant of the main body measuring just a few metres by the end of summer. New photos of the mountainside – taken either side of snowfall this month – confirm that no ice remains.

Matija Zorn, director of the Anton Melik Geographical Institute, said Mount Triglav “holds a central place in Slovenian national identity” and the demise of the glacier represents the loss of an important data source and indicator of climate change.

“Every year I knew that the ice was going to be less and less,” he said. “But I was shocked every year [by] how much less ice was up there.”

Fierce heatwaves made hotter by carbon pollution have ravaged the Alps and reduced big white glaciers to what scientists have called “moonlike” rock deserts. Last month, Swiss glaciologists found that nearly a fifth of the country’s ice had disappeared in the last five years, with a 5.5% drop in volume in 2026.

In Slovenia, home to the south-eastern tip of the Alps, the Triglav glacier suffered most of its decline in the last century. Historical photographs and documents suggest the glacier covered about 40 hectares in the mid-1800s. By 1946, when scientists conducted the first formal measurements, its area had shrunk to just 14 hectares.

Zorn, who first took measurements at the glacier in 2001, said the rate of change was surprising even in the decades in which the glacier defied expectations. The spot at which he had to put on crampons to prevent slipping jumped back year after year as the glacier receded.

“The melting is incredible,” he said. “In 2002 … we took a core of ice out for some geochemical analysis and it was like 5 metres [deep]. The next year, I was standing on the same spot … not on ice, but on rock.”

Triglav is Slovenia’s highest peak, a cultural touchpoint that every person in the country of skiers and climbers is expected to climb at least once. A second glacier on the Skuta mountain has also shrunk and thinned, but the remaining patch of ice is better shaded than Triglav and is expected to hold out for a few more years.

Western Europe suffered through its hottest summer on record this year, causing at least 50,000 estimated heat deaths and costly secondary effects that ranged from failed harvests to power plants being forced to shut down. Scientists are still assessing the damage done to ecosystems.

Miha Pavšek, a glaciologist and mountain guide, said “nature is changing in front of our eyes” but people were not yet taking it seriously.

#14. Frontier AI labs say their models refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis. What does that mean?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI released solutions to three blockbuster math problems that have long vexed researchers. One was a surprise rejection of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), a durable assumption in computer science that plays a large role in how we understand what is difficult to compute.

As with previous proof-drops from the frontier lab, scientific communities responded with a mixture of thrill and dismay. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge suggested that OpenAI was basing its solutions on pre-existing work, and hours later, Anthropic subsequently released its own refutation of ETH.

However, OpenAI’s process is evolving. This time, the lab structured its releases differently for each of the three disciplines involved: Mathematicians got the work dropped on GitHub; the computer scientists formed a working group, asked for the proofs to be shared publicly and OpenAI to fund a conference to discuss them; the physicists formed a working group that will determine how their result will be released.

MIT professor Ryan Williams is the computer scientist who is organizing his field’s response to the new finding. Williams, whose work focuses on these questions of computational complexity, has criticized OpenAI’s approach in the past, calling its proof dump earlier this October a “DDOS attack on mathematicians.”

However, he agreed to collaborate with the frontier labs in order to help shepherd ideas that are vital to the field, he told TechCrunch. The lab asked him to help interpret the solution, along with UC San Diego computer scientists Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who first published the ETH in 1999.

Their first step was to release an open letter calling for feedback from scientists interested in these problems and a discussion of the norms around AI contributions to their work.

“Some parts [of OpenAI’s proof] are, maybe you could say routine, but then yes, there are two or three alien-looking parts, and it’s exactly these parts that I saw that made me think, wow, this is something that the whole community needs to work on together,” Williams told TechCrunch.

The ETH is part of a broader class of problems known as P versus NP, which has animated computer science and many other fields for decades. Reductively, researchers are trying to understand if any problem whose solution can be easily verified (NP) can also be easily solved (P).

A simple example is the game Sodoku: Once a solution to a puzzle is in place, it can be quickly verified, but finding the solution is time-consuming. That dynamic is true across a range of important problems, and the insight underpins cryptographic systems: When you have the key to an encoded message, you can reveal it, but actually cracking an effective code takes too long to be useful.

However, scientists have yet to prove whether that is always true. “P = NP is saying that, not only can you verify Sudoku solutions when they exist, you can just generate them,” Williams said. “P = NP is asserting that this is true for every single problem you can verify quickly — if you can verify it quickly, you can solve it quickly. So this just seems too good to be true.”

That’s why most scientists assume that P ≠ NP. ETH provided evidence for that. It basically says that even the fastest computer algorithm needs exponentially more time to find a solution as the number of variables grow in a specific class of tricky logic puzzle.

The claims from OpenAI and Anthropic is that their models have come up with algorithms that solve those puzzles in “sub-exponential” time, meaning faster than the speeds previously thought possible. If true, they may have proven the EFT to be false — refuted it, in scientific terms — and maybe opened up a path for algorithms that generally perform P = NP.

“Every computer scientist should probably put a little more weight on the plausibility of P = NP, but one’s weight may vary,” Williams said. “I do think that it kind of calls into question a lot of the intuitions that we thought we had about … how tractable [hard NP problems] are. I also think that this is probably not the final answer either. I don’t think that OpenAI’s algorithm could possibly be the best algorithm you can get.”

The durability of ETH has lead to it being included in many scientific papers over the past decade that will need to be revisited if the AI labs claims that they’ve proven it wrong holds up, Williams said.

“This is partly why I was asking or pushing OpenAI to support a conference on this — it’s going to affect many researchers’ works, and we need to figure out, okay, what happens next besides understanding the algorithms. We need to understand, how should the foundations change compared to what we had before?” he said.Before scientific concerns, there are cybersecurity questions. Williams said that an improved algorithm could imply that cryptographic systems relying on certain key sizes could be considered broken. So one of the first questions he asked OpenAI science lead Dan Roberts “was if they had checked that this affects any crypto systems … is anything insecure now? And apparently, they checked, and they were reasonably confident that this didn’t … imply any breaks.”

This new AI paradigm of scientific discovery is still a work in progress, but Williams says he is optimistic despite real concerns, particularly about training the next generation of scientists. Being a computer scientist who does high level math has perhaps made the adjustment easier for him than for pure mathematicians.

Still, he says AI is “forcing people to be a little more ambitious about the kind of work that they do, and to think more deeply about, okay, if all the things I used to work on are now low-hanging fruit for AI, how can I raise the bar for the kind of scientific questions I want to ask?”
