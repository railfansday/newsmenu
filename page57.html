The contracts trade around the clock through Crypto.com’s App and Exchange as more platforms bring perps to the U.S. market.

Crypto exchange Crypto.com and its derivatives partner OG.com began rolling out crypto perpetual futures to "eligible" U.S. traders on Tuesday.

The contracts offer up to 5x leverage and let traders bet on rising or falling crypto prices with no expiration date. The offering comes with typical take-profit and stop-loss orders and isolated margin. The Tuesday release did not specify eligibility requirements.

Crypto.com said it recently registered as an introducing broker with the National Futures Association to be able to offer the perps. The products run through OG.com's "CFTC-regulated exchange and clearinghouse and futures commission merchant."

The launch follows the CFTC's May decision to open the U.S. markets to crypto perpetuals. The agency approved Kalshi's bitcoin perps and issued a no-action relief for Coinbase to offer digital commodity derivatives. Kraken then launched U.S. perps on Kraken Pro in June, following its acquisition of Bitnomial.

Crypto.com is also looking to push into stock derivatives. Nadex, the exchange operating under OG.com, registered with the SEC to trade security futures last month. At the time, CEO Kris Marszalek said the company was also working with the SEC and CFTC to offer single-stock perpetual futures.

Ondo Perps CEO David Wells told The Block in September that U.S. perpetuals are a "huge opportunity," though offshore platforms would need different clearing and settlement arrangements to meet U.S. rules.

#5569. A judge halted D.C. golf course renovations. Trump says they'll still start next month

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Trump, seen touring the East Potomac Golf Links in late June, says his administration will appeal the judge's ruling and close the course for renovations next month. Kent Nishimura/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

After weeks of tense hearings and late-night court filings, a federal judge has temporarily blocked the Trump administration from making any further alterations to East Potomac Park, the municipal D.C. golf course that the president wants to redevelop into a championship-level venue.

The long-simmering legal battle kicked into high gear in recent weeks, after the government cut down 430 trees on the golf course with less than a day's notice. That prompted a pair of lengthy court hearings that at times turned heated, with government officials repeatedly insisting that the tree-clearing was unrelated to the renovations President Trump had touted on social media. They contradicted the president's own social media posts from that very morning by saying there was no final plan for those renovations at all.

"Rarely, if ever, has the United States Department of Justice urged a federal court not to believe the sitting president," U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes wrote in her 70-page opinion. "And yet, here we are."

Reyes extended what was initially a two-week halt to tree removal, ordering the government not to cut down "or substantially prune or otherwise damage or impair" any trees at East Potomac Park without a court order. She also ordered it to provide "no less than ten business days' written advance notice" before closing or making any "irreversible modification" to any parts of the park while the legal battle plays out.

"By issuing this preliminary injunction, the Court does not end the round: it simply marks the ball until the parties can fully and fairly litigate this case," Reyes wrote.

Monday's ruling is a win for the D.C. Preservation League and a pair of local golfers who sued to prevent renovations earlier this year — and for the growing grassroots movement to protect the municipal course and the surrounding recreational area, which is beloved by runners, cyclists, birders and others.

"We are grateful the court intervened to stop the Trump-Vance administration's greed and lawlessness while the case continues, and we are eager to argue the case in court," said Skye Perryman, the president of the nonprofit Democracy Forward, which is representing the plaintiffs.

In a Truth Social post late Monday, Trump said the Department of the Interior will appeal the ruling. NPR has reached out to the Department of Interior but did not hear back in time for publication.

Trump also doubled down on his earlier announcement that construction will begin on Nov. 15 and keep the park closed for 15 months, adding that the "judge has absolutely no right to hold up this very important and beautiful Project."

"To have to give advanced notices to a judge who has no expertise in Golf, Architecture, Landscaping, Construction, or anything else having to do with building such a World Class Project, is not fair, appropriate, or Constitutional," Trump wrote.

Reyes herself has said she is not interested in policing the federal government's landscaping efforts.

That's why, as she explained in Monday's opinion, she repeatedly declined to intervene in the case — instead issuing repeated warnings against any unannounced major alterations — until "the Government did what it assured all it would not do."

"The Court earlier stated, 'I am not the Lorax. I do not speak for the trees,'" Reyes concluded. "The Court still does not speak for the trees. It speaks only for the rule of law."

On Tuesday afternoon, Reyes made a record of Trump's post and gave the government about 24 hours to clarify whether a plan exists to close East Potomac in mid-November and, if so, "how that can be reconciled" with what its lawyers previously told the court. She said she will schedule a status hearing for either Friday or Monday.

A golfer plays near some of the 430 trees that were cut down at East Potomac earlier this month. Saul Loeb/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Reyes' strongly-worded 70-page opinion came exactly a week after a seven-hour hearing in which she clashed repeatedly with the government's lawyers. She had already rejected the administration's request to recuse herself from the case because of alleged bias.

Last week's hearing featured sworn testimony from several officials from the Department of the Interior and National Park Service, to provide a fuller account of the origins and purpose of the controversial tree-clearing. But it teed up bigger questions about whether the administration had misled the court and the public over its intentions for the golf course.

"This started as a case about golf. That good walk spoiled. But it has become much more," Reyes wrote on Monday. "It now forces the Court to reconcile assertions by President Donald J. Trump with the sworn testimony of multiple government employees and the argument of senior Department of Justice attorneys."

Much of Reyes' opinion examines the gap between what Trump has said publicly and what his administration has told the court.

The most notable: Trump posted online the morning of last week's hearing that work would begin on the golf course "immediately." But hours later, in the courtroom, administration officials said in sworn testimony that they were not aware of any final plan.

Weaving together timelines and testimonies, Reyes ultimately agreed with plaintiffs that there does seem to be a golf course renovation plan (they refer to it as the "Washington National Plan") despite the administration's repeated denials.

"To be sure, the Court cannot pinpoint the exact date at which the Plan became final," Reyes wrote. "But it determines without hesitation that it occurred at some point well before October 15, 2026."

She doesn't accept the government's explanation that it cleared hundreds of healthy, non-invasive trees on such short notice to enact vegetation recommendations from a 2019 National Park Service Report (whose other recommendations, including for the city's other two municipal golf courses, went largely unaddressed in the last seven years). She called that justification "pretextual."

She took particular aim at the credibility of one witness, Gregory Wischer — a deputy chief of staff at the Interior Department — who was photographed carrying rolled-up concept plans while accompanying Trump and golf course architect Tom Fazio on a tour of East Potomac in June.

"Yet Wischer maintains that he — as Deputy Chief of Staff to the Secretary of the Interior — just spontaneously became interested in tree and vegetation management. In mid September. At the East Potomac Golf Course," Reyes wrote.

Reyes said the government "has made no compelling factual rebuttal." At one point, the administration accused the plaintiffs of conjecture "based on a handful of tweets." Reyes shut that down in Monday's opinion.

"The 'handful of tweets' are not complaints about the Red Sox's latest blown lead in the ninth. They are statements from the President of the United States, and from the Secretary of the Interior, about actions within their purview," she wrote. "And the posts say, in no uncertain terms, that a plan exists and that construction will start imminently — one even contains a final schematic of the Course's redesign."

East Potomac Park, seen from the observation deck of the Washington Monument in July. The manmade peninsula is home to a municipal golf course and recreational areas, including a 3-mile road around the perimeter. Kent Nishimura/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Trump said as recently as Monday night that he believes the renovations do not require approval. But, as Reyes pointed out in her opinion, administration officials acknowledged in a July hearing that major renovations would require federal environmental and historic preservation reviews.

Last week, however, administration lawyers presented an "alternative argument" that the renovations actually cannot be reviewed under the Administrative Procedure Act.

"It says, Emily Litella-style: 'Never mind,'" Reyes wrote, referring to the perpetually about-facing Saturday Night Live character. "But parties cannot change course whenever and however the evidence suits them … And so the Court does not credit this new argument."

Plaintiffs in the lawsuit argue that the government is purposefully approaching the golf course renovations as smaller projects — like dumping debris from the White House East Wing demolition last year or cutting down hundreds of trees this month — to avoid federally required environmental reviews.

That argument seems to hold water with Reyes, who said, the Interior Department likely violated the National Environmental Protection Act "by impermissibly segmenting its review of the Plan's environmental effects."

Monday's ruling — and the administration's promised appeal — could slow that process even more. Trump said on social media that work "must begin, immediately, if it is to be completed in a timely and seasonal manner."

But there are still many questions about the final product.

The rendering Trump shared earlier this month shows the park's three courses of varying ability levels combined into one that overtakes the entire peninsula, seemingly removing the perimeter road that runners and bikers use every day and raising questions about whether the historically affordable golf course could soon be less accessible.

Trump said Monday the redeveloped course will be able to host events like the PGA Championship and Ryder Cup, but will also be "open to the Public, at a reduced rate for those living in Washington, D.C."

The project is one of many ways he's looking to make his mark on the nation's capital, from building the White House ballroom to adding his name to the Kennedy Center to pushing through plans for a triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery.

#1174. Billy Dodds was a Dundee hero but is now viewed as a pariah among Dens Park fans... It's time that changed

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There are two chapters in Billy Dodds’ roller coaster story with Dundee. But there should really be three.

When the Dens Park club appointed Jim McIntyre as manager in October 2018 (ironically replacing Killie boss Neil McCann), former Dark Blues striker Dodds was in the building and set to be unveiled as his assistant.

That was until managing director John Nelms was given a brief but impactful history lesson about events on Tayside seven years earlier.

Dodds and former Dens skipper Gordon Chisholm, the management team who were brutally removed when the club entered administration for the second time in 2010, cast their votes against the creditors voluntary arrangement required to save the club from financial oblivion.

Fast forward to 2018 again, and Nelms was told that the Dundee supporters would not accept Dodds as part of McIntyre’s management team, given the perception he and Chisholm had voted to kill the club off years before, and Jimmy Boyle got the role instead.

Speaking to the Courier at the time, Nelms said: ‘I’m responding to the well-written emails I’m receiving which have true animosity.

‘I’m looking at this from a third party perspective and we weren’t here. I understand what happened at the time. It was a horrible situation for everyone – the fans felt shafted, the staff felt shafted, everyone felt shafted.

Yes, Dodds and Chisholm voted against the CVA back in 2011, earning them the pariah status they still have in Dark Blue circles. But they did so only AFTER receiving assurances that the CVA would succeed regardless of their actions.

Their votes were symbolic, another act of defiance in a season that would eventually be remembered for that very word. The acts of two men who were among the many victims of the club’s financial mismanagement.

The pair took over at Dundee in March 2010 charged with the task of ending the club’s damaging spell in the second tier.

Dodds himself spoke of building the club back up, reawakening the latent part of the Dark Blues support. Seven months later they had the rug pulled out from under their feet as administrators were called in over tax arrears.

As McLean put it: ‘By all means have a go at Doddsy for playing for United but he and Chisholm did NOT try to kill the club.

‘Chisholm and Dodds get a lot of criticism for their decision to vote against the CVA. They only voted against it after coming to see me and making sure it was going to be OK no matter how they voted. They came and made sure that Dundee would be OK before they voted against it because they didn’t want to put the club under, they wanted to make a protest about how they lost their jobs.

‘It’s one of these things that needs to be put straight before we are all too old to remember.’

Dodds is currently assistant manager to Neil McCann at Kilmarnock

‘How does that make me feel? It hurts, no doubt about it.

‘It’s always been about what’s right and wrong for me and that’s what gets me about this thing with the Dundee fans.

‘They’ve got it completely wrong. I can sit here and say that Chis and I did everything we could to help Dundee at that time. If people don’t accept that, then there isn’t much I can do. But I’ve got nothing to apologise for. The idea that I voted against the CVA for money is nonsense.’

Given McLean’s revelations, perhaps it’s time Dodds is recognised as he should be, as one of the club’s greatest players of the late twentieth century.

Dodds scored 77 goals in 195 appearances in dark blue between 1989 and 1994, not bad considering the club spent most of that time in the doldrums.

They were fairly traumatic times for the Taysiders as relegation followed a prolonged spell in the second tier. Absentee owner Ron Dixon was more interested in racing greyhounds around Dens Park than setting the club up for success on the pitch.

Dodds, though, can stake a claim to be one of the shining lights of the 1990s, alongside Morten Wieghorst, McCann and Darius Adamczuk.

The young striker was part of the team relegated under Gordon Wallace in 1990 but stuck around to help them return to the top flight in 1992. Everyone remembers Simon Stainrod’s fedora when Dundee beat Rangers 4-3 at Dens in 1992 but Dodds was the best player on the park that day amid a star-studded and all-conquering Rangers team.

In a career that saw him score goals by the bucketload at neighbours Dundee United, St Johnstone, Aberdeen and Rangers, Dundee is the club he played most games for. Dodds should be lauded at Dens, paraded on to the pitch to do the half-time draw, holding court in the hospitality lounges.

Instead, when he and Kilmarnock manager McCann take their seats in the Dens Park dugouts for tonight’s match, Dodds will likely face boos and catcalls.

McCann himself is a bona fide legend at Dens, making his name as a fleetfooted winger before returning as a ‘trialist’ in 2011 when the club successfully fought a 25-point deduction.

Anyone who witnessed his stoppage-time winner against Raith Rovers, at the age of 36, will take that golden memory to their grave. His managerial reign at Dundee may have turned sour but not before he masterminded a Red Adair rescue act to save them from relegation in 2017.

Dodds deserves a bit of that adulation and McLean’s revelations should be a game changer on that front. It’s time he was re-evaluated through a dark blue lens: as a goalscoring talisman who carried the flame through dark times.

#4551. Raw Denim vs. Regular Denim: Which Is Actually Worth Buying?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Here’s what to know about both kinds of jeans.

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

I COME FROM the denim era where streetwear juggernauts like FUBU and Rocawear reigned supreme, along with mall favorites like Abercrombie & Fitch and Hollister. I was in my preteens rocking all these clothing brands and you couldn’t tell me their baggy-stacking-at-the-shoe jeans weren’t the flyest on the market. Since I began working as a fashion editor, I’ve practically worn every pair of jeans in the denim universe. Slim, relaxed, stretch, washed, distressed—you name it, I’ve probably tried it. I’ve even tried my hand at denim-making in Japan, the mecca of old-school, high-quality denim, where I produced my own pair of selvedge jeans. Denim is one of those rare items in a guy’s wardrobe that never goes out of style.

And after years of being in the menswear space, I’ve gotten familiar with the nuances that distinguish one kind of denim from the next. Two worth knowing are regular denim and raw denim. Raw denim usually looks darker, stiffer, and more uniform in color, while regular denim has a softer feel and a more broken-in appearance with visible fading or whiskering from factory washing and finishing. Those differences also affect how each pair feels when you first put it on and how it changes over the years. Here’s what to know about both and which one deserves a spot in your rotation.

Regular jeans are usually made by weaving cotton yarn into denim, then cutting fabric into patterns and sewing them together to create the finished garment. These jeans have already gone through washing or finishing treatments to soften the fabric and give them that broken-in look, so you don’t have to spend weeks waiting for them to feel comfortable. If I want to throw on a pair of jeans and get on with my day, this is the kind I’m reaching for. I also like that you can find them in practically every fit and wash, whether you’re after a clean, dark indigo pair or something faded and lived-in. Most everyday options from brands like Levi’s and Wrangler run roughly $40 to $100, while designer and premium styles can cost considerably more. For me, prewashed denim makes the most sense when you want that comfortable feel from the jump. There’s nothing wrong with skipping the break-in period and getting straight to the good part.

I like raw denim because you get to see how jeans change the more you wear them. Unlike prewashed jeans, raw denim typically hasn’t been washed after the dyeing and manufacturing process, so it typically arrives stiff, dark, and pretty rigid. One thing to watch out for, though, is dye transfer. Indigo can rub off onto lighter-colored clothes, shoes, and other materials, including suede. I’ve had this happen before, so I'd be mindful of what you wear with a fresh pair. But that’s part of the appeal for denim enthusiasts. As you wear it, the fabric softens and develops fades and creases based on how you move, sit, and live in your jeans. You’re essentially breaking them in yourself, which makes the finished look feel uniquely yours. Prices can start around $60 to $100 for entry-level options and climb to $150 to $300 or more for premium denim, particularly Japanese-made styles. I’ll admit, the break-in period takes some patience, but if you enjoy watching your jeans evolve over time, raw denim is worth considering.

Raw denim and selvedge denim get lumped together all the time, but they actually refer to different things. Raw denim describes the fabric’s finishing state: It’s typically sold without a post-production wash that softens the fabric or creates a faded look. Selvedge, on the other hand, refers to how the denim is woven, creating a clean, self-finished edge you can often spot by rolling up the cuff. You can have raw selvedge denim, washed selvedge denim, or raw denim that isn’t selvedge at all. Having made my own pair of selvedge jeans in Japan, I’ve seen firsthand how the construction differs. Once you know what to look for, the distinction is pretty easy to spot.

At the end of the day, I don’t think you need to drop $300 on raw denim to dress well. After years of working in menswear, I’ve learned that a higher price tag doesn’t automatically make something the right choice for your wardrobe. I’ve seen guys look just as good in an affordable pair of Levi’s as they do in premium Japanese denim. What matters more is finding comfortable jeans that fit your body and work with the clothes you already own. I’m always going to appreciate the quality craftsmanship behind a great pair of raw denim, but there’s no single right way to wear jeans. I think every guy should have one dependable pair, whether that’s raw or prewashed. But ultimately, like most things in style, it comes down to personal preference—and knowing what works for you.
