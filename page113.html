Engines seem to be under the microscope lately, and Ford’s Godzilla V8 is the latest to get the spotlight. This 7.3-liter beast, usually found powering heavy-duty trucks and commercial haulers, is now being investigated by the NHTSA for alleged camshaft and lifter failures.

According to the NHTSA’s document, the Office of Defects Investigation cracked open a defect petition (DP26011, for those keeping score). The original complaint landed back in June, asking the agency to dig into possible engine gremlins lurking in several Ford models.

The probe covers about 54,787 vehicles, including the 2021 and 2022 Ford E-350, plus the 2021 F-59 and F-750. All of them pack the 7.3-liter gasoline V8 – better known as the Godzilla engine.

The complaint claims that when oil pressure drops to around 6 psi at low idle, the lifter rollers and camshaft lobes start to eat themselves alive. Let things go too far, and the engine could grenade itself without warning – even at highway speeds.

The petition goes further, saying Ford knew about the problem and quietly pushed out a software update to bump idle speed to about 1,000 rpm. But, according to the complaint, there was no recall or heads-up for owners.

However, it’s important to note that the agency’s first summary only mentions a single reported incident. No crashes, no fires, no injuries, no fatalities. So, that big number of vehicles? It doesn’t mean all of them have actually failed.

Right now, the NHTSA is just looking into the petition. That doesn’t mean they’ve decided the engine is actually faulty. The agency will dig into the details and then choose whether to move forward or shut things down.

It’s an important difference: just because there’s an investigation doesn’t mean Ford has to recall anything yet. Regulators still have to weigh the claims and evidence before making any big moves.

The summary doesn’t say how many vehicles have actually had this failure, or even if the oil issue is really to blame. What happens next depends on what the agency finds as it digs deeper.

#185. NASA’s Oldest Martian Spacecraft Have Been Running for Decades. Here’s How

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NASA’s Mars Odyssey spacecraft has been operating for so long, it can rent a car without any fees. The spacecraft arrived at the Red Planet on October 24, 2001. It, along with its 20-year-old sibling, the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO), have operated longer than anyone watching their launches could have dreamed possible.

Having such a long record of observations from above the Martian surface has rewritten the story of water on the planet, past and present alike. Keeping the wizened orbiters going for so long has been possible only thanks to great engineering and constant problem solving—and a little luck. But scientists wonder whether, despite their storied legacies, Odyssey and MRO will be the last of their kind, at least for the Red Planet.

“Both Odyssey and MRO are some of the most productive spacecraft that have ever existed,” says Briony Horgan, a planetary scientist at Purdue University who isn’t affiliated with either mission, calling them “critical” to NASA’s overall Mars research program. Odyssey’s observations of the planet’s ice, for example, and MRO’s ability to see surface features change over time have each given scientists unprecedented insight into Mars as a vibrant planet rather than a lump of rock. Odyssey and MRO also connect humans to every NASA rover, which can’t communicate with Earth directly. It’s an impressive legacy for a pair of orbiters that were only meant to last just a few years.

Space is a notoriously harsh environment that takes luck and resilience to survive for decades. Particularly tricky is the barrage of high-energy particles hitting machinery; a powerful enough strike to a delicate enough apparatus can be fatal. And whereas engineers can keep constant tabs on satellites orbiting Earth, bandwidth to Mars is more limited—science spacecraft might only check in once or twice a week, says Rob Lillis, a planetary and space physicist at the University of California, Berkeley, who has worked on a range of more recent Mars orbiters.

“A Mars craft has to be able to manage by itself,” he says.

A concept image of the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.

Losing an instrument is bad, but losing the power or computing systems can brick the whole craft. NASA often doubles up on vital parts of a spacecraft to ensure that if something important fails, the mission can run off the spare until engineers can troubleshoot a problem.

In the MRO’s case, one challenge it’s faced is ironically due to its staying power. The orbiter determines its precise location and orientation by comparing what its cameras see with a stellar atlas onboard—with just one hiccup. “MRO is actually old enough that some of those stars have moved across the night sky—very slightly, but they have moved,” says Paul Fieseler, an engineer at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory and chief engineer for MRO. The tiny misalignments confused the spacecraft, and last year, engineers had to send up an updated map to keep MRO oriented into the future.

Odyssey’s greatest risk right now is simply running out of propellant it uses to make tiny adjustments to maintain its orientation in space. The spacecraft doesn’t have a fuel gauge–without gravity to push the fuel down, there’s no volume to measure–so NASA can’t be sure how much juice it still has. Engineers have improvised new ways to fully operate the spacecraft on just a few drops at a time in a bid to extend its life.

Experts say both spacecraft benefited from failures during NASA’s infamous “faster, better, cheaper” era. In 1999, NASA lost two missions arriving at Mars: First the Mars Climate Orbiter burned up in the atmosphere, and then, just months later, the Mars Polar Lander botched its arrival and slammed into the surface. Each catastrophe, investigations determined, was caused by what might in more pedestrian realms be dubbed careless mistakes. The first was due to miscommunication between a ground system relying on imperial units and the flight system using metric, while the second was due to a software error not caught during testing.

“Those failures were very much on the mind of the team pulling together the Odyssey spacecraft,” says Glen Havens, an engineer at JPL and project and mission manager of Mars Odyssey. With those losses stinging, NASA invested more in perfecting the Odyssey and MRO spacecraft before launch, designing each to be as resilient as possible and testing every tiny component and every system configuration.

Topographic imagery from Mars captured by the MRO.

One key principle is what NASA calls “margin”—the spacecraft equivalent of carrying a snack, just in case.

“Unexpected things might happen, and you want to make sure the spacecraft will survive,” says Nilton Renno, a spaceflight engineer at the University of Michigan who hasn’t worked on either mission. MRO launched with a larger battery and more propellant than it strictly needed, Fieseler notes, and that margin is now keeping the mission going, with NASA hoping to operate it for another decade.

But orbiting Mars, it’s wise not to take anything for granted. Just last year, NASA lost the 11-year-old Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution—its youngest Red Planet orbiter—after the spacecraft unexpectedly lost power on the far side of the planet. As Odyssey and MRO continue to age, questions linger about what Mars orbiters come next for NASA.

The agency has hired Blue Origin to build a dedicated communications satellite to beam scads of data back to Earth for at least five years—but unlike its forebears, which began as science missions before turning to communications work, this orbiter will only relay signals for other spacecraft.

NASA’s Mars science agenda includes only relatively limited projects. One mission, which Lillis leads, features two orbiters currently on their way to study how the planet responds to the constant stream of charged particles flowing off the sun. But even he calls the mission “right on the edge of being too small to do good science.” Then, there’s Skyfall, the trio of small helicopters meant to launch aboard the first nuclear-propelled spacecraft within just two years—a shockingly rapid timeline, and for a mission with minimal ability to answer meaty research questions.

To scientists, none of these initiatives quite build on the legacy of Odyssey and MRO and may even raise the specter of the infamous Mars failures that came before. “Certainly they’re pushing for faster, cheaper,” Horgan says. “It’s going to be a lot more challenging to do good science in that environment.”

#1815. Ballon d'Or winners by club: Real Madrid, Barcelona, Man United

By Matt Law | 27 Oct 2026 10:41, Last updated: 27 Oct 2026 10:44

Harry Kane secured the 2026 Ballon d'Or on Monday evening in London, with the Bayern Munich and England striker recognised for his excellent 2025-26 season.

The 33-year-old held off competition from Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal to claim the prize, and in doing so, he became the first English player to win the award since Michael Owen (2001).

Kane has joined an exclusive list of players to have won the Ballon d'Or.

Here, Sports Mole looks at the Ballon d'Or winners by club, analysing the most successful teams when it comes to the main prize.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, Real Madrid and Barcelona are at the top of the list with 12 each when it comes to the Ballon d'Or winners by club.

Real Madrid have had eight players to win the main prize, while Barcelona have had six, with the legendary duo of Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi dominating the Ballon d'Or.

Messi has incredibly won the accolade on eight occasions - six while at Barcelona - while Ronaldo is second with five, with four of those successes coming at Real Madrid.

Alfredo Di Stefano won the Ballon d'Or twice while at Real Madrid in 1957 and 1959, while Raymond Kopa (1958), Luis Figo (2000), Ronaldo (2002), Fabio Canavarro (2006), Luka Modric (2018) and Karim Benzema (2022) are added to Cristiano Ronaldo's four to take Los Blancos to 12 when it comes to successes.

As for Barcelona, Luis Suarez was the first player to win the award while turning out for the Catalan side back in 1960, while Johan Cruyff won it twice (1973, 1974).

There was then a 20-year wait for a Barcelona player to win it again, with Hristo Stoichkov securing the main prize in 1994.

Rivaldo (1999) and Ronaldinho (2005) are added to Messi's six at Barcelona to take the La Liga giants onto 12 wins in the Ballon d'Or.

Juventus and AC Milan are then joint-second in the list with eight wins from six players, while Bayern Munich are next with six triumphs, including the success for Kane on Monday night.

Manchester United are next with four triumphs; Denis Law was the first of those to secure the trophy while with the Red Devils back in 1964.

Bobby Charlton claimed the prize in 1966, George Best in 1968, then Cristiano Ronaldo in 2008 following an extraordinary 2007-08 campaign for the Manchester giants.

In today’s episode of “goodness, geopolitics is complicated,” airlines from Taiwan have officially once again started using Russian airspace, nearly five years after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. As you’d expect, there’s a rather complicated reason for this.

In February 2022, we saw Russia invade Ukraine. Obviously this has had far-reaching implications that go way beyond aviation, including an unconscionable death toll. But as it impacts aviation, perhaps the biggest implication has been that airlines from many countries have stopped using Russian airspace.

Airlines from some countries were banned, due to reciprocal measures put in place by Russia. Meanwhile airlines from other countries voluntarily stopped using Russian airspace, on safety and insurance grounds.

While airlines from Taiwan have never formally been banned from Russian airspace, Taiwan’s Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) did voluntarily put a Russian airspace ban in place, preventing airlines from Taiwan from using Russian airspace. That appears to have been lifted as of the start of the IATA winter season, which kicked off on October 25, 2026.

As OMAAT member atzkowski had pointed out, on October 24, 2026, China Airlines flight CI75 from Taipei (TPE) to Rome (FCO) operated almost the entire way over Russia (the flight took off late at night on October 24, so it was October 25 by the time it was using Russian airspace).

As a point of comparison, previously this flight would typically fly much further south, over India, Pakistan, Turkmenistan, etc.

Why would airlines from Taiwan suddenly start using Russian airspace? Some media sources in Taiwan suggest it’s because an overall risk assessment has been performed, and the decision was made that this was no longer necessary.

Particularly in winter, headwinds going west can be really strong when taking a more southerly routing, so there’s a bigger advantage to flying over Russia, as it could potentially shave an hour or so off the total flight time.

However, Enilria suggests that as of October 25, 2026, Pakistan has banned airlines from Taiwan from its airspace. While I haven’t been able to find an official notice of this, that explanation does seem to check out.

For example, take China Airlines flight CI81 from Taipei to London (LHR). Prior to October 25, the route consistently operated over Pakistani airspace. Keep in mind airlines from Taiwan can’t use mainland China airspace for overflight, given the politics at play.

However, if airlines from Taiwan are actually banned from Pakistani airspace (which seems to be the case), their only option is to either fly north of China or south of Pakistan, given that the two countries border. So flights to Europe that haven’t been operating using Russian airspace have instead started flying over the Middle East, just a few miles from the Strait of Hormuz.

So, why would Pakistan ban Taiwanese airlines from its airspace? China and Pakistan have very close relations, so the idea is that China pressured Pakistan into this policy. That would hardly be the first time that China has tried to pull something like that.

One thing to keep in mind is that airlines from Taiwan will likely limit their use of Russian airspace. For example, the United States doesn’t let US carriers codeshare on flights that use Russian airspace, so expect most US routes of Taiwanese carriers still won’t use that airspace.

I’m curious to see how this all evolves. It’s not easy to be an airline from Taiwan, if you can’t use the airspace of China or Pakistan.

Airlines from Taiwan have gradually started resuming the use of Russian airspace, nearly five years after they voluntarily stopped using it. It would appear that what’s going on here is that Pakistan has banned Taiwanese airlines from overflying the country, due to pressure from China.

#5934. Over 75% Of European Pilots Surveyed Say They Fell Asleep In The Cockpit, And Many Fear Speaking Up

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More than 3/4 European pilots responding to a new survey reported experiencing at least one “microsleep” in the cockpit during the preceding four weeks. That is alarming enough, but the concerns about reporting fatigue and refusing longer duties may even more troubling.

A new report by aviation consultancy Baines Simmons, commissioned by the European Cockpit Association (ECA), examines responses from 7,198 pilots across 32 countries. The survey was conducted in August 2026 and asked pilots about their experiences with fatigue, work schedules, and reporting systems.

A microsleep is a brief, involuntary episode of sleep. This is different from a planned rest period under an approved procedure. Of course the survey does not establish that both pilots were asleep simultaneously or that an aircraft was left without an alert pilot, like this case on ITA I wrote about.

We also need to describe the sample accurately. These are the experiences reported by the pilots who answered the survey, not proof that three-quarters of every European airline’s pilots are falling asleep at work. Nevertheless, nearly 7,200 responses warrant considerably more than a shrug.

> Read More: Both ITA Airways Pilots Fall Asleep On New York – Rome A330 Flight

The ECA’s summary highlights another concern: 24.2% of respondents said they usually do not report themselves unfit when fatigued and fly anyway.

Then there is “Commander’s Discretion,” which permits flight-duty extensions in exceptional circumstances. Some 38.3% reported using it at least once during the previous four weeks, while 69.1% expressed concern about negative consequences if they refused an extension.

ECA board director Captain Coen George put it this way:

“When pilots fear career consequences, fatigue goes unreported and the risk returns to the cockpit.”

That strikes me as the heart of the issue and potentially a much bigger problem than I imagined.

I want pilots to exercise good judgment, including recognizing when they should not fly. But an airline cannot reasonably expect honest reporting if employees believe that exercising that judgment will damage their careers. A reporting system can look excellent in a presentation and accomplish very little if the people expected to use it do not trust it…

I hope this was an honest service, but even a low number of fatigue reports would not necessarily reassure me. If pilots are reluctant to submit them, management may be measuring silence rather than safety.

I understand the operational consequences. A pilot declaring himself unfit can mean finding a replacement, delaying a flight, or cancelling it. Passengers miss connections, crews are displaced, and the airline incurs costs. Those are real problems, but problems an airline needs to be prepared to handle. The alternative cannot be relying on someone who believes he is too tired to operate safely.

I complain about delays frequently, particularly rolling delays that leave passengers unable to make sensible decisions about their day. In fact, I have a prime example I’ll share tomorrow that directly highlights this tension.

But as much as I hate delays and cancellations, I would never want a pilot to continue simply because passengers are impatient or management wants the aircraft moving. Pilots must use their rest opportunities responsibly and be honest about their fitness for duty. Airlines must build schedules that allow adequate recovery, which is a statutory requirement, but also make it possible to report a problem without intimidation.

A duty being legal should be the starting point for assessing it, rather than the end of the discussion.

One more thing. I would not use this survey to declare a particular airline (or flying in general) unsafe. It is based on self-reported experiences and was commissioned by an organization representing pilots. That context belongs in the story. It does not make the findings irrelevant, but neither does the report establish the circumstances of every episode or allow us to predict an accident.

What I take from it is a serious warning about the gap between fatigue policies and how pilots experience them in practice. I’d like to think that the minimum rest periods already enshrined in law are enough, but I’d welcome commentary from pilots as to whether you actually do feel well-rested before work, especially if you use your off-duty period as intended.

A survey of nearly 7,200 European pilots found widespread reports of cockpit microsleeps, inadequate recovery, and concern about the consequences of refusing extended duties.

The headline number is striking, but the lack of trust in fatigue reporting deserves just as much attention.

I want my flight to depart on time and I want the pilots to be fit to operate it considerably more. An airline that takes safety seriously should make it easy for a tired pilot to say so…and have a plan for what happens next.

#256. GM will switch to Nasdaq from the NYSE on Nov. 17

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DETROIT — General Motors plans to transfer shares of its common stock from the New York Stock Exchange to the Nasdaq as the automaker attempts to position itself more as a technology-focused company.

The Detroit automaker said Tuesday that it expects to begin trading as a Nasdaq-listed company on Nov. 17. Its ticker symbol is expected to remain "GM," the company said.

"GM's customer-focused, technology-driven strategy is creating value for our stockholders, making Nasdaq a natural home for our listing as we grow," GM CFO Paul Jacobson said in a release. "We're grateful for our long-standing partnership with the New York Stock Exchange and its remarkable legacy."

GM noted the move will provide eligibility for potential inclusion in the Nasdaq-100 Index.

Current shareholders do not need to take any action with respect to the transfer, the company said.

The switch marks a notable loss for the NYSE, as GM first listed on the exchange in 1911. The Detroit automaker also relisted on the stock exchange in 2010 following a government-backed bankruptcy.

GM follows other companies such as Walmart, DoorDash and Palantir Technologies that have transferred from the more traditional NYSE to the historically technology focused Nasdaq in recent years. Hilton Worldwide Holdings last week also announced plans to move trading from the NYSE to Nasdaq.

The key drivers for a listing transfer differ from company to company, but the Nasdaq in 2024 said common reasons include "index inclusion opportunities, cost effective fee structure, brand visibility partnership, access to Nasdaq's event space, and Nasdaq's suite of investor relations and ESG products."

GM's decision comes a week after the automaker held a technology-focused event where executives laid out the company's roadmap for artificial intelligence and self-driving vehicle technologies.

Shares of GM, which has been aggressively buying back stock, have more than doubled since 2024. GM's stock closed Monday at $76.31 per share. The company's market cap is roughly $64.4 billion.
