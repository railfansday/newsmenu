Samsung’s HW-Q600F soundbar is down to $389.99 at Amazon from $499.99, a $110 discount that brings it to its lowest price ever, according to price trackers. This 2025 model sits in the middle of Samsung’s Q-Series lineup and pairs a sleek, angular bar with a subwoofer in a 3.1.2 setup, where the bar’s nine drivers cover the left, center, and right channels as well as two up-firing height channels. Those up-firing drivers let it handle Dolby Atmos and other object-based audio correctly, an advantage over Samsung’s 5.1-channel HW-B750F, though it gives up the extra audio channels, app support, and broader connectivity of the pricier HW-Q800F.

For everyday listening, the HW-Q600F performs particularly well, with balanced tuning across the stereo and center channels that keeps dialogue clear without requiring much adjustment. It also preserves the dynamics of a soundtrack, so an explosion still lands with more force than the conversation before it. Dolby Atmos also brings a modest sense of height, and effects track cleanly from left to right. Surround sound, on the other hand, is less convincing, since the soundbar doesn’t include rear satellite speakers and downmixes surround formats, which limits its ability to convincingly place effects behind you. You do get a graphic equalizer and sound presets for adjusting the audio to your preferences, though without a companion app, you’ll be making those changes through the included remote.

On the connectivity side, the bar includes an HDMI input, so you can plug a game console into it and pass video through to your TV, while an optical port covers older devices. That said, HDMI audio latency runs slightly high, potentially requiring an adjustment in your TV's settings to keep dialogue synchronized with what's happening on screen. As for wireless connectivity, it is limited to Bluetooth, meaning no wifi streaming or multi-room audio. Also, Samsung's SpaceFit Sound Pro room correction and Q-Symphony integration, which lets the TV’s speakers play alongside the bar, only work with compatible Samsung sets.

Gemini 3.1 Pro launched back in February of this year—quite a while ago, in AI terms. Now Google has jumped to the next full number with Gemini 4 Argon, which it says "excels at deep work in complex, real-world tasks."

You can get the new AI model now inside the Gemini app if you're on the AI Pro ($19.99 a month) or AI Ultra ($99.99 or $199.99 a month) plans. Users on the free and AI Plus ($4.99 a month) plans miss out for now, though you can expect a smaller, cut-down version of Gemini 4 Argon to roll out to these tiers eventually.

Google has a long list of areas where the new AI model excels. There's mention of complex engineering tasks (like checking millions of lines of code for efficiency improvements), predicting molecular structures in chemistry, and delivering 3D visualizations. It also enhances knowledge work across legal, finance, science, and coding.

Google says Gemini 4 Argon is now better at advanced physics modeling, picking through genetic databases, protecting software from vulnerabilities, and resolving problems in physical space (handy for robotics development). The new model "answers questions more directly with less hedging, doesn't repeat itself more than necessary, and skips clichés throughout the responses," according to Google.

With many in the industry worried about an AI-driven apocalypse of some form or another, Google has upped its defenses against misuse and jailbreaking. The company has significantly upgraded its blockers for harmful requests, limits on AI agent overreach, and resistance to malicious prompt injections.

All of these stats are things you would expect to read about in a new AI model announcement, whether it's from Google, Anthropic, or OpenAI: "Our new model is better and safer than ever, and particularly good in these specific benchmarks we've chosen." But is it any more helpful for the average user tapping away in the Gemini app?

I don't have any pressing scientific breakthroughs to research or large-scale software optimizations to run, but I have put Gemini 4 Argon to the test against GPT-6 Sol, which is the latest model available to consumers through the ChatGPT app—though you need to be on at least the Plus plan ($19.99 a month) to get to it.

Make no mistake: Both of these new AI models are quite capable. I first prompted Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-6 Sol with the following: "Using information online, create a web app I can run locally that plots the lighthouses of the world on a map. Clicking on a lighthouse should give me some details about it, show how far its light reaches, and display a simple 2D model showing its shape and height (if known). Put a lighthouse keeper in each model pop-up for scale."

At the highest, slowest levels of "thinking," these AIs took a while to get back to me: Nearly 10 minutes for Gemini and almost 26 minutes for ChatGPT. Both hit the same problem—restricted access to map data online—but both were able to make a rudimentary world map of their own when prompted a second time.

The ChatGPT effort, dubbed "Beacon," wasn't bad, apart from a bit of map clunkiness (it clearly needs to work on its cartography skills). It presented plenty of information, laid out everything well, and gave me nice, neat diagrams on each of the lighthouses featured. The day/night mode worked fine, and it was an impressive bit of coding work, even if it did take 26 minutes.

However, the Gemini effort, called "Pharos," was better in almost every respect. The map was more detailed, lighthouses actually showed their light range (with an impressive animation), and there was more information on each location—perhaps due to Google's expertise in web search. Some of the layout choices were a bit off, but Gemini 4 Argon produced what was clearly the superior app in less than half the time.

Next, I fed PDFs of 10 recent scientific studies into each AI model. The prompt: "Give me a brief summary of each of these studies in plain English, with a single sentence each for the main finding, one study strength, one study weakness, and the main implication." While I hadn't read the studies through entirely myself, I had read news stories on all of them, and I lowered the "thinking" level to low.

This time ChatGPT was faster—25 seconds versus Gemini's 55 seconds—and gave what I thought were the better summaries, at least for my purposes. Gemini did well, and its answers were accurate and to the point, but the summaries ChatGPT gave were easier to understand and shorter. ChatGPT also offered some overall thoughts on the most promising study, which Gemini didn't.

Finally, I checked the imagination and creative juices of the models: "With the success of the Apple TV shows Severance, Slow Horses, Silo, and Widow's Bay, give me five pitches for new show ideas on the platform that combine elements from some or all of these shows. Try and be as original as possible and keep each pitch to around 100 words." I put the "thinking" level back up to "high" for this job.

(Is this how Apple TV executives actually get their ideas nowadays? I hope not.) As far as the answers the AIs gave me, Gemini was faster than ChatGPT (1 minute and 28 seconds against 3 minutes and 48 seconds, respectively), and both served up responses that were a bit more literal in their mashing up than I wanted: failed secret service agents and supernatural terrors abounded, which is I suppose what I asked for.

The ideas were mostly "Widow's Bay set somewhere else" or "Slow Horses set somewhere else," and didn't make a whole lot of sense for the most part, but the best Gemini offering was about a Silo-style underground bunker whose operators go through a Severance-style procedure every 24 hours: outside, the bunker protecting it from zombies by night; inside, the bunker planning social activities for residents by day. It's not perfect, but I might watch the trailer at least.

My favorite of the ChatGPT suggestions was a show about a remote, Severance-style weather station whose employees discover they're not recording the weather—they're creating it. Again, it didn't really logically hold up, but overall I found the suggestions from GPT-6 more nuanced, more likely to be an actual show, and better "thought" through. Maybe Gemini should've given itself more time to "think."

This is only a narrow set of quick tests, but, in my view, it seems Gemini is back to, at the very least, an even playing field with ChatGPT. It performed much better on the lighthouse task, though its summarizing and TV show pitching weren't quite as impressive. Access to the best models requires a subscription on both platforms, but if you're able to, it's worth comparing them against each other on the jobs you most commonly need AI for.

YouTube TV is one of several streaming services for live TV—and a particularly good pick for sports fans who want to be able to watch and record every matchup from their favorite leagues. If YouTube TV is your platform of choice, here are the tips and tricks to optimize your experience.

If you and members of your household have wildly different streaming tastes, you can create unique profiles for each person to prevent recommendations from one from influencing another and to separate DVR libraries. A single subscription can be shared with five additional family members, who technically must reside in the same household, and those who are outside of the home need to check in once every three months (more on that below) to maintain access. You're also limited to three active streams at a time, so six people can't be watching on six separate devices at once.

If you travel frequently or are part of family sharing based anywhere besides your home, make sure you launch YouTube TV whenever you are in the account's home area. YouTube TV requires all users to check in every 90 days to avoid losing access when away from your home area. (For baseball fans, MLB imposes viewing restrictions after just 30 days.) If you get in the habit of resetting your 90-day window, you're less likely to find yourself locked out when you're on the road.

YouTube TV has pre-built multiviews that show you three or four channels at once—these are often either several games across a single sport or a combination of sports channels. Earlier this year, the platform launched customizable multiview, which allows you to select up to four channels across content categories. This means you can choose the specific games you want or mix and match sports with news, TV shows, and movies so that everyone in your household can be entertained. From any livestream, press the down button and select Multiview > Your multiview to choose up to four channels.

YouTube TV is heavy on features benefiting sports fans. If you can't begin a broadcast when a game starts, the platform offers you several options when you launch the stream: Start from the beginning, join live, or catch up to live through key plays. The latter shows you only the highlights of the action then drops you into the live stream, so you don't miss anything important but don't have to watch or fast-forward through the entire game to see what's happening now. You can also find the key plays view via Scores & stats at the bottom of the screen. This feature is supported for events from the NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL, college football, and college basketball.

Another way to avoid spoilers for live sports is to hide scores, which prevents final scores or live previews from launching before you start watching. For NFL, NBA, and MLB games, you can hide scores on a team-by-team or league-wide basis. If a league doesn't have pages for individual teams, you can hide scores for the whole league. On the team or league page, go to More and turn on Hide all scores for this team/league.

If you aren't sure what you want to watch on YouTube TV and none of the recommendations do it for you, you can use the platform's Live Guide just like the channel guide from traditional cable. However, this version is customizable, so you don't have to scroll through dozens of channels you never watch to reach the ones you do. On the web browser or app, go to Settings > Live Guide to reorder, favorite, or hide channels from your list. This can't be done directly from a streaming box, but when you open the live guide on your TV, select the Custom filter view to see your updates.

Your watch history informs recommendations on YouTube and YouTube TV, which is useful for discovering new content but also means that your algorithm can be influenced by anything and everything you watch. If you don't want certain content affecting what you're served—let's say you're binge-watching a show you wouldn't normally or you have someone staying at your house and using your account—you can pause your watch history temporarily. Tap your profile photo, go to Settings > Privacy, and toggle on Pause watch history.

YouTube TV has unlimited DVR, but that doesn't mean you need to fill your storage with content you don't plan to watch. You can record everything from an entire sport or league, or you can select only the specific teams or franchises you care about. This depends on how you set up recording: You can search for the league and tap Add + or the "+" icon to get every game, or search for your favorite team or individual matchup. DVR playback has similar options to live events you start on delay, including resuming where you left off and watching key plays.

Some content you record from the live feed may also be available as video on demand (VOD). With the former, you can fast-forward through ads; with the latter, ads can't be skipped. However, you may be able to choose your version if you used DVR. Click down on the stream to see whether you're on VOD or DVR, then go back to the show page and select Choose a Version > DVR (if available).

If you have technical issues with YouTube TV, the built-in Stats for Nerds feature can help you diagnose the problem (and whether it's coming from your network or YouTube TV itself). For example, if your resolution drops significantly, connection speed, buffer health, and dropped frames can give you a clue as to whether your wifi is the culprit. Access Stats for Nerds on your TV via player controls > More > Bug icon. On your computer, right-click on the video player; on mobile, tap More > Stats from the video player.

#4372. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026) Preview - A Beautiful Step Back In Time

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

For nearly 20 years, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time was my favorite game. While it was eventually supplanted by The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, Ocarina of Time remains a formative, treasured gaming experience that laid the foundation for the wider 3D action genre. However, I am the first to admit that while the original Nintendo 64 version – the only one available on Switch and Switch 2 – has aged better than most games of its era, much like its iconic hero, it doesn't take long to realize it's from a bygone era.

For years, I wanted Nintendo to bring the 2011 3D remaster to Switch, but it's doing me one better: a full, from-the-ground-up remake with lush visuals, several quality-of-life improvements, and even voice-acted cutscenes.  We are just weeks away from the launch of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time on Nintendo Switch 2, and ahead of its November 5 release, I spent over an hour exploring Hyrule as both Young and Adult Link.

My time begins the moment I take control of Link after Navi wakes him up. Following a brief conversation with Navi and Saria, who also ran up to Link's treehouse, it's time to retrieve the Deku Shield and Kokiri Sword so Mido will let me past to see the Great Deku Tree. As increasingly endearing as Saria is thanks to her improved facial and body animations, as well as her more fleshed-out dialogue, Mido is just as increasingly obnoxious; I was definitely happy to make him eat his words after he mocked Link for previously being the only one without a fairy.

Running around Kokiri Forest is nearly enough to sell me on this remake. The visuals are absolutely stunning. The new jump button and ability to sprint out of a roll make it feel like an all-new game. The freely controlled camera adds to this feeling, even with some relatively basic level design. And while I never had a problem with how Young Link looks in this remake, I'm even more impressed and comforted by seeing him in action in the actual game. In fact, everything surrounding Link also looks great; Kokiri Forest is much more bustling than its previous forms, light fog dissipates as you run through it, and swinging your sword looks better than ever before. Though I love Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom's stylish anime-inspired art style, the sheer amount of detail present in each and every character, enemy, and environment accentuates the masterfully executed maximalist approach Nintendo took with this remake.

After finding 40 rupees to buy a Deku Shield, I crawl through the tunnel to the area where you get the Kokiri Sword. This time, however, there's added verticality as you dodge the rolling boulders to reach a ladder, which you climb and then jump across a gap to reach the chest that contains the blade. This is indicative of small changes dedicated fans will find scattered throughout; I didn't uncover anything monumentally different from the original game, but eagle-eyed viewers of last month's Nintendo Direct will notice that Lon Lon Ranch is now on level with the rest of Hyrule Field rather than up on a plateau, and that Kakariko Village is now slightly rearranged. Outside of some small upgrades to the diversity and placement of foliage, I didn't notice many more differences, but it's also been several years since I last fired up either the N64 or 3DS version of Ocarina of Time, so it's entirely likely they were subtle enough that I didn't notice.

After entering the Great Deku Tree and completing the first part where you jump off the ledge and break through the spiderweb, I must jump to the next part of the demo. Still as Young Link, I watch as Saria gifts Link the Fairy Ocarina through a touching, fully voiced (outside of Link, obviously) cutscene. From there, I'm dumped into Hyrule Field. It doesn't feel nearly as impressively endless as it felt in 1998, but it is definitely way more beautiful. After a brief conversation with the owl, Kaepora Gaebora, I sprint across Hyrule Field, catching passing yet impressive glimpses of landmarks like Lon Lon Ranch and Death Mountain. One new addition is apparent as I navigate the menus: The Threads of Time. Here, a tapestry tells the story of the Hero of Time in real time; I can look back at what has already happened in Link's adventure, as well as see how much is still on the horizon. Additionally, if you get lost, the tapestry can give hints for where you're supposed to go.

After a brief exploration of Hyrule Castle Town, including a short tour of the Temple of Time, it's time to jump ahead to the final segment of my demo: the Forest Temple. This section lets me try out Adult Link, who is equipped with the hookshot, and later, the Fairy Bow. This was the one place where my memory felt fuzziest, as I struggled to speedrun the puzzles that I last played through more than a decade ago.

Fighting Wolfos, Stalfos, and Skulltulas is more straightforward than most modern third-person fights, which may be the one area that fell slightly short of my admittedly lofty expectations. Using the lock-on targeting allows Link to dodge and counterattack, but I didn't find a simple way to swap targets when you're fighting multiple enemies at once. Instead, I had to release the left trigger, move the camera, and squeeze the trigger again. I get that this is how it worked in the original game, but I would have liked switching to the next target without going through that. Maybe there is an easy way to accomplish that, but I didn't find it in my nearly 90 minutes fighting through Hyrule.

Regardless of that one reservation, there is no doubt in my mind that The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time will consume my life when it comes out. And with October already halfway in the rearview, we really don't have long before a whole new generation gets to experience what could very well be Link's greatest adventure. This remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time comes to Switch 2 on November 5.

Mortgage rates being back above 7% isn’t exactly the news most people wanted. But it’s changing the market in ways that matter whether you’re buying or selling.

The number of homes for sale is growing faster than it was this time last year. And interestingly, the main reason isn’t a flood of new listings. It’s because homes aren’t selling as quickly.

Here’s why – and what that means if you’re thinking about buying or selling.

According to HousingWire Data, after inventory growth slowed down for most of the past year, it’s accelerated again in recent weeks. You can see that in the graph below. Just look at the blue line:

And what’s behind this shift might surprise you.

When rates rise, some buyers hit pause. That means fewer homes sell each week. And as those unsold homes stay on the market alongside new listings, the number available starts to grow (see the blue line in the graph below).

Here’s another way to look at it to make it even more obvious. The graph below shows the relationship between rates and inventory. Again, this year is the blue line. Last year? That’s the green.

With rates inching up this year, you can see inventory has grown by about 25k. But when rates went down this time last year, buyers jumped back in, and inventory actually dropped by about 9k.

“With mortgage rates over 7%, demand tends to soften and inventory rises . . .”

Homes Are Spending More Time on the Market

Here’s another metric that shows the impact rates are having: time on market. Data from Realtor.com shows the typical home spent 61 days on the market this September. Back in September 2021, it was just 41 days (see graph below):

Here’s what that tells us. In 2021, when rates were low, more buyers were active, and homes sold fast. But now that rates are a bit higher, not as many buyers are interested, so it takes a bit longer to close a deal.

So, what does all of this mean for your plans? That depends on which side of the move you’re on:

If you’re buying, more homes for sale means more choices and less pressure to rush. And more room to negotiate too. A house that’s been sitting for a while could have a seller who’s more willing to talk – not just about price, but repairs, closing costs, or even helping buy down your mortgage rate.

That doesn’t mean every seller will play ball. But compared to the frenzy of a few years ago, buyers have more leverage now.

If you’re selling, the right strategy can make all the difference. Buyers have more homes to choose from, so making yours stand out is especially important. That starts with pricing it right from day one, making a great first impression, and working with your agent to highlight what makes your house worth choosing. Buyers are still buying and homes are still selling. The key is making sure yours is positioned to attract the right buyer.

The market hasn’t stopped. It’s just moving at a different pace. And whether you’re buying or selling, your strategy needs to move with it.

The Charge 6 and the Edge both have GPS capabilities, allowing the device to know where you are in the world, even if you left your phone at home. At launch, the Fitbit Edge doesn't support Google Maps directions—but Google's spec sheets say it's "coming soon." To be fair, Google Maps directions were never particularly useful. #3303. Don't let inflation bite — how to hedge with TIPS, commodities and infrastructure funds

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Consumer Price Index hit 3.7% in September – the latest sign that inflation remains a problem that isn't going away.

It's a tough situation for investors looking for protection, but there are some good ways to hedge.

Long-term Treasury Inflation-Protected securities (TIPS) yield 2.9%, near multi-decade highs. Beyond TIPS, analysts suggest commodities, infrastructure, and other assets that can hedge against rising prices.

While inflation is down sharply from its 9.1% peak in 2022, it's still well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target. Headline CPI's 3.7% rate in September topped estimates for 3.6%. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, reached 2.6%, also higher than expected. Rising energy prices drove much of the increase. But the Personal Consumption Expenditures index, the Fed's preferred gauge, also remains elevated at a core 3% rate.

Americans' inflation concerns are also rising. The New York Fed's Survey of Consumer Expectations showed the one-year inflation outlook in September climbed to 3.9%, its highest level since May 2023.

Despite their name, TIPS haven't provided much inflation protection this year. The iShares TIPS Bond ETF, which tracks the ICE U.S. Treasury Inflation Linked Bond Index, has a total return of -1.16% year-to-date.

Like most other bonds, TIPS have been hit hard by rising rates. The 10-year Treasury note is up sharply this year and spiked Wednesday to 5.29%, near the highest levels since 2002. Bond prices and yields move inversely, and as rates climbed many TIPS funds suffered.

But higher yields have made TIPS more attractive. The 10-year TIPS' 2.9% yield is up from roughly 1.9% early in the year.

"Given where rates are, it's getting interesting," says Jason Draho, head of asset allocation Americas at UBS Global Wealth Management.

TIPS pay a fixed coupon, or interest rate, but as inflation moves higher, the principal value of the bond increases. The adjustments reflect changes in inflation, based on the non-seasonally adjusted CPI for All Urban Consumers, with a three-month lag.

The upshot is that TIPS investors earn interest on a higher principal value over time. That's the essence of the protection compared to traditional Treasuries.

TIPS' poor performance this year isn't because of a surge in inflation expectations. Almost all the increase has been due to rising real yields — the nominal yield minus expected inflation. Real yields are up because of factors like robust economic growth and a U.S. fiscal deficit around 6% of gross domestic product (requiring far more government borrowing).

If nominal rates were rising just due to inflation, then TIPS total return — a mix of yield and price — would be fine, said R.J. Gallo, chief investment officer of global fixed income at Federated Hermes. But that isn't the case.

"TIPS have produced losses in total return because their income accruals have been insufficient to offset the price decline produced by the rising underlying real yields," Gallo explained.

TIPS maturing in 10 years or more are highly sensitive to duration risk, Draho notes. "You're making a big call on rates not rising any further if you buy TIPS, especially longer maturities of 10-year plus," he said.

The bull case is that inflation comes down quicker than expected. That's not the market's expectation, based on short-term Treasury yields, notes Draho. But if inflation does ease next year, which he expects, the yield curve would adjust lower.

One way to manage duration risk is to own individual TIPS rather than a fund that has to maintain duration consistent with a benchmark index. Investors could create a TIPS bond ladder with varying maturities, pegged to their income needs over the next decade, for instance.

If you hold a TIPS to maturity you may experience price volatility, but you'll get the original principal or inflation-adjusted price, whichever is greater, when the bond matures.

Short-term TIPS provide more protection against rate increases and they look attractive, said PIMCO portfolio manager Greg Sharenow.

"While as a firm, our view is that inflation will ultimately come down, that view keeps on being pushed out as real world events unfold," he said. "We've seen over and over again the last five to seven years — where we're now in our third supply shock — that you end up actually underpricing versus realized inflation," he added.

Funds focusing on short-term TIPS include Vanguard Short-Term Inflation-Protected Securities ETF, iShares 0-5 Year TIPS Bond ETF and PIMCO 1-5 Year US TIPS Index ETF.

TIPS, of course, aren't the only way to go. Rick Rieder, BlackRock's chief investment officer for global fixed income, suggests a multi-pronged strategy, including equities, commodities, and alternative assets.

"I'd start layering in more income, keep interest-rate exposure moderate, and then what a lot of people are doing is use things like alternatives to to create more balance in a portfolio," he said. "That makes a lot of sense to me."

Broad inflation-protection funds include State Street Multi-Asset Real Return ETF, Fidelity Strategic Real Return Fund and PIMCO Inflation Response Multi-Asset Fund.

The PIMCO fund's holdings include TIPS, commodities, gold and real estate investment trusts.

"The idea is to have a number of different exposures so that we can have a more balanced return and not try to be so specific in getting the exact right asset class at any one moment," said Sharenow, a manager on the fund.

He adds that he sees "significant value" in TIPS, which have the heaviest weighting in the portfolio. Commodities are the next largest holding. They have price "pass-throughs," which is how much companies can shift costs to customers through price increases. That is beneficial to investors, he noted.

Separately, the fund holds about 10% each in gold and REITs.

Draho recommends commodities, based partly on long-term asset-allocation studies that include the 1970s when inflation was far higher. "Over a long period they can help reduce risk and improve diversification," he said. "Their benefits have been most pronounced when inflation was overshooting expectations."

Rather than picking individual commodities, he suggests owning a basket that's consistently rebalanced. Gold and oil, for instance, perform well in different economic and monetary policy scenarios, and it can be tricky to get the timing right.

Commodity ETFs to consider include the Invesco Optimum Yield Diversified Commodity Strategy ETF (PDBC) and the iShares S&P GSCI Commodity-Indexed Trust (GSG).

Another asset class Draho suggests is infrastructure — operators of roads, ports, bridges, and other hard assets. Companies in the space tend to be toll takers, collecting long-term revenue under contracts that include inflation riders or built-in price adjustments.

Investors can get exposure to infrastructure through ETFs such as the iShares Global Infrastructure ETF (IGF) and ProShares DJ Brookfield Global Infrastructure ETF (TOLZ).

Draho isn't sounding alarms on inflation but says it makes sense to add protection against another move higher.

"The risk now is there will be supply side shocks that could push up inflation again," he said.

“Researchers are exploring whether some metabolic conditions commonly seen in women with PMOS, such as insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes, [and] elevated cholesterol, could play a role in dementia, since these are already known risk factors for cognitive decline,” Yesika Garcia, MD, board certified endocrinologist with Medical Offices of Manhattan, who was not involved in the study, told Healthline.

“In other words, any potential connection may be related to these underlying metabolic issues rather than PMOS itself. I think it’s important to clarify that an association does not mean causation,” she added.

The researchers searched PubMed for studies published through 2025 examining brain structure and function, cognitive performance, biomarkers associated with neurodegenerative diseases, and dementia risk in people with PMOS.

Because PMOS is a newer term, the researchers searched for studies using both PMOS and PCOS.

Some brain imaging studies using MRI and PET scans identified differences in brain structure and function among people with PMOS. Researchers also observed hormonal changes associated with differences in gray matter.

Some studies identified elevated biomarkers associated with Alzheimer’s disease in people with PMOS. These findings appeared to overlap with metabolic changes seen in type 2 diabetes, particularly those associated with obesity and insulin resistance.

The researchers also found that neurological differences were less pronounced when people with PMOS were compared with metabolically healthy control groups, suggesting that associated metabolic conditions may play an important role in these findings.

Overall, the review suggests that metabolic conditions associated with PMOS may contribute to changes in brain health, although more research is needed to determine whether PMOS independently affects Alzheimer’s disease or dementia risk.

“I think we need to be careful about drawing conclusions too early. The research raises some good questions, but it doesn’t prove that PCOS [PMOS] causes Alzheimer’s disease,” said Dung Trinh, MD, internist, of MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“What it does reinforce is how important metabolic health may be when it comes to protecting our brains as we get older,” he added.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who also wasn’t involved in the study, agreed. She also added that more attention and research need to be given to PMOS, brain changes, and future risks of dementia.

Trinh explained that insulin resistance may be one of the most important connections between PMOS and dementia.

Although insulin is commonly associated with diabetes and blood sugar regulation, it also plays an important role in brain function. When insulin signaling doesn’t work properly, it may interfere with how brain cells use energy, potentially contributing to problems with memory and thinking over time.

Chronic inflammation, which can occur in people with PMOS, may also play a role in Alzheimer’s disease, Trinh told Healthline.

“Researchers are looking at how these metabolic changes may affect amyloid and tau, two proteins associated with Alzheimer’s. Some brain imaging studies have even found differences in brain structure and function among women with PCOS [PMOS],” he said.

Ross added that elevated androgen levels, which are common in people with PMOS, may also play a role in dementia risk.

“An important distinction is that these risk factors are not present in every woman with PMOS, and having PMOS does not automatically mean someone will experience cognitive decline,” cautioned Garcia.

Garcia noted that many of the dementia risk factors associated with PMOS are modifiable.

“Regular exercise, including both cardiovascular activity and strength training, can improve insulin sensitivity, help with weight management, and support cardiovascular and brain health,” she added.

“A Mediterranean-style diet, with plenty of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, healthy fats, and lean sources of protein, is a reasonable place to start,” he said.

Managing blood sugar, cholesterol, and blood pressure is also important, Trinh and Garcia noted.

“Medication, like metformin, can also improve insulin resistance, which may further reduce the risk of dementia,” Ross added.

Getting adequate sleep, avoiding smoking, and staying mentally and socially active are additional ways to support long-term brain health, Garcia told Healthline.

“For someone with PCOS [PMOS], I would encourage regular visits with their healthcare provider to monitor metabolic health and address problems early,” said Trinh.

“While we cannot say that these interventions specifically prevent dementia in women with PMOS, we know they benefit overall metabolic and cardiovascular health, which are closely connected to brain health,” explained Garcia.
