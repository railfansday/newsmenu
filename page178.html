#2561. FEATURE | Niclas Füllkrug is proving that returning to Werder Bremen was the perfect decision

Niclas Füllkrug’s return to Werder Bremen is quickly becoming one of the best stories of the 2026/27 Bundesliga season. The 33-year-old scored twice in Saturday’s 3-2 victory over Mainz, taking his tally to eight goals in seven league matches and helping Daniel Thioune’s side climb to sixth place.

After two difficult years abroad, Füllkrug has rediscovered the goalscoring form that once made him one of Germany’s most effective strikers. More importantly, his performances are demonstrating that returning to a familiar environment can sometimes be more valuable than pursuing another opportunity at a bigger club.

Against Mainz, Füllkrug demonstrated his composure by converting two penalties in the opening 25 minutes. His first came after Arthur was fouled inside the penalty area, while the second followed a challenge on Marco Grüll.

Although Anthony Caci reduced the deficit before half-time, Ludovit Reis restored Bremen’s two-goal advantage in the 61st minute. Jaesung Lee responded almost immediately, but Werder held on to secure their first away victory of the season.

Füllkrug has now scored in six consecutive Bundesliga matches, including braces against Paderborn and Mainz. His eight goals in seven matchdays underline just how influential he has become since returning to the Weserstadion.

What makes Füllkrug’s impact particularly impressive is that his contribution extends beyond scoring.

His physical presence provides Bremen with a reliable attacking reference point, allowing teammates to advance into dangerous positions while he occupies defenders. His ability to compete in aerial duels, protect possession and link attacks gives Thioune’s team something they desperately needed.

Against Mainz, Füllkrug contested 15 duels, more than any other player on the pitch, winning approximately 58% of them. Those numbers demonstrate his willingness to contribute even when he is not directly involved in goalscoring opportunities.

When Füllkrug left Bremen for Borussia Dortmund in 2023, it was the right moment to pursue a bigger challenge. Reaching the UEFA Champions League final confirmed that decision. However, his subsequent move to West Ham United proved far less successful, with injuries and limited opportunities disrupting his career.

Returning to Bremen has changed that narrative completely.

Füllkrug is once again playing regularly, scoring consistently and enjoying the responsibility of leading a Bundesliga attack. His performances have also strengthened the argument for a return to the Germany national team.

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of this story is how quickly he has transformed Bremen’s expectations. Thioune’s side are now unbeaten in six consecutive Bundesliga matches and have collected 12 points from their opening seven games.

For a club that has spent recent seasons worrying about relegation, having a striker capable of delivering eight goals in seven matches provides genuine optimism.

Füllkrug’s return was initially celebrated as an emotional homecoming. Seven matchdays later, it is becoming something much more significant.

Bremen have rediscovered their goalscorer, while Füllkrug has rediscovered the environment that brings out his best football. Sometimes, coming home really is the best decision.

Many blame U.S. President Donald Trump’s decision to go to war with Iran, as well as his trade war with Canada, for hurting their wallets. And while Trump himself isn’t on the ballot, voters seem increasingly likely to turn against his party.

Most major election forecasters now predict that the Democratic Party will win control of the House. Should that happen, Democrats would oversee the writing of the chamber’s annual military and foreign aid spending bills. They’d also have the power to convene high-profile hearings to examine the executive branch’s foreign-policy moves.

The outlook for control of the Senate is murkier, though forecasters say Democrats’ chances of winning it have improved in recent weeks. If Democrats win the Senate, they will gain the additional power of blocking votes on all of Trump’s national security nominees, which could be important leverage for looming policy fights over Iran, China, artificial intelligence, and troop movements.

Here are the top five foreign-policy priorities that Democrats are likely to focus on if they win control of one or both chambers of Congress in early November:

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks in front of the new Air Force One, gifted to him by Qatar, at Joint Base Andrews in Maryland, United States, on June 19.Brendan Smialowski/AFP via Getty Images

Democrats see alleged corruption committed by members of the Trump administration as a potent campaign message, connecting voter anger over the high cost of living with reports of self-dealing by Trump’s friends and family. And they’re pledging to investigate these allegations if they win the House.

“Trump has used national security and foreign policy as one of his primary tools for monetizing the government,” Rep. Jason Crow, who is leading House Democrats’ efforts to win in battleground districts around the country, told Foreign Policy. He accused the president of carrying out “with astonishing disregard” a “racketeering shake-down operation to earn money and promote his businesses.”

One very visible target for Democratic inquiries could include Trump’s acceptance of a $400 million luxury jet from Qatar. Another could be the alleged links between Abu Dhabi’s investments in the Trump family’s crypto business and the White House’s decision to approve the export of 500,000 advanced chips to the United Arab Emirates. That happened despite fears that the sensitive technology would be shared with China.

“The blatant corruption you’re seeing at all levels, in particular foreign corruption, foreign money flowing to the president’s family and his top associates, and the ways that they have not only blurred the lines but clearly stepped over them in clear violation of the law and ethical standards” is also likely to be a top focus in the Senate if Democrats win control there, said a Democratic Senate staffer, speaking on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to be quoted.

Sen. Chris Murphy told reporters in October that he wanted to see Democrats use any new oversight powers to probe Trump’s decision to rely on family members and personal friends—including Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff—to conduct high-stakes diplomatic negotiations.

“The White House and Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff want to keep diplomacy with the Gulf to themselves because it’s a grift,” Murphy said. “They don’t want powerful ambassadors to ring alarm bells about their corruption, and so there are not going to be any qualified ambassadors serving the Gulf, because if there were, those people would likely stand in the way of Trump’s corruption.”—Rachel Oswald

The casket of U.S. Army Sgt. Angel Rampersad, who was killed in action at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, is brought out during a funeral service in New York City on July 31. Spencer Platt/Getty Images

The Iran war is deeply unpopular with U.S. voters and could hurt Republicans on Election Day perhaps more than any other issue. The war has been frustrating to the U.S. public for an array of reasons—from driving up gas prices and inflation to the Trump administration’s shifting goals and opaque strategy. Trump has also repeatedly downplayed the economic fallout from the conflict. Meanwhile, there’s no end in sight to the war.

Though some Republicans on Capitol Hill have also been critical of the conflict and the administration’s handling of it, Democratic efforts in Congress to end the war have so far fallen short.

But if Democrats win back one or both chambers in November, they will have more avenues to make continuing the war difficult for Trump—including by moving to cut off funding and ramping up scrutiny of the administration’s approach via hearings and investigations.

“Priority one is taking control of committees and issuing subpoenas and going after the information we’ve been seeking: How much does the Iran war cost? How many casualties? What is the damage to facilities? What is the current state of the weapon stockpile?” Crow said.

Crow, who sits on the House Armed Services Committee, said one of his most urgent questions is learning what the U.S. military is doing to protect the roughly 55,000 U.S. service members deployed to the Middle East from drone attacks.

Murphy, who sits on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, accused U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth of covering up the “cost and consequences of this war from the American public.” He said ending the war should be a priority for Democrats.

“It is absolutely outrageous that I and Republican senators are being denied the ability to see the damage for ourselves,” Murphy said. “This is a really dangerous moment, when the administration is giving its middle finger to Congress, pretending as if we are totally irrelevant.”

Murphy said that if Democrats win in the Senate, they will have “an ability to decide whether we’re going to fund this war and whether we’re going to pass a defense authorization bill that allows this war to continue.” The senator, who is among the most prominent Democratic voices on foreign policy in the upper chamber, said that if the war is still ongoing in early 2027, then Congress should not “appropriate funding for the Department of Defense without a requirement that this war end … and we should not pass” any new funding without that stipulation.—John Haltiwanger

The Trump administration has rattled European partners by threatening to annex Greenland, mooting a major drawdown of U.S. troops, and pursuing policies favorable to Russia.

A Democratic win would challenge some of those plans.

“We know they are trying to change the footprint in Europe. We know this plan to review the footprint in Europe is designed to eventually reduce it, … and we’ll resist those things because we have broad policy disagreements with the administration,” Crow said.

Ukraine, long starved of new supplies of U.S. aid, could see Congress seek to rush more weapons to the country. “There’s pretty robust agreement that we should support Ukraine, so we are going to figure out how we bolster the [Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative] money and how we put that into the NDAA and how we compel the administration to procure those weapons,” Crow said, referring to the National Defense Authorization Act.

Aid packages for Ukraine could also move faster, said Rep. Jim Himes, the ranking member of the House Intelligence Committee, speaking last week in Warsaw.

“It means that aid packages move not in nine months but in nine days or nine weeks on the outside and that the message [of support to Ukraine] is very different,” he said.

However, there is also now a broader consensus that Europe should do more for its defense, even among Democrats. “There will be a continuation on the part of Americans to keep pushing the Europeans to assume more of a role,” Himes said. “We may simply not have the money to play the role that we have played in the past.”—Sam Skove

Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro arrives in New York on Jan. 5, following his capture from Caracas during a U.S. military raid. XNY/Star Max via Getty Images

The Trump administration has been conducting strikes on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Eastern Pacific for more than a year. The strikes, which experts have decried as illegal under both domestic and international law, have killed at least 246 people. The Trump administration has said the strikes are targeting dangerous “narco-terrorists,” but it has not provided evidence to back up its assertions.

Congressional Democrats have been critical of the strikes and the Trump administration’s broader goals in Latin America, as the United States maintains a sizable military presence in the region and urges more governments to participate in joint operations against drug cartels. But Democratic efforts to rein in Trump in Latin America and stop continued operations via war powers resolutions have so far failed.

Much of what the Trump administration has done in Latin America has occurred quietly or been overshadowed by other developments and events, such as the Iran war. But a Democratic win in November could significantly increase oversight and put more of a spotlight on the administration’s activities in the region via hearings and investigations. If they gain a majority in either chamber, Democrats could also potentially move to curb military operations in Latin America by leveraging or restricting funding.

The boat strikes began last September as a prelude to the operation that captured Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, whom the Trump administration has accused of drug trafficking. (Experts have said this is difficult to prove.) And the White House’s actions in Venezuela since have also been controversial. Trump effectively embraced the existing regime, minus Maduro, rather than pushing for a democratic process or supporting exiled opposition leader María Corina Machado.

The White House has justified many of its actions in the country by pointing to the potential for the United States to tap into Venezuela’s vast oil reserves. The administration in late August announced an agreement with the Venezuelan government that would give the United States “control” over more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s oil. But there are many reasons to doubt that the United States will benefit from the deal to the extent that Trump has claimed.

Democrats will be poised to press Trump officials for more answers on these issues if they win big in November. Top Democrats in both the House and Senate have repeatedly called for more transparency and accountability regarding both the boat strikes and U.S. involvement in Venezuelan oil production.—John Haltiwanger

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg speaks to the press with Trump in Washington on Sept. 29. Kent Nishimura/AFP via Getty Images

The leaps in AI capabilities this year alone have alarmed policymakers in Washington nearly as much as the broader geopolitical conflicts the United States is engaged in.

AI models are going rogue and hacking websites, bad actors are allegedly using them to try to fine-tune ballistic missile technology, and companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic are warning that their technology could end humanity and imploring the government to regulate them.

But Trump doesn’t appear interested in doing so and instead has urged companies to keep developing—with only a “morally binding” self-policing pledge that Trump had top AI companies sign.

Democrats feel differently. “Donald Trump thinks he is the smartest man in the room and he can handle it, and he is wrong,” Crow told Foreign Policy. “We know that the government has a role. We know that we need to pump the brakes on AI development so we have time to put appropriate guardrails on.”

That view appears to be bicameral (and possibly bipartisan), according to Murphy. “The biggest issue of trust is the lack of trust the people have that we’re going to actually protect them from the poison, protect them from the potential enormous downside of unregulated, unchecked AI,” the senator told Foreign Policy onstage at the FP Tech & AI Forum in September. “It might be the end of our democracy if the people beg, on both sides of the aisle, for us to do what we’ve done with every complicated new technology in the history of this country since the Industrial Revolution—regulate and control it to make sure we get the good but not the bad—[and] we don’t step up and do the work.”

Murphy outlined three areas that lawmakers can focus on: outlawing the use of deepfakes in politics or mandating watermarks for AI-generated content; banning so-called AI companions or “friendship bots” that he described as “poison for our kids”; and instituting an approval process for new advanced AI models before they are released.

“Those three things are achievable, not terribly political, and could foreseeably get done and then give the public some faith that we have the political capacity to handle some of the more nuanced threats down the line,” he said.—Rishi Iyengar

Michael Owen has never been one to shout. At his absolute fresh-faced turn-of-the-century peak, there was no swagger, no smug look-at-me celebration – just the sense of someone going about his job to the best of his considerable ability, banging in goals for club and country.

In 2001, Owen was irresistible. He picked up six trophies that year: the FA Cup, League Cup and UEFA Cup treble, plus the Charity Shield and UEFA Super Cup – and then, just three days after turning 22, the Ballon d’Or. He remains the last Englishman to win the award.

The gong came on the back of 31 goals for a Liverpool side desperate to reinstate itself as a force domestically and in Europe. The bigger the game, the better he played. A highly intelligent footballer with brilliant movement, he could time his runs to perfection. He was also, despite his youthful stature, an absolute fiend to get off the ball.

Owen was blessed with a ruthlessness as well; a single-mindedness worthy of Roy Keane. Michael was there, Robbie Fowler, Danny Murphy, Steven Gerrard, Didi Hamann, Jamie Redknapp – people I’m still close with now were in that team. Sharing those things together is special.”

That particular Reds outfit wasn’t the easiest on the eye. The path to victory was often built on frustrating the opposition and grinding out results. With Manchester United and Arsenal frequently operating on a completely different level to everyone else, Gerard Houllier’s charges worked within their limitations, making the most of counter-attacking opportunities and a dogged team spirit. “We were a tough side, with a fantastic back four,” recalls Owen. “Sami Hyypia, Stephane Henchoz, Carragher – they were like trojans in every single match they played. We had Hamann and Gerrard too, of course. We had guts, and no one played more games than we did that season. We played every game that was available to us, and that in itself is no mean achievement.”The first half of that mammoth 63-match campaign had been fairly humdrum, with little to suggest what was to come. Owen, unable to help England out of their Euro 2000 group, hit seven goals in his first six league appearances of the season, including a hat-trick against Aston Villa, but then scored only once in his next 13. A seven-month period from October to April brought him two Premier League goals. Liverpool weren’t pulling up any trees, either, losing to Ipswich and Middlesbrough in December. Then, something began to spark around the turn of the year. A lot of that was down to Owen clicking into gear.“In that season, with Owen, Fowler, Emile Heskey and Jari Litmanen, we possibly had the best set of strikers in the world,” says Carragher. “Michael didn’t really have a great first half of the season, but as soon as he got his form back, it was scary.”

Owen actually didn’t feature in the first of Liverpool’s trophy wins that season: he was an unused substitute as Houllier started Heskey and Fowler in the League Cup final at Cardiff’s Millennium Stadium. They eventually beat second-tier Birmingham in a penalty shootout. But while Fowler started in Cardiff, he found himself falling down the Anfield pecking order. Houllier clearly preferred the pairing of Heskey and Owen. The Frenchman had been steadily building his team since arriving on Merseyside in July 1998, initially as joint-manager with Roy Evans. Like his countryman Arsene Wenger, Houllier was something of a footballing Anglophile, keen to marry a Gallic eye for detail with classic English strengths of teamwork, resilience and belief.“He really dragged the club into this new era of how you look after yourself and prepare,” explains Owen. “We were looking at Arsenal and they were mind-blowing. We were thinking, ‘These buggers don’t get injured, they run faster than us, they run longer than us, they’re never resting players – what the hell are they doing?’“Houllier was the first to introduce those ideas at Anfield. He could never tell Gerrard how to pass a ball, or me how to finish, or Carragher how to defend, but what he was really good at was judging players. Tactically he was very astute. He was good at motivating and getting us working as a unit. He was a very good leader of people.”After lifting the League Cup, it was full steam ahead on all fronts, with the UEFA Cup proving a particularly potent platform. The club sensed that it was back on the European stage once again, free from the long shadows cast by events at Heysel and the subsequent ban from Europe.

“The UEFA Cup was massive back then,” continues Owen. “We played Barcelona, Roma, Porto, Olympiakos – and that’s before we got to the final. It was a really difficult competition to win.”Owen had scored twice against Fabio Capello’s in-form Roma at the Stadio Olimpico in February. Liverpool’s quarter-final tie against Porto began with a goalless draw in Portugal, but two quick first-half goals at Anfield from Murphy and Owen, heading in a Gerrard corner, gave Liverpool a comfortable lead that they protected without much grief.Barcelona awaited in the semis, live on the BBC. Incredibly, kick-off for the first leg at the Camp Nou was put back to 8.10pm at the Beeb’s request, to avoid clashing with EastEnders and the revelation of who shot Phil Mitchell. Presumably all of Barcelona wanted to find out, too. But there was little drama on offer in Catalonia as the Reds defended in numbers to thwart a Barça side featuring Rivaldo, Luis Enrique, Pep Guardiola and Frank de Boer. The return leg brought a famous victory, with 36-year-old Gary McAllister scoring the only goal from the penalty spot following a Patrick Kluivert handball. It was to be Liverpool’s first European final in 16 years. “We could always take big scalps,” smiles Carragher. “That was the great thing about that team – we may not have been the best, but we could beat the best.”They were also repeating the trick domestically, working their way to the FA Cup final against Arsenal, back at the Millennium Stadium. Owen went into the game in peak form: in his previous two matches, he’d scored three goals against Newcastle, then two against Chelsea. Liverpool faced a Gunners side above them in the league table, and Wenger’s side dominated for the most part. Arsenal had three shots blocked on the line – one by Henchoz’s hand, which went unnoticed by the match officials – and once Freddie Ljungberg had rounded Sander Westerveld to put Arsenal in front with 18 minutes remaining, time was rapidly running out for the Reds. Nobody was prepared for what happened next – except perhaps one man.

In the 83rd minute, McAllister launched a free-kick into the penalty area, Markus Babbel nodded the ball down and Owen found space in the crowd to fire in the equaliser. He looks back on that moment now with something approaching reverence; that it was meant to be, even when playing rope-a-dope in the blazing Welsh sun.

“Once I scored that first goal, I jogged back to the halfway line and this feeling came over me: I just knew,” he reveals. “It was like the Ali fight when Foreman was smacking him on the ropes and it seemed impossible, but suddenly, one punch and I knew that Arsenal were gone. They were gone. The only question in my mind was, ‘Have I got enough time to score before the final whistle goes?’ It wasn’t ‘if’, it was ‘when’. I’d never had that feeling before, and I’ve never felt it since.”

Was there time for a winner? Barely five minutes later, Owen had his answer. He latched onto a long pass from Patrik Berger, shrugging off Lee Dixon, before one touch took him away from Tony Adams. On his weaker foot, Owen coolly dispatched a shot into the small goal-bound gap just beyond David Seaman’s outstretched left hand. Michael Owen had won the FA Cup. The forward-flip goal celebration; the screaming, wide-eyed delight; the mayhem in the stands – it all belonged to him.

Once I scored that first goal [in the 2001 FA Cup final], I jogged back to the halfway line and this feeling came over me: I just knew.

“It was such a magical day,” he says. “It’s funny: good things happen and you get caught up in the joy of winning for your team-mates and fans. There’s nothing bad about it when you have a day like that.”Days were becoming a precious commodity at Liverpool. They had only three of them between the FA Cup showpiece in Cardiff and their third final of the campaign, against Alaves in the UEFA Cup. The club from Vitoria-Gasteiz, capital city of the Basque Country, were the big surprise package of the season: they had triumphed 2-0 at the San Siro against an Inter team boasting Christian Vieri and Clarence Seedorf, before shellacking Kaiserslautern 9-2 on aggregate in the semi-finals.Although Liverpool’s players were now feeling the strains of a long season, they were quickly 2-0 up in Dortmund thanks to Babbel and Gerrard. Alaves pulled a goal back, but Owen tumbled in the box, and McAllister converted the penalty. Houllier’s side led 3-1 at the interval. “I always remember coming into the dressing room, looking around and almost giggling,” Owen admits to FFT. “Some of us were thinking, ‘How good is this? It’s almost a gimme’. You’re expecting the final to be hard, and at half-time I was thinking that they were so easy to beat. Never, ever have I been so wrong. We almost blew it.”

Six minutes into the second half, the score was 3-3. Liverpool’s solid defence was suddenly a shambles. Fowler put them back in front, only for Jordi Cruyff to glance home another Alaves equaliser in the final minute of normal time. “On the way to the final, everyone was saying we were boring – lots of 0-0s and 1-0s – and then it all just went mad in the final!” recalls Carragher with a laugh. In the end, Liverpool and Owen were able to complete their treble. Alaves had two men sent off in extra time, then Delfi Geli’s own goal gave the Reds a dramatic 5-4 win via the short-lived golden goal rule. It was the club’s first European trophy since Rome in 1984.“It was where the club needed to be,” explains Owen. “It was such a buzz. The fans were amazing – it felt like we’d taken a million of them to Dortmund.” It was also an important moment for Houllier. “He was desperate to win the UEFA Cup, to put us back on the European map,” adds Owen. “He understood the club’s history and values.”After two knackering cup finals, there was still a final Premier League match to play in May. Win at Charlton, and Liverpool would be in the Champions League for the first time, back in Europe’s elite competition for the first time since that fateful night at Heysel.

Three days after the UEFA Cup final, Liverpool emerged 4-0 victors at The Valley. Owen set up Murphy to net the third, and scored the fourth himself. The Reds had come 3rd in the table, 11 points off champions Manchester United but down from 24 the previous campaign. “We’d won all those trophies that season and went away that summer proud as punch,” says Owen. “We just needed one or two more players to get a bit better. We wanted to win the league with Liverpool so much. Me and Carra used to speak about it all the time. The desire within us was almost unbearable.”Owen had the same desire to achieve something with England. With the summer over, and the 2001-02 season only one month old, the national team travelled to Munich to take on Germany in a World Cup qualifier. Trailing to a sixth-minute Carsten Jancker goal, Sven-Goran Eriksson’s men were soon back on level terms, with Owen finding the empty net after Nick Barmby had beaten Oliver Kahn to a high ball. Gerrard put England in front and then Owen ran riot, lashing a strike past a flat-footed Kahn before charging clear to wrap up his hat-trick. It confirmed the 21-year-old as Europe’s most dangerous marksman.In December came recognition of that fact, as Owen was named the winner of the 2001 Ballon d’Or. He received a total of 176 votes from a panel of sports journalists across UEFA’s member countries. Raul was second with 140 votes, Kahn third with 114 and David Beckham fourth with 102. Francesco Totti, Luis Figo, Rivaldo, Andriy Shevchenko, Thierry Henry and Zinedine Zidane filled out the top 10, but none of them received even a third of Owen’s total votes.

We wanted to win the league with Liverpool so much. The desire within us was almost unbearable

Owen may not have been able to call himself the best player in the world – only in 2007 did the Ballon d’Or become a global award – but he could officially proclaim himself the best player in Europe. He was only the fourth Englishman to receive the honour and the first since Kevin Keegan, a back-to-back winner with Hamburg in 1978 and ’79.Back in 2001, however, there was very little fanfare about Owen’s prestigious achievement. In England, the cult of the individual had yet to take hold. Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo had yet to emerge. Owen wasn’t even presented with the award at Anfield until April, on the pitch a few minutes before a run-of-the-mill 2-0 win over Derby. Owen scored twice that day, but that was as far as the celebrations went. No gala ceremony. No garish designer tux.“I believe I don’t get the sort of credit that other people would get for winning the Ballon d’Or,” Owen admits today. “It’s never been that important in our country. When I was in Spain, they said I was a hero for winning it. I’m immensely proud of winning it. I look down the list of names, and I still have it saved on my phone – a photo of the points system and who was second, third, fourth and the rest of it.“You look at that list and it’s awe-inspiring. That’s all I ever wanted as a kid. I didn’t just want to be a footballer; I wanted to be the best footballer. Winning it feels better now than it did when I was playing. You’re 22 – it’s just another trophy, and now I’ve got to win it again.”

He never did, of course. Injuries, bad luck and Liverpool’s below-par signings didn’t help, as finishing runners-up to Arsenal in 2001-02 was followed by a slide down the table. “We brought in El Hadji Diouf, Salif Diao and Bruno Cheyrou – guys who were expected to propel us, and it just went totally the opposite way,” says Carragher.Owen and his team-mates struggled to push on from that cup treble of 2000-01 and Houllier eventually departed in 2004, having appeared to run out of ideas. When the chance of a fresh start presented itself to Owen that summer – and at Real Madrid, no less – the 24-year-old was ready to listen.“The last thing I thought was that I was going to leave Liverpool,” says Owen. “We were on a pre-season tour in America and my agent phoned me while I was in my room with Carragher, who got wind of what we were talking about. I put down the phone and he said, ‘Pffft, don’t go. They’ve got Raul, Ronaldo and Fernando Morientes – you won’t get a game!’ I was 50-50, but I just had this overriding feeling towards it – and I’m blaming Ian Rush, actually! I thought, ‘If I do go, then I’ve already played for Liverpool for a long time and, hopefully, I could always come back, so just go and sample it – the Galacticos; that white kit, where everyone prances out like an angel; that amazing stadium; a different culture’.

The last thing I thought was that I was going to leave Liverpool

“Eventually I agreed. But do you know when you sign something and think there’s no going back? When you think, ‘Oh my God, what have I done?’ I remember crying my eyes out as I went off to the airport, thinking, ‘What am I leaving behind?’”Owen stops short of using the word ‘regret’, but leaving Liverpool still weighs heavily on him – as does the rather mixed opinions of some on the Anfield terraces towards their former player. His fine international career possibly didn’t help, never quite shaking the sense that he was England’s Michael Owen rather than Liverpool’s Michael Owen. Nor did that squeaky-clean image, with all of the rough edges smoothed out by PRs and agents.John Gibbons, of Liverpool fan site and podcast The Anfield Wrap, thinks that it’s probably down to sheer numbers – that, and signing for Manchester United in 2009.“We’ve always been spoilt for strikers,” says Gibbons. “We’ve found it a lot more difficult to find a decent left-back over the past 20 years than a centre-forward. That allows people to discard Michael Owen too easily. Robbie Fowler was more loved, but Owen was brilliant for Liverpool. He would always put so much into it when he was on the pitch. You could tell that he was desperate to win.

“I can understand why he gets so frustrated, because he probably sees players spoken of a lot more fondly when they didn’t put in half the work he did. A lot of it is about what happened afterwards. Going to Real Madrid didn’t go down very well, then when he came back to England and joined Newcastle, it was a surprise. But it was signing for Manchester United that really severed all ties.”Carragher believes his former room-mate’s heroics of 2001 should place him alongside the Anfield greats; that he should get more love from the club’s fanbase. “I think in time that will come,” he suggests.Virgil van Dijk had hopes of getting his own hands on the trophy in 2019. But for all of their legends, for all of the club’s European glory, only one player has ever bagged the Ballon d’Or while playing for Liverpool. Michael Owen.

NASA is overhauling how it selects and develops science missions, with changes intended to reduce costs, streamline competition and get missions into space faster.

The new approach, called Competition Model 2.0 (CM 2.0), was announced on Oct. 21 in an open letter from Nicky Fox, associate administrator of NASA's Science Mission Directorate (SMD), to the NASA science community. The changes, directed by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, could significantly reshape how scientists, universities, NASA centers and commercial companies vie for future missions.

"We have heard you. We have listened. We know that the burden of bringing great ideas forward has been too heavy for too long," Fox stated in the letter.

The new process aims to prioritize scientific merit over unduly complicated engineering and financial planning during initial evaluations, and reduce selection timelines to encourage innovation.

Fox and other top NASA science officials will provide an overview of the reforms during a virtual town hall today (Oct. 26) at 2 p.m. EDT (1800 GMT). A livestream of the event will be available here, with an opportunity for the public to submit questions here.

NASA currently selects many of its science missions through competitive Announcements of Opportunity (AOs), inviting scientists and research institutions to submit proposals. Under the existing two-step process, competing teams must include thorough engineering and financial details as part of their proposals before NASA selects finalists for further study. The process can take almost three years, requiring significant investments of time and resources from teams that may ultimately never get selected.

CM 2.0 aims to reduce that burden by emphasizing scientific distinction during initial evaluations, allowing teams to defer much of the engineering and financial planning until later. Instead, initial proposals will focus on the scientific objectives of a mission and a preliminary plan for how to accomplish them.

NASA organizes its science missions into different classes based on their size, cost and objectives, with selection procedures varying between them. These range from smaller Explorer missions to large, expensive flagships like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

Under CM 2.0, programs including smaller Discovery planetary missions, larger New Frontiers spacecraft and Astro-Probes, a program that focuses on mid-sized astronomy missions, will retain a streamlined version of the existing two-step process, but with nearly half of the material required for initial proposals. "This means that the original 35-months timeline will be reduced to about 19.5 months, all while having a stronger, more mature mission at the time of selection," Fox wrote.

Flagship missions, meanwhile, will continue following their current procedures, which were recently revised based on lessons learned from a study of best practices examining the development of JWST.

Medium-class Explorer (MIDEX) and Earth Explorer missions will open to a wider range of applicants, including universities and commercial organizations, and will have similar changes implemented for their two-step selection process. NASA plans to evaluate prospective mission developers based on their track records with projects of similar size, cost and complexity, and offer principal investigator-led teams the opportunity to request support from NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center or Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) during the first stage of selection.

Smaller, lower-cost science programs will see a more substantial selection process shakeup. "Our smallest missions will be riding the momentum of a maturing commercial space economy and will move even faster," Fox wrote.

Under CM 2.0, these programs will move to a single-stage selection process, eliminating the need for a second round of competing proposals entirely.

These include the Small Explorer (SMEX) and Earth Venture missions, which conduct astronomy and heliophysics research, and targeted studies of Earth and its climate. Missions of Opportunity, which allow researchers to piggyback scientific investigations onto other missions, as well as hosted payloads and rideshares, will fall under the same one-step umbrella, which is expected to drop final selection timelines from approximately 32 months to just 19.

"This means more missions, more often, with more incredible science at a lower cost," Fox wrote.

Beyond shortening competition times, the changes put particular emphasis on the roles of Goddard and JPL for developing NASA's science missions. NASA hopes that involving those centers more directly in mission maturations will reduce competition between the centers while maintaining the expertise of the agency's workforce.

The reforms also come with stricter oversight, with NASA insisting on "rigorous cost and schedule control" across its mission classes. "Breaches of control thresholds will carry real consequences," Fox wrote, adding that such consequences could include mission cancellations in the face of unmet expectations.

Together, the changes represent an attempt to shift NASA's mission selection process to one focused on prioritizing scientific merit. The longer-term outcome of the new approach, the agency hopes, will reduce the financial and technical burden on competing research teams and ultimately get missions from idea to orbit with efficiency and speed.

"This is a moment of real opportunity," Fox wrote. "We have an administrator who is empowering us to move faster and a commercial market that is ready to help. We have NASA Centers with unmatched expertise, and a science community whose ideas expand and redefine the bounds of human achievement in the cosmos."

#373. The Tragedy of Having a Wikipedia Page—and Then Losing It

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What do you need to know—need to know—about Xavier Trudeau? The eldest son of former Canadian prime minister Justin, the teenage Trudeau has recently begun performing R&B under the mononym Xav. But, with all due respect to Xav, do those facts make him a person of note? Should you expect to be able to read about him on Wikipedia? What about Evanescence guitarist Tim McCord, or 2012 Yachtmaster of the Year Rainer Holtorff?

All three have had a biography on Wikipedia; all three of those biographies were considered for deletion this year on the grounds that the subjects were not notable enough to warrant a biography at all. Every day, dozens, sometimes hundreds, of Wikipedia biographies—of child actors turned LA realtors, retired ambassadors, soccer players who have played less than 10 minutes of professional soccer—are purged from the world’s free encyclopedia.

It’s not as though Wikipedia, which hosts over 7 million articles in the English language, doesn’t have the space. But Wikipedia has only so many volunteers to monitor its contents. To this day, Wikipedia editors are discovering hoaxes that have gone undetected for decades. The time it takes to verify the release dates of Xav’s three singles is better spent—the logic goes—by asking whether the American Civil War officer Nathaniel Lyon really drank mustard during battle, as was claimed in his Wikipedia biography for 17 years. Wikipedia removed the mustard claim, not because it was proven wrong, but because there was no reliable evidence for it. “We’re not really looking for what’s true,” Britta Gustafson, who has been editing Wikipedia since 2001, told me. “We’re looking for what’s verifiable.”

Five hauntings from the other side of technology.

Have a ghost story of your own? Share it in the comments below.

Wikipedia editors do not themselves decide that a person is not notable, only that there is a lack of reliable evidence to support the claim. One person who recently lost her Wikipedia biography on notability grounds is Kimberly Small, the CEO of a Nevada nonprofit that supports survivors of sexual assault. The debate occurred in a public page on Wikipedia itself; it’s in that space that the really tricky decisions about what is and what isn’t good enough for the encyclopedia are made. One editor raised a question: “How should we judge not-for-profit executives? By the nature of their job, the focus is, as it should be, on the organization and the work the organization does, not them.” Another editor replied: “I don’t think there is anything wrong with not-for-profit executives being less often individually notable. Wikipedia coverage is not a prize.”

Is it not? “Having an article says something,” Gustafson admitted. “It implies something.” For six weeks over April and May, I reached out to hundreds of people who were about to lose their Wikipedia page, almost all of whom were learning that information from me. “If you want to write something to save the page, I would appreciate it,” a Spanish filmmaker told me. A biography on Wikipedia might not be intended as a prize, but nobody knows its value like the people who’ve had one and then lost it.

Martina Dimoska is a scientist, originally from North Macedonia, who works at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston. Specifically, Dimoska is an “analog astronaut”—she takes part in missions that simulate a space environment—and a “science communicator.” She posts often about her work in the can-do, aspirational register that is de rigueur on LinkedIn, where she cites her “recognition by … Forbes, Bloomberg, the United Nations … and global innovation networks.” A Facebook reel of hers, filmed during the crew announcement of NASA’s upcoming Artemis III mission, shows Dimoska alongside the text, “When you work hard so you belong in rooms where history gets proactively written.” On TikTok, she’s had paid partnerships with a makeup brand whose products are tested for effectiveness during spaceflight.

On April 29, Dimoska posted on Facebook, “Wait … I’m on Wikipedia now???” She went on: “A few years ago, building a career in space from the Balkans felt impossible. And now … somehow, that journey is part of the public record. I’m incredibly grateful that [the biography] is there … My hope is that through my journey, I will help a new generation to begin to see space as accessible, not distant.”

The next day, Dimoska’s page was nominated for deletion.

Anne Clin, who has edited Wikipedia since 2005 under the name Risker, warned me that it’s folly to celebrate becoming the subject of a Wikipedia article. “Especially if you’re what we call ‘borderline notable,’” she said.

The other taboo is editing information about yourself. You can’t do that—even if the existing facts about you are wrong—without disclosing a conflict of interest. Even then, it’s strongly discouraged. Jimmy Wales, the cofounder of Wikipedia, has been caught editing his own Wikipedia biography multiple times; that he did so is now part of his Wikipedia biography. In 2022, the novelist Emily St. John Mandel, unable to update her biography to reflect her recent divorce, sat for an interview solely to create a secondary source for that information.

Some high-profile Wikipedia editors and volunteers have bio pages about themselves. Clin is occasionally asked if she would want one. She doesn’t merely refuse; she actively sabotages the possibility. “You cannot write a proper biographical article about me, because I don’t give out enough personal information to do that,” she said. “Absolutely not. Absolutely not.”

Among the people reading Martina Dimoska’s Facebook post about her new Wikipedia page was Kiril Simeonovski, an economist in Skopje, Macedonia, and a Wikipedia editor since 2008. Simeonovski found the post self-congratulatory and the biography itself entirely promotional. He also doubted the inherent notability of an “analog astronaut,” a role that he later likened to a lab mouse.

A Wikipedia editor can tag an article for deletion, and unless satisfactory edits are made within a certain timeframe, the page will disappear. Simeonovski didn’t do this—he referred the article for debate among his fellow editors.

Arguing the case for deletion, Simeonovski described the Dimoska biography as “merely [a] chronology of overhyped personal achievements,” each meticulously deflated by Simeonovski, that did not credibly establish Dimoska as a notable individual.

More damningly, in his argument, was the timing of Dimoska’s Facebook post, coming “shortly after the article was approved.” To him, this was a smoking gun as much as anything. How could she have so promptly promoted the page if she were not somehow connected to it?

When an editor proposes a Wikipedia article for deletion, they append a caveat to the marked page: “If you created this page, please don’t take offense.” This courtesy is not extended to the subject of the article. When I reached out to these people, in the hundreds, to ask them how it felt to be considered non-notable, I was not surprised that the response rate to this question was about 1 percent. Maybe lower.

Matthew Songer, a Michigan orthopedist with three decades of experience, had his biography flagged for deletion (“Unlikely to be notable”) in April. Songer was among the 1 percent of respondees. He told me it’s not for vanity that he needs that page. He is often enlisted as an expert witness in trials, he said, because of the extra imprimatur of being on Wikipedia. The page “is a good reference for attorneys.”

Maneli Mirkhan, a French-Iranian adviser on international affairs, agreed that having a Wikipedia biography inspires confidence in her services. Her biography was called out by Wikipedia editors for reading like a résumé.

The biography of Andrew Wyant, a Phoenix-area executive who works in fitness and nutrition, was also deemed insufficiently notable, due to a lack of coverage from secondary sources. Wyant said he blamed himself for failing to “feed” his public persona through self-promotion. “What I know to be true is that I am largely responsible for the growth of the profession of personal trainers and fitness coaches by building the entire category over the last decade,” he told me. “I feel my accomplishments and impact are notable, but I don’t feel the need to promote them in media so I can have a Wikipedia page.”

Wyant’s biography was deleted. Songer’s biography was deleted. Mirkhan’s biography was deleted.

Of those, only Mirkahn’s reappeared, rewritten and with new sources, a few days later. I asked Mirkhan if she had been involved in re-creating the page. No, she said, but she wasn’t surprised. Mirkhan had been, and continues to be, sought out by the French media to comment on events in Iran, and more coverage means more demonstrations of notability. But there’s always that chicken-and-egg problem: Mirkhan thinks that what gave the media confidence in choosing her as a commentator was not just her career and her expertise. It was the fact that she was on Wikipedia.

Kiril Simeonovski did not lead with his suspicions that Martina Dimoska wrote her own Wikipedia biography. He argued, on Wikipedia’s public page for editorial debates, that the biography’s sourcing was weak, relying in part on posts from Dimoska’s LinkedIn page. It promoted Dimoska’s social media accounts, where she posted things like—and this Simeonovski found objectionable enough to cite in his argument for deletion—a picture comparing herself to NASA software engineer Margaret Hamilton. Published three separate times, it became one of her most popular posts, engendering sexist mockery and vicious defense.

To Simeonovski, self-promotion like this suggested that the case for Dimoska’s notability was regularly oversold by Dimoska herself. Though he could not prove a connection, it was difficult to imagine the Wikipedia biography as anything but an extension of that project.

That afternoon, a new Wikipedia user added comments to Simeonovski’s public arraignment, noting three existing articles about other individual analog astronauts. A fair contribution, tempered by a shot across the bow at Simeonovski: The user’s name was “Kiril’s mom.” The user was not Simeonovski’s mom, and the account was subsequently blocked. Hours later, another new user asked Simeonovski, rhetorically, “If I publish a blog post about someone and he finds it amazing and celebrates it does that mean that he/she created it?” Their username was a coded reference to the actual name of Simeonovski’s mother. They also got blocked.

Nine days later, another anonymous user—whose IP address, according to Simeonovski, placed them in Skopje, North Macedonia—said Dimoska’s biography had improved “considerably,” with new sourcing from publications like The Telegraph. The revised article, the user claimed, was superior to “85%” of Wikipedia articles about living Macedonians.

Three days later, Simeonovski dismantled each of the 32 new sources in turn. It wasn’t really the British newspaper The Telegraph that had been cited, for instance, but the Albanian news website Telegrafi. “I fail to find a single independent and reputable English-language source,” he later wrote.

The next day, the anonymous user rebutted Simeonovski point by point. They said it was “strange” that Simeonovski could not seem to find a single appropriate source for Dimoska’s biography but had found “10 sources from non-independent websites” when he wrote an article about the former governor of the National Bank of North Macedonia. The National Bank of North Macedonia, the user added to Simeonovski: Isn’t that where you work?

At one point, Simeonovski said he would report his detractor to the Wikimedia Foundation legal department.

Of Rosemary Bush, a professor at Northwestern University in Illinois, a Wikipedia editor recently wrote: “It will be many years until she qualifies as notable.” Bush herself agreed, to a point, that the Wikipedia page about her did not make a strong case, though she found the biography itself to be strange, possibly AI-generated, and not wholly representative of her career. “The world is fine without a Wikipedia page on me,” she said, but added, “I do think the comment proposing deletion is a bit rude.”

A little rudeness is unavoidable in this line of work. Sonia Jones, a Welsh singer best known for the theme song from the 1979 film Monty Python’s Life of Brian, is indeed best known for the theme song from Monty Python’s Life of Brian; therefore, Wikipedia editors decided that her Wikipedia biography should be deleted and henceforth redirect to the article about the song. Jones, when I asked her for a comment, did not agree that her achievements since 1979 do not merit documentation. “No, I do not want them to remove my page,” she said. “Why would they?”

“Canada has a national dodgeball team? Who would have thought?” wrote one editor in their case to delete the biography of Savannah Burton, the first transgender athlete to compete for Canada’s national dodgeball team. “Even though dodgeball is not considered a mainstream sport, competing at the highest level in any sport is a significant accomplishment,” Burton told me. “Given the ongoing attempts to erase transgender people, along with infringements on our rights and our ability to live normal lives, this action is not surprising.”

There is, when you look for it, a careless and cocky tone that can creep into the verdicts of Wikipedia editors who have been doing the job for a long time. “This guy is a nobody. Delete him.” “I’ve got more followers on Instagram than he does.” “All we have here are stand-up comedy, opinion columns, being a guest or collaborator on some non-notable podcasts … This is more or less every twentieth person who lives in NYC.”

Rosemary Bush’s biography was deleted. Sonia Jones’ biography was deleted. Savannah Burton’s biography was retained. “It was easy to find more sources,” wrote an editor who came to the page’s rescue. Editors’ participation in this process is not mandatory. Articles, once marked for death, can be deleted simply because nobody showed up to debate the matter. That anyone would volunteer their time to save an undeserving article from oblivion is a kindness: a good deed in a rude world.

Kiril Simeonovski never did refer the anonymous editor of Martina Dimoska’s biography to the Wikimedia Foundation’s legal team. Instead, he and his antagonist continued their debate on sourcing and semantics for days, and then weeks.

Per her Wikipedia page, Dimoska’s achievements included the command of two analog astronaut missions in Poland, but Simeonovski argued that while this information was verifiable, the missions themselves did not meet the threshold of notability and therefore neither did Dimoska. “You’ve apparently regressed to using children’s books to support the notability of the person we’re discussing,” said Simeonovski. “I expect [you] to continue gaslighting with multi-paragraph comments, but that won’t change the observation that the person isn’t notable [enough] for a Wikipedia article.”

“Count your words and then mine … and see who is the one gaslighting with multi-paragraph comments.”

“You seem to mix up ‘prominence’ and ‘notability.’”

“Your argument relies heavily on personal anecdotes and assumptions about how Macedonian media operates.”

Other editors with an eye on the debate pleaded for colleagues to lend their perspective. Most supported Simeonovski, but they didn’t linger long. “At this point the most visible thing about this individual seems to be [this] article,” one said, before bouncing.

She added: “Watching strangers debate your life, work, and credibility in such an intense way is definitely a strange experience to process.”

On April 25, a user named “DavidfromArizona” took to Wikipedia to announce that his friend Sheldon Marks—“the world’s leading vasectomy reversal expert,” per Marks’ own website—wished for his Wikipedia biography to be removed.

“It is inaccurate and does not truly represent his achievements,” David wrote. “[His] ego is hurt by having such a page. He either wants a detailed page or wants this one deleted.” A request from the subject of a Wikipedia article is not grounds for deletion in itself, although it can tip the balance if there is no clear consensus. On examination, Wikipedia editors found the biography lacking in sources that demonstrated notability.

On May 6, a Wikipedia user who 17 years earlier had created a biography for Sophie Aston, a British landscape artist, said that Aston had recently asked that the page be deleted. “She has retrained as a psychotherapist and wishes to forget her career as an artist.”

Having seen how much a Wikipedia page matters to certain people, I asked Marks and Aston why they wanted theirs removed.

“A kind and enthusiastic man made my Wiki page as a surprise for me about 17 years ago,” Aston told me. “We’d been on two dates. I found it a bit embarrassing but sweet. I didn’t see him again; I wasn’t into him. The info on the page was never [very] accurate and is now very out of date.” It was “ostentatious,” she added, for Wikipedia to present her as an artist of note, not least because for the past 10 years she has been a professional psychotherapist. The biography, she said, “makes it sound like I am an important artist, and I am not—it’s inappropriate.”

The Wikipedia editors could not treat Aston’s request with undue favor, but given the chance to cast fresh eyes over the article, they found her biography lacking. “The request to delete her article is not relevant,” one wrote bluntly, “but I don’t view her artistic career as meeting [notability] guidelines regardless of the request.” Aston’s biography was deleted.

Sheldon Marks didn’t respond to me. After receiving my email, however, he fled straight to the debate unfolding on Wikipedia. “I did not request deletion of this article,” he wrote. The statements made by DavidfromArizona, whom Marks did not know, were apparently fabrications. Marks wanted to keep his page. He admitted it could be improved but maintained that he was a notable individual: “I am the author of Vasectomy Reversal: Manual of Vasovasostomy and Vasoepididymostomy (Springer, 2018), translated into Chinese by Springer Nature in 2025; the surgical instrument known as the Marks Vas Cutting Forceps is named for me; I have served as Course Coordinator for the AUA post-graduate course on vasectomy reversal; and I have been a contributor to WebMD since 2005.”

The Wikipedia editors briefly conferred, then replied that they had already voted to delete.

There’s a final twist in the biography of Martina Dimoska: She knew Kiril Simeonovski—the guy trying to get her deleted.

“I had interacted with him on social media before and had crossed paths through mutual friends,” Dimoska said. “One recent example I remember clearly is seeing his post after he was injured by a cyclist who had left the scene.” Dimoska recalled the post in detail, though she had not responded or reacted to it at the time.

Simeonovski said he didn’t remember ever speaking with Dimoska in person but admitted the possibility of “virtual interaction.” Both are active on Facebook, and though they do not follow each other, Simeonovski said they have enough mutual friends in North Macedonia that their respective algorithms often put them in each other’s path. Just as Dimoska saw Simeonovski’s post about his accident, so Simeonovski came across Dimoska’s celebration of inclusion in Wikipedia, which led him to discover Dimoska’s earlier Instagram post comparing herself to Margaret Hamilton, then to a much older Reddit thread asking if Dimoska was the smartest Macedonian woman. (“Isn’t it just all hype about that girl?” one Redditor replied.) Simeonovski didn’t comment on any of those posts. But in Wikipedia he had found an opportunity to contest her notability on surer ground.

“It was somewhat surprising to me, perhaps because I have become accustomed to professional environments where people tend to be more openly supportive of one another’s work,” Dimoska told me. “At the same time, having grown up in North Macedonia, I am aware that recognition within a small community can sometimes generate more scrutiny than one might expect.”

One month after Simeonovski nominated Dimoska’s biography for deletion, he lost. The page was kept. “Given the bludgeoning, canvassing, and sockpuppetry evident here, this is a no consensus closure without prejudice against speedy renomination,” wrote an editor in a determination. “The arguments to delete here are stronger on the merits, but sufficient reasoned opposition exists that I do not see a consensus for deletion.” Obstinacy won the day, though Dimoska’s page now carries a disclaimer that it contains “promotional content.”

Wikipedia pages, for better or worse, matter to people. To be included is a validation of a life. If it were not so desirable, then one of the very first things Kiril Simeonovski did upon joining Wikipedia in 2008 would not have been to create a page about himself. Which he did do. He “wasn’t aware about the notability policies” back then, he told me.

The page was promptly deleted. “If your achievements, etc., are verifiable and genuinely notable and thus suitable for inclusion in Wikipedia,” an editor chided Simeonovski at the time, “someone else will probably create an article about you sooner or later.” To date, nobody has.

#4532. Hormone Therapy May Boost Weight Loss on Wegovy, But Not as Much on Zepbound

Females using menopause hormone therapy (MHT) alongside GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic or Wegovy may experience greater weight loss, according to a study presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society.

Weight gain is a common concern among middle-aged women. While much of this is associated with aging, hormonal changes during menopause can also contribute to changes in body fat and its distribution.

GLP-1 medications have been shown to be effective for weight loss. However, the results may vary by sex and reproductive stage.

For example, studies have found that women and adults under age 55 may experience greater weight loss with these medications.

A previous small observational study in postmenopausal women found that using MHT alongside semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, was associated with greater weight loss.

However, whether hormone therapy influences weight loss outcomes with different GLP-1 medications remains unclear.

To investigate this question, researchers compared weight loss in middle-aged women receiving either semaglutide or tirzepatide, with or without MHT. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The study included 3,855 women with an average age of 57 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 36 kg/m2.

The semaglutide cohort included 2,379 individuals, while the tirzepatide cohort included 1,476 individuals.

The average follow-up in the semaglutide cohort, both with and without MHT, was 26 months. The average follow-up in the tirzepatide group was 17 months for those using MHT and 16 months for those not using it.

The researchers found that in the semaglutide cohort, those using MHT alongside the GLP-1 medication had significantly greater weight loss at 9 months than those not using MHT.

These differences were sustained at 12 and 15 months and at the final follow-up. Those using MHT were also more likely to reach up to a 30% total body weight loss than those not using it.

In the tirzepatide cohort, those using MHT had a modestly greater weight loss at 3 months and were more likely to achieve more than 5% total body weight loss by the final follow-up. However, no significant difference in weight loss was observed at later time points.

These findings suggest that MHT use may be associated with greater weight loss among middle-aged women taking semaglutide. However, the researchers did not observe a sustained, statistically significant difference in weight loss among those taking tirzepatide.

Betul Hatipoglu, MD, Director of the Diabetes and Obesity Center at the University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center, who was not involved in the study, told Healthline that the findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that people should be careful not to interpret them as evidence that hormone therapy should be added to GLP-1 treatment specifically to enhance weight loss.

“This is an association, and the study provides an opportunity to discuss how menopause, aging, changes in body composition, and metabolic health can be present,” she said.

It is important to keep in mind that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved certain hormone therapy medications to treat menopause symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness. However, these medications are not FDA-approved for weight loss or obesity management.

The study authors noted that larger studies are needed to confirm their observations and to inform individualized strategies for middle-aged females.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB-GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who was not involved in the study, noted that weight gain is one of the most common concerns she hears from women during menopause. Beyond affecting self-esteem and quality of life, excess abdominal fat can increase the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

“Finding effective, sustainable ways to manage weight is important for women’s long-term health and healthy aging,” she said.

“I would like to see prospective, randomized studies examining GLP-1 medications with and without hormone therapy to determine whether hormone therapy directly improves weight loss and helps women maintain it over time. In the meantime, treatment decisions should be individualized and guided by a healthcare professional.”

Ross advised that the decision should be based on your individual symptoms, health history, and treatment goals.

“Hormone therapy can significantly improve disruptive menopausal symptoms, including hot flashes, sleep disturbances, and mood changes. By helping women feel better and more energetic, it may also make it easier to exercise and manage their weight,” she said.

Hatipoglu noted that GLP-1-based medications, on the other hand, are effective treatments for obesity and can improve important aspects of metabolic health. Their use should be based on an individual’s weight-related health risks, medical history, and treatment goals.

“For a woman who has both bothersome menopausal symptoms and obesity, it may be entirely appropriate to consider both treatments. But I would not recommend starting hormone therapy solely to improve the weight-loss response to a GLP-1 medication based on the evidence currently available,” she said.

“It is also important to remember that successful weight management during and after menopause is about more than the number on the scale. Preserving muscle mass, maintaining bone health, improving metabolic health, and supporting long-term physical function are equally important.”

You should speak with your healthcare professional about the risks and benefits of all treatment options.
