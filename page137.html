#633. Harry Kane claims men’s Ballon d’Or to end England’s 25-year wait for winner

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Alexia Putellas picks up women’s award for third time

England captain acclaimed in London ceremony

Harry Kane is the Ballon d’Or winner for 2026, the first Englishman to win football’s most prestigious individual trophy in 25 years.

Two hours into a lavishly excitable ceremony at the Palladium Theatre in London, Kane’s name emerged from the final envelope to wild cheers inside the auditorium. The women’s Ballon d’Or, awarded moments earlier, was won by Alexia Putellas of London City Lionesses, a third Ballon, five years on from her first in the colours of Barcelona.

In the men’s voting, Kane finished ahead of co-favourites Lamine Yamal of Barcelona and Kylian Mbappé of Real Madrid, an outcome that will perhaps cause the familiar consternation among the hierarchy of those clubs, not to mention the vast online fan army attached to the superstar players in Kane’s wake.

But Kane is also a fully deserving winner of football’s ultimate gong. The trophy, organised by magazine France Football (owned by L’Équipe Group), is awarded by a vote of journalists and administrators across the footballing super-scape. For Kane, it is reward for a year during which he has won the European Golden Boot, played a major role in Bayern Munich’s Bundesliga title, captained England to a World Cup semi-final, and above all shown himself to be a wonderfully humble superstar player.

“It was really tough this year, the incredible players I was up against,” Kane said as he received the trophy, going on to congratulate everyone else nominated for anything else, and thank pretty much everyone he has ever played with.

“I am almost speechless. Being here in London has made it extra special,” he added.

As a pure goalscorer, his numbers are astonishing this year, with 89 goals for club and country since the start of August 2025. At Bayern, Kane has also become a darling of the Bundesliga for his all-round game, creativity, intelligence and professionalism. Bayern paid a record €100m fee up front for Kane three years ago, a transfer that broke the club’s usual recruitment patterns and raised some concerns, given Kane was 30 at the time.

It is a measure of his contribution on and off the pitch that this outlay is now seen as entirely vindicated. As a Ballon d’Or winner, Kane now enters rarefied company, heir to a prize that revolved around the Messi-Ronaldo two-hander for so many years, and which promises not just a form of sporting ultimacy but a vast uplift in current and future commercial earnings.

More widely, the award is a coronational moment for Kane himself, 16 years into a professional career that has taken in loan spells at Leyton Orient and Millwall, running himself into the ground to make it in the Premier League, and now, for posterity, into the lineage of elite footballers.

Earlier this month, Kane equalled England’s all-time caps record, while nudging his international goal tally on to 91 in 125 appearances. Aged 33, he still has a home Euros two years from now in his sights as England’s captain and a final opportunity for a different kind of footballing immortality.

#2066. I am the Scottish juror on the Ballon d'Or panel and here's why I had to vote for nemesis Harry Kane

He still hasn’t been forgiven for what happened at Hampden. No Scotland fan can ever look at Harry Kane without thinking back to that injury-time equaliser in June of 2017. Leigh Griffiths, scorer of two glorious free-kicks on a day of footballing mayhem, would be justified in throwing darts at pictures of the England striker as part of his daily routine, even after all these years.

But quality is quality. And the Ballon d’Or is an award that recognises not just achievement and skill, but the ability to do the remarkable. Repeatedly.

Which is why, in an incredibly tight year absolutely bursting with worthy potential winners, my vote as Scotland’s juror went to a guy who contributed so much to the Auld Enemy coming very, very close to making the worst Tartan Army nightmares a horrible reality. And his efforts at the World Cup, scoring six times as England reached the semi-finals, are only part of the story.

No, the Ballon d’Or is not an award dished out to the guy who has bagged the most goals. In the easy years when all we ever had to do was choose between Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo, a lot of fans used to get very confused about the difference between football’s most prestigious individual honour and the variety of golden-boot prizes handed out to strikers.

The decision to vote for Kane wasn’t entirely about the numbers, as impressive as they are. I mean, 73 goals in 65 games is just jaw-dropping stuff.

As a qualified tactical analyst and Uefa-licensed coach, however, it’s impossible not to be impressed by everything else that Kane brings to the debate over who deserves what prize. Because, not content with being the best centre forward in the world, he’s virtually created a new and unique role for himself in a Bayern Munich team more than willing to accommodate his brilliance.

Still the best finisher in the game, regularly finding himself in scoring positions when it matters most and usually hitting the net, he does all of this while dropping deeper than some midfielders and building play through a variety of phases. Blessed with superb instincts, he’s found a way to bypass a lot of the defensive tactics that make life so hard for the modern centre forward.

By his own admission, Kane is not overly quick. Nor is he even in the top ten best dribblers in world football. If astute judges can marvel at the footballing IQ of midfielders who always know how to unlock a game, however, why are some reluctant to recognise the genius – yes, genius – of the Englishman?

As he puts it himself: “Until you really understand football, it’s so easy to say ‘Stay high, you’re going to score.’ But it doesn’t work like that.”

Now, amid all of the admiration for Kane, we must add a caveat. And admit that any one of three or four other candidates on the official short list would have been worthy winners.

Lamine Yamal is a glorious talent destined to dominate the game. An established game breaker and match winner for Barcelona, he was a big part of a Spain team who may already be establishing themselves as one of the best international sides of the modern era.

The problem for Spanish players seeking individual recognition is, ironically, that their team is so damned impressive. Half a dozen Spaniards made the short list, with Pedri and one or two others genuinely unlucky to miss out, creating the possibility of votes being split.

As isolated as jurors may be from the politics, it’s also impossible to forget that every ballot cast can contribute to some incredible reactions from grown-ups who should know better. Real Madrid, you may have noticed, made a return to Monday’s ceremony in London, having gone in a very regal huff over Vinicius Jr being pipped to the top prize by Rodri in 2024.

Now that the votes have been counted and trophies handed out, of course, all of the arguments can begin in earnest. Even when Messi was mesmerising the world in his very best years, CR7 fans used to mount semi-organised campaigns seeking the dismissal of any journalist who dared vote ‘against’ their man.

A good couple of decades into life as a juror, it’s safe to say that this has been one of the tougher years, with so many genuinely deserving contenders. Kylian Mbappe, Khvicha Kvaratskhelia, Rodri again big Erling Haaland … and that’s without counting the defenders – Dayot Upamecano, anyone? – who rarely get the love.

Sometimes it’s best just to go with the guy whose ability, vision and sheer audacity make you smile and shake your head in wonder. Even if, in this instance, he’s the same player who prompted so many of us to curse his very existence, persistence and predatory instincts on a day of high drama in Glasgow.

Far-right senator banned from leaving country as supreme court judge’s ruling cites money-laundering case

The far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro was banned from leaving Brazil and ordered to surrender his passports just hours after his dramatic loss in the presidential election to the leftist president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva.

The ruling was issued by Alexandre de Moraes, the same supreme court justice who oversaw the trial of the senator’s father, the former president Jair Bolsonaro, for attempting a coup.

Bolsonaro posted a video saying the decision was based on an interview his brother, Eduardo Bolsonaro, gave last Friday urging his brother to leave Brazil if he lost the election.

On Monday night, Bolsonaro’s party issued a statement acknowledging the election result, saying: “We recognise the presidential result proclaimed by the electoral court, but believe that the questions raised need to be properly clarified through a balanced and democratic political debate.”

Bolsonaro, like his father four years ago, has still not conceded defeat to Lula.

The court’s brief ruling regarding his passport only refers to a criminal case in which the federal police are investigating Flávio Bolsonaro over his possible involvement in a money-laundering scheme. The ruling orders him not to have any contact with his friend Willer Tomaz, a lawyer linked to betting companies that police suspect were being used to launder about £680m.

It also cancelled all of Bolsonaro’s passports, including the diplomatic one he holds as a congressman, and ordered him to hand them over within 24 hours.

Bolsonaro was the first to speak publicly about the matter, before Brazilian media were even aware of the judge’s decision.

In a video posted on social media, he said he had just left a visit to his father, who is under house arrest in Brasília, when “Lula’s federal police were at his door and in the middle of the street, and I was served with an order to hand over my passports and I am also prohibited from leaving the country”.

He added that, although his brother – who is in self-imposed exile in the US – told a US podcast on Friday that he favoured the senator leaving Brazil if he lost the election, “I never said that”.

When his Senate term ends early next year, Bolsonaro will be out of public office for the first time in 24 years, and therefore without the special immunity afforded to officeholders.

He is under investigation in another criminal case, too, over what happened to the £20m he was captured on tape asking a disgraced banker accused of corruption to provide for a biopic about his father.

The Folha de S Paulo newspaper reported that Flávio Bolsonaro had been persuaded to drop his plan to seek to annul the election, which he had announced after the result was declared on Sunday night.

Bolsonaro had claimed that the two-hour extension of voting – intended to allow voters who had been unable to access the mandatory free public transport to cast their ballots – had harmed the election, despite the fact that his own campaign had also argued for an extension.

According to the newspaper, allies and lawyers told him his argument was legally weak.

Bolsonaro had still not publicly reversed his demand for an annulment, and in the same video he said he would file another case to have Lula’s ticket disqualified over alleged abuse of economic power, a similar case to others his legal team brought during the campaign.

#4497. 2027 Mercedes-Benz C350 Leaves the ’27 C300 Behind

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

They’re both 2027 models, but only the C350 gets a comprehensive set of updates.

There’s a funny thing going on with the 2027 Mercedes-Benz C-class: There are two distinctly different flavors being bandied about. The 2027 C300 model is effectively the same as the 2026 example. Then there’s the 2027 C350, which has a raft of upgrades and enhancements. Confused? Hopefully you won’t be by the end of this.

First off, the 2027 C350 sports a new look with an enlarged and more formal stand-up grille festooned with myriad three-pointed stars. It’s flanked by substantial cheek pouches, and the new headlights are each comprised of a single large DRL that is unmistakably another Mercedes star. The same is true at the rear, where each taillight now contains a pair of prominent three-pointed stars you can’t miss at night or every time the brakes are applied.

But it goes much deeper than that, because the C350 uses a new M254 EVO version of the C300’s engine, which is called the M254. What’s the difference? Well, both are 2.0-liter four-cylinders that are rated at 255 horsepower and 295 pound-feet of torque, but the EVO displaces 1999 cubic centimeters while the M254 is slightly smaller at 1991 cc. The bigger difference is the EVO’s new electric supercharger, which helps keep the turbocharger spun up and at the ready when you crack open the throttle at lower engine speeds. It also has something the old engine does not: a 27-hp overboost feature that brings the total up to 282 horsepower for 20 seconds at a time. Mercedes’s 60-mph estimate of 5.8 seconds for the C350 lags what we recorded when testing a 2022 C300 4Matic, which was 5.3 seconds. The extra power alone should make the C350 quicker, so we’re betting on 5.1 seconds for the new car.

That sounds terrific, but it doesn’t amount to much when cruising along in Comfort mode while plying the back roads of Germany. Benz’s nine-speed gearbox still has to shuffle down the various cogs until it finds the right one, which kind of obscures the result. In Sport mode, it’s another matter, and the engine definitely feels more present and willing to play along. We didn’t get into overboost, but the engine still has the beans and the means to make things interesting, especially down low in the rev range.

Mercedes says the dampers have been retuned for greater agility, while maintaining “a high level of long-distance comfort.” That’s the trick, right? The suspension hasn’t undergone any major design changes, though: It’s still a multilink setup in front and rear. The 19-inch tire sizes (225/40R-19 front, 255/35R-19 rear) haven't changed either. The car we drove wore Pirelli P Zero PZ4 summer tires, so that’s a nod toward a modicum of performance. The difference between Comfort and Sport is clear, with the former allowing a touch of float in the chassis and the latter reining that in. The thing is, Germany’s smooth roads don’t have anything like America’s potholes, frost heaves, or freeway chop. We’ll have to wait until we get one on home soil to tell you more, but the baseline is solid enough.

Our car was equipped with rear-wheel steering, which is a new feature for the C-class in the European market but won’t be coming to America. It’s therefore hard to tell what to make of the steering. The car responded smartly and accurately to inputs from the helm, but it was a question of feel and feedback. In Comfort mode, it felt somewhat distant and didn’t much correspond to what the tires were experiencing, especially just off-center. Sport mode tightened things up some, but the steering still didn’t feel like it was transmitting the full story to the driver. Is it going to be better in a U.S.-spec car without rear-wheel steering? We’ll see.

At first glance, the interior of the C350 looks largely the same as before, but there are some significant differences. Gone are the much-hated capacitive-touch zones on the steering wheel, replaced by real roller and rocker switches for the volume and cruise control, respectively. By far the bigger deal is the AI-based virtual assistant, made possible by a switch to the latest Mercedes-Benz Operating System, or MB.OS. This also allows for OTA updates, conversational recommendations for audio tracks and navigation destinations, and an optional augmented-reality head-up display straight from the S-class. There’s an enhanced ambient lighting scheme made up of 64 separate colors divided into 10 “color worlds.” You can opt for an upgraded Burmester audio system that now features 15 speakers and supports Dolby Atmos.

And those star-themed headlights also now can be upgraded with adaptive high-beams that don’t need to transition to low beams for oncoming traffic, as the MB.OS-powered system can dim the small portion of the light that affects a specific oncoming driver.

The 2027 C350 that has all these upgrades will be sold alongside the 2027 C300 that doesn’t, but only for a short time, as Mercedes will phase out production of the C300 in early 2027. The C350 will surely cost more than the C300’s $51,500 base price, so we’ll estimate $53,000 as the opening bid for the C350. A $2000 premium now exists for the 4Matic all-wheel-drive system, and that’s likely to persist. Based on our initial drive time, we think the 2027 Mercedes-Benz C350 sedan has much to offer over the C300, and that makes it worth the wait.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With a modest set of changes for this update, the C-class remains so good it’s a little boring.

The C-class is arguably the most important car Mercedes-Benz makes. The model line is the mass-volume driver for the automaker and a common entry point for Benz newbies all around the world. That means the company can’t mess it up. Changes and upgrades are important but must be handled delicately. For 2027, the C-class formula is unchanged; it’s an approachable baby version of what Mercedes does best.

For 2027, Mercedes tweaked the C-class’s styling, updated the powertrain, and threw a bit more tech at the car. The U.S.-bound, non-AMG, gas-powered C-class is available as a C350 with or without 4Matic all-wheel drive, but as of right now, no other models have been announced. The C350 packs a new, mild-hybrid, 2.0-liter turbocharged four-cylinder engine that uses an electric supercharger to help fill torque gaps off the line. Mercedes says it’s also adjusted the adaptive damper suspension setup on the car for reduced body roll and extra sportiness, while maintaining comfort as a central pillar. Of course, there’s also a full-electric C-class heading to the U.S., but here, we’re just focusing on the internal-combustion car.

Our expert editors test every vehicle we review. Read more about how we test and review cars here.

Transmission: nine-speed automatic, rear-wheel drive

My test drive of the Euro-spec C300 primarily took place on German B-roads, bouncing from village to village. Which proved that, in short, this is a perfectly competent small sedan.

The 2.0-liter inline-four was quick enough to get me around slower traffic in passing zones and negotiate highway on-ramps without issue, but it’s far from potent. The average C-class buyer—presumably someone looking for a classy-yet-subtle small car—won’t be startled if they put their foot down, but when they need to get out of a hairy situation, its 255 hp will do just fine. I didn’t find the electric supercharger’s torque fill or the turbo’s temporary 27 hp of automatically engaged overboost to be noticeable at all, but perhaps that’s the point. This isn’t meant to be a sports car. There is adequate power when you need it, calm whenever else. Even in non-all-wheel-drive guise, it wouldn’t skirt tires on dry pavement.

The nine-speed automatic transmission remains a distinction in a segment where most rivals use the familiar ZF eight-speed auto or—in the case of the Audi A5—a seven-speed dual-clutch. This worked well under relaxed progress and certainly didn’t feel as if it was shifting too often. But in the switchable Sport mode I found it held on to lower ratios for longer than it needed to during part-throttle acceleration. Like it was trying a little too hard.

From a handling perspective, the C300 was perfectly comfortable and compliant on the road. I didn’t find any serious twisties in my short time with it, so I can’t speak much to its claimed reduction in body roll, but for general commuting purposes, I didn’t find anything to complain or brag about. Same goes for its brakes.

The interior of the C-class is largely unchanged in this update. That’s a good thing. There are screens, of course, but the gas-powered models don’t get the massive Mercedes Hyperscreen, which pretty much takes over the whole dashboard. Instead, here you get a 12.3-inch screen for the gauge cluster and an 11.9-inch infotainment display. Both were reasonably friendly to get accustomed to and navigate through.

The big update that Mercedes is touting here though is its new artificial intelligence integrations. The new C-class packs a virtual assistant that can use AI from ChatGPT, Microsoft Bing, and Google Gemini, which you can have conversations with and ask various questions. I’m not personally big on having conversations with my car, but if that’s your kind of thing, the new C-class is here for you.

With its exterior changes—lighted grille with Mercedes tri-stars there and in the lights on both ends—the new C-class is flashy enough to be cool but not too over the top to threaten its maturity.

Its optional 15-speaker, Dolby Atmos–equipped Burmester 3D Surround Sound System is also a great touch for the music lovers out there.

The 2027 C-class doesn’t stray from its formula. It’s the distilled, near-entry-level essence of the Mercedes-Benz brand. It looks like a baby S-class—which is to say, good—and has more technology than your average driver could fathom. If you’re a Mercedes loyalist, or you’re just looking to dip your toes into the world of owning (or leasing) a genuinely nice but not over-the-top sedan, you will be satisfied. But if you want something with performance focus front and center, wait for one of the AMG models to come down the pipeline. Word on the street is V-8 models are right around the corner.

This small set of changes for 2027 stands in contrast with the new G50-generation BMW 3-series and its radical Neue Klasse styling. Behind the different visual approach, the performance numbers for both cars are almost identical, with the BMW 330i having identical claimed power and torque peaks and a near-identical 0–60-mph time.

With just minor tweaks to the front end, Mercedes managed to improve the C-class’s looks significantly. I’m particularly a fan of its updated tri-star headlight design. It’s not gaudy or overly futuristic. It’s a nice, classy way of implementing the brand’s new design language and its core branding piece, while maintaining a mostly normal-looking headlight. Good work.

#3767. This Star Trek Actor's Canceled Sci-Fi Show Has A Terrifying Premise

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The ABC mystery-box sci-fi series "FlashForward," based on the 1999 novel by Robert J. Sawyer, was co-created by "Star Trek" luminary Brannon Braga (who briefly quit the franchise after "Star Trek: Voyager") and celebrated superhero scribe David S. Goyer, and, boy howdy, does it have an intriguing premise: On October 6, 2009, every single person on Earth mysteriously faints and stays unconscious for exactly two minutes and 17 seconds. When everyone comes to, they have all had visions of their own futures, specifically what they will be doing on April 29, 2010, about six months from the present. Too bad the short-lived show couldn't foresee its own fate.

John Cho (himself a "Star Trek" veteran who joined the property without even reading a script) and Joseph Fiennes star in "FlashForward" as Demetri Noh and Mark Benford, two partnered FBI Special Agents who are assigned to find out what caused the bizarre flash-forward. Half of the series is devoted to untangling the cosmic mystery, while the other half consists of all the personal drama that arise from the characters knowing what will happen to them in the near future. Special Agent Janis Hawk (Christine Woods), for instance, will have a vision of getting a pregnancy sonogram, despite her being single at the time of the blackout. Meanwhile, Dr. Olivia Benford (Sonya Walger) sees herself having a romantic evening with a man who is not her husband.

Most chillingly, Noh's flash-forward reveals nothing at all, making him suspect that he is destined to die within the next six months. As for Benford, he sees himself investigating the flash-forward event when men with guns burst into his office.

The setup for "FlashForward" is perfect for manufacturing personal drama, has some philosophical sci-fi intrigue (Can you change the future?), and contains an ineffable mystery to be solved. But in spite of its terrifying ideas and opportunities for mystery and drama, the show just didn't fare well enough ratings-wise to last beyond a single 22-episode season. In the end, it proved to be one of many "Lost" imitators that came and went in the wake of that ABC series' massive success.

Like "Lost," the mystery in "FlashForward" gets pretty convoluted pretty quickly. Dominic Monaghan and Jack Davenport co-star as Dr. Simon Campos and Dr. Lloyd Simcoe (respectively), two individuals who may have caused the blackout or possibly just enhanced its effects. In addition, at least one character is in possession of a high-tech ring that allowed them to stay awake during the blackout, and there is, perhaps predictably, a shadowy cabal of government agents who seek to control any future blackouts. Indeed, the FBI is searching for an enigmatic individual nicknamed Suspect Zero, as well as D. Gibbons or Dyson Frost, a figure who definitely has some kind of arcane knowledge that adds to the mysteries.

"FlashForward" is dripping with conspiracies, so it's difficult to talk about without giving away its many secrets. For me, though, the most interesting aspect of the series is its constant pondering of predetermination. Philosophically, "FlashForward" is all potential energy and, in a refreshing twist, not all of the flash-forwards are intriguing. For example, FBI Assistant Director Stanford Wedeck (Courtney B. Vance) merely sees himself merely sitting on a toilet reading a newspaper. It seems to me that his would be the more common experience.

"FlashForward," in addition to being a little too convoluted, was also overpopulated. Yes, it was a global series, and everyone on the planet was wrestling with the same questions of destiny, but the main cast included at least 10 characters, which was a lot of ground to cover. On top of that, the show snagged a huge roster of captivating guest stars, which only made things more complex. Pay attention, though, and you'll be impressed by visits from the likes of Michael Ealy, Gabrielle Union, Annabeth Gish, and the late, great Ricky Jay.

Structurally, the first and only season of "FlashForward" was presented in two parts. Part 1 ran from September to December 2009, while Part 2 aired from March to May 2010. The series cleverly butted up against its own future.

Critically, "FlashForward" received generally good reviews. Writing for Salon, Heather Havrilesky listed it as the best of that year's fall TV, comparing it favorably to shows like "Lost" and "Fringe." As she put it:

"You have to love a fall pilot that can incite paranoid thoughts within minutes. And who doesn't enjoy the eerie mood and the sense of mystery and doom built into this picture? No one has any idea what's going on. And in contrast to the limited scope of similar serial dramas, this phenomenon affects everyone on the planet."

That said, Havrilesy admitted that the "FlashForward" pilot is "wildly cheesy, cheesy in ways that only an aggressively slick, suspenseful heir to '24's' throne can be." Similarly, many critics loved the show's premise but tended to agree that its plotting is weak. At the very least, everything pays off when the series catches up to the forward flashes.
