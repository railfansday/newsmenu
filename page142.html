The saddest part of Mass Effect 5's death is the end of BioWare and the developers whose livelihoods are being upended. Yet it's difficult for fans not to think of the RPG that might have been, too. After an apparent trailer for Mass Effect 5 leaked, we now also have an idea of what the game's music might've sounded like thanks to legendary Journey composer Austin Wintory.

As it turns out, Wintory had been in discussions with the developers to score Mass Effect 5, and he's now shared a 20 minute long "elaborate production" of "early musical drafts" he'd envisioned for the game. He notes in the description of the YouTube video that he had "not yet learned any narrative details of what they were planning," so his compositions are a pure "exploration" of what the game might sound like, but there's a world where Mass Effect 5 would've featured these tracks or something close to them.

"Working on Mass Effect 5 was my career's biggest dream project, my white whale," Wintory explains. "It is my favorite franchise of all time, and for the last couple years I'd been in serious conversations with BioWare and EA to jump in."

The music itself is pure Mass Effect – a combination of otherworldly synths and traditional orchestral sounds that paint a picture of a galaxy full of mystery and danger. "My suggestion was to treat the original game's masterful Jack Wall / Sam Hulick score as a starting point, and to build on it from there with deeper use of physical, analog synths and a more tightly interwoven use of the orchestra," Wintory says.

There are eight tracks here, and yes, they include Wintory's fantastic take on the track for scanning the galaxy map. Throughout the video, bits of text explain his approach for each song, and they've all managed to make me even sadder that we'll never see this vision of the game come to life.

"Thank you BioWare, Casey Hudson, Jack Wall, Sam Hulick, and countless others for several decades of my most cherished gaming memories," Wintory concludes. "And thank you to those at BioWare and EA who recently emboldened me to dream of what my place in that universe might sound like."

In a follow-up Twitter post, Wintory notes that he "was NOT hired," and in fact, "no one was." He'd "prepared this music as a discussion item" for the aforementioned conversations with EA and BioWare, but while things weren't final, he recalls the response being a positive one.

"The response was extremely enthusiastic from all who heard it but MUCH still needed to happen before it was official."

As part of our ongoing coverage and commemoration of the closure of BioWare, we've been reaching out to RPG developers to hear what the studio meant to them. Lead SEO editor Lauren Morton got in touch with 16 devs who owe their careers to the legendary RPG studio, and today I can share the words of another, Skald: Against the Black Priory lead developer Anders Lauridsen.

Baldur’s Gate 1 was the first game I truly loved.

I’d played a bunch of CRPGs before but they were older, clunkier titles. Though I was deeply fascinated by the genre, I was also a little too young to grok some of those older titles. Meanwhile I had started dabbling in AD&D (second edition to be specific) and that blew my mind like nothing before it. Like all fledgling DMs, however, I very soon ran into the dreaded “scheduling conflicts” that are the bane of tabletop groups. I had been shown paradise and then had the keys snatched away. I was creatively and intellectually starved. Into this tween turmoil Baldur’s Gate 1 was delivered and I was saved.

I had never experienced anything like it and, to this day, I still think it’s the best of the Infinity Engine games (I still replay it yearly). To me, nothing has so perfectly captured that free-roaming, chaotic, murder-hobo style of play that was [Second Edition] AD&D to me in the late '90s. Halcyon days.

Since then, Bioware has been a constant companion to me and their games are without a doubt one of the biggest reasons I ended up building a career making CRPGs. There’s a lot of Bioware hidden in Skald and a large reason for that is that simply implementing and testing that content just triggers so many happy memories for me and gives me a feeling of fellowship with some of my biggest childhood heroes.

I choose not to be sad for the passing. There is a time for everything. Instead I chose to celebrate the amazing games Bioware has given us and I hope that newer generations keep getting the same joy and inspiration from them for many years to come.

Thank you for all the amazing adventures!

I had a similar experience with BioWare RPGs as a substitute for tabletop growing up, lacking friends who were interested in the hobby until well into adulthood, and I'll wager that's not an uncommon experience.

Hearing Lauridsen say there's a lot of BioWare DNA in Skald made me smile. The game's art recalls those clunkier RPGs the developer initially bounced off, but its mechanics and combat are Baldur's Gate and Fallout to the core. I also gotta say I feel less bad about my own lack of facility with earlier generations of CRPG after hearing that a developer like Lauridsen struggled with them at first.

His enthusiasm is a heartening reminder that BioWare's legacy is more durable than EA's quarterly profits or private equity predation: The things we love about those games have dispersed into RPGs all over the world, and Skald is a perfect example of that.

It has the tight, low-level focus of the original Baldur's Gate, but with a shocking inflection of cosmic horror, plus Larianesque reactive systems I never would have expected from a pixel art game⁠—Larian's own Swen Vincke is a fan of Skald himself. It's $15 on Steam and GOG (half-off at the latter at the time of writing), and I reviewed it on Steam Deck, where it runs like a dream.

#254. Why Anthropic’s IPO Prospectus Is Missing So Many Key Figures

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic filed its hotly anticipated IPO prospectus on Monday, spelling out parts of the company’s business, strategy, and risk profile ahead of its public debut. Though the filing, known as an S-1, is several hundred pages long, it has some notable omissions—namely, certain financial figures.

“Claude has experienced the fastest enterprise adoption of any technology in history, as demonstrated by revenue growing from $0 at commercial launch in the first quarter of 2023 to $_____ for the three months ended September 30, 2026,” reads the first of dozens of such examples.

It’s relatively common for companies to redact certain information in their S-1 filings, and Securities and Exchange Commission rules allow for a certain degree of leniency around the practice, particularly when it comes to information considered “confidential.”

Still, it’s helpful to know what, exactly, these companies decide to leave out. In Anthropic’s case, the missing details largely revolve around financials for its most recent quarter, which concluded at the end of September, and lease agreements tied to its compute investments. While experts expect many details to be disclosed before Anthropic goes public, their current absence may hint at the uncertainties of investing in artificial intelligence, as companies work to secure sufficient compute.

Anthropic did not respond to a request for comment.

Michael Dambra, an accounting and law professor at the University at Buffalo School of Management, tells WIRED that there are “practical reasons” for Anthropic’s third-quarter numbers to be absent: September 30 was only a few weeks ago, and “closing the books for a company of this size takes time.”

Companies can also be liable for misstatements in their registration statements, “so there is a strong incentive not to publish numbers before they have been fully vetted,” Dambra says. SEC rules require that “Anthropic would need to update the filing with third-quarter financials” if the company goes public any later than about mid-November, he says.

As for data center lease agreements, Erica Neuman, an accounting professor at Youngstown State University, says it’s “not unusual” for companies to redact confidential details from “contract exhibits,” which could include lease terms.

However, Neuman adds that it’s worth scrutinizing whether Anthropic’s redactions would prevent, or make it more difficult, for investors to analyze Anthropic’s financial obligations and whether they may impact the company’s cash flow.

Many of Anthropic’s financial obligations relate to compute, which is the “most significant operating cost in our business,” according to the S-1. It’s also a business risk for the company, and it’s laid out both in terms of undersupply and overinvestment. (Parts of the AI industry have been warning that chip constraints could slow the progress of AI.)

Anthropic’s prospectus puts more emphasis on the risks of overinvestment, stating that the company needs to make forward investments in compute resources that will seem “irresponsibly large” at first glance.

Chips, Anthropic explains, are approached as a “single, fungible pool from multiple strategic partners,” including Amazon, Google, Nvidia, and AMD. The company notes that it has entered into contracts “with an aggregate committed spend of more than $____, substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure.” Some of these contracts are set to be completed before December 2030, while others may extend through 2045.

Though the prospectus touches on Anthropic’s compute uncertainties due to factors like limited land and power resources, Patrick Moorhead, CEO and chief analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, believes investors are owed more detail.

“It’s about characterizing the odds and impact individually across each of the areas. Anthropic owes investors more information, such as a probability of these individual issues, characterizing the percentage chance and the degree of the impact,” he tells WIRED. “Not ‘could materially impact.’”

Anthropic does provide some eye-catching estimates in footnotes. Since December 31, 2025, it has started or amended “compute and infrastructure” leases with “future minimum payment obligations” worth approximately $417 billion in aggregate. Since June 30, it has added an additional $245 billion to its future payment obligations, all related to infrastructure agreements. It also expanded “certain existing commercial agreements” that it caveats “have not yet been finalized,” for an additional $125 billion to $150 billion.

Include “short term” agreements with “minimum non-cancelable payments,” Anthropic says, and that’s another $21 billion. In total, it will owe, at minimum, an estimated $808 billion if it follows through on all of this.

Ben Bajarin, chief executive and a principal analyst at Creative Strategies who tracks the chip industry, says that this is likely just the first draft of Anthropic’s prospectus, and he expects a lot of gaps to be filled in an updated version.

Despite how common S-1 redactions are, Dambra describes them as “controversial.” While companies may argue that they’re “protecting proprietary information,” Dambra says that skeptics accuse them of using redactions “opportunistically” in order to “avoid disclosing politically or socially sensitive information.”

However, Dambra argues that redacting information may come at a cost. He notes that companies with redactions in their S-1s were also more likely to “underprice” their shares at the initial public offering.

When companies redact information in their S-1, Dambra says, they typically understand that they’ve created an “information gap” between them and outside investors. Understanding that investors will treat this gap with “suspicion,” companies may feel inclined to “entice” them to participate in the IPO by setting a lower starting price.

It’s unclear what Anthropic’s opening price may end up being or how investors may be interpreting the redactions in the company’s S-1. But according to Bajarin, the whole AI industry is one form of “blank” or another when it comes to crunching the numbers based on access to chips and infrastructure right now. So, yes, it’s all a risk.

“All of this,” he says, “just shows how absurdly early we are in enterprise adoption of an AI market that will be more than $3 trillion yearly and likely growing.”

#5230. Can a Saturday Halloween Open House Actually Lure Buyers in an Uncertain Market?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This year, Halloween falls on a Saturday and if your home is on the market, a Halloween open house could work in your favor.

“Families are already out and about, and some buyers may be more willing to stop by an open house while they’re running errands or enjoying weekend activities,” says Danielle Andrews, real estate broker and owner at Realty ONE Group Next Generation in Tallahassee, FL.

The key is to time the open house strategically to lean into seasonal decor without distracting potential buyers or cluttering the space.

By doing so, you can take full advantage of Halloween foot traffic and give interested buyers a memorable experience without going overboard.

Ideally, you’d host your open house earlier in the day—between 11:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m.—before families start preparing for trick-or-treating and evening festivities.

When planning the event, embrace Halloween without allowing it to overshadow the home.

“You want buyers to remember the beautiful kitchen, spacious living room, or backyard, not the giant inflatable ghost in the front yard,” Andrews explains.

A few simple touches can go a long way. A tasteful wreath, some pumpkins near the entrance, and a fall arrangement in the kitchen or dining room are all good options. You can easily find them at farm stands, orchards, pumpkin patches, apple orchards, and cider mills.

Don’t forget to add a small bowl of individually wrapped candy to make the home feel warm and inviting.

Whatever you do, avoid excessive decorations, fake cobwebs, loud Halloween music, or anything that makes rooms feel smaller or distracts from the home’s features. Again, the focus should be on the property—not the holiday.

“With the days getting shorter, you want the home to feel bright and welcoming, especially if the open house extends into the late afternoon,” says Andrews.

And don’t forget the marketing. Promote the open house as early as possible so that people can plan ahead and coordinate their schedules around their Halloween activities. Social media could be a great tool for this.

“A little seasonal creativity on Facebook or Instagram, for example, can help your listing stand out,” Andrews adds.

At your Halloween open house, you may see a mix of serious homebuyers, curious neighbors, and families who happen to be in the area.

Families with children may appreciate a quick stop earlier in the day, while buyers without evening Halloween plans may have more flexibility to tour homes.

Eliza Dieterle, real estate salesperson at Douglas Elliman in Vermont and New York City, explains that she rarely sees trick-or-treaters during showings as that typically happens more in the evening.

“However, I do have a condo available for sale, and there may be a designated time for residents to trick-or-treat. It’s always a great idea to check if you’re in a similar situation and double up on your candy supply if needed,” adds Dieterle.

Halloween can also create an opportunity to introduce the property to neighbors who may know someone looking to move into the community.

“I believe neighbors can be some of your best unofficial marketing partners,” says Andrews.

Keep in mind that not everyone who walks through the door is actively shopping for a home.

“There may be more casual or this-is-convenient-so-I’m-going-to-stop-in types of buyers,” Andrews explains.

That’s why it’s important to engage visitors naturally, ask thoughtful questions, and distinguish between casual visitors and potential buyers without making anyone feel unwelcome.

“At the end of the day, a successful open house isn’t measured by how much candy you give away or how many people stop by. It’s measured by the quality of the conversations, the follow-up opportunities, and whether you move closer to getting that home sold,” adds Andrews.

While two byes may be needed to sell an 18-game season to the players, the league wouldn’t have added a second bye to a 16-game season. (Indeed, it didn’t add a second bye to a 17-game season.)

More importantly, and despite Rodgers’s claim, a full-league international week never was (and never will be) a realistic possibility.
