#348. The Hottest New Side Hustle Is Stripping Copper Wire and Posting About It

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A woman wearing blue overalls and a pink tank top sits on a bench in a workshop lined with tools. Holding a broken copper wire in one hand and a small mechanical tool in the other, she looks at the camera and smiles. “Let’s make money stripping …” she says, pausing for effect. “Wire!”

This play on words is common in a specific corner of TikTok where users document the process of stripping copper wire in order to sell it for extra cash. Most of these creators don't appear to collaborate or use a common hashtag, and they haven’t coined a new term ending in -maxxing. But many of their videos have a similar, visually striking setup: A complex appliance is broken down into bits, or an industrial wire is split like ropes of licorice, and in the end, what’s left is a bundle of glistening amber.

The participants are diverse: Some are hobbyists or electricians looking for extra income. There are also people who claim to be saving for a big purchase, and others who appear to be cash-strapped teenagers. They speak a wide range of languages, including English, French, Spanish, German, and Romanian. At least one has an Etsy shop for selling hand-cast copper bricks, also known as ingots, which are made by pouring molten metal into molds.

Selling scrap copper is nothing new. The metal is relatively scarce, a strong electrical conductor, and fully recyclable—qualities that have made it a particularly valuable scrap metal for centuries. But copper prices reached an all-time high this year, making it an opportune moment for scrappers. In September, according to International Monetary Fund data, copper was worth about $14,473 per metric ton, or about $7.23 per pound, more than double what it was worth in September 2020.

While there’s no reliable data on how often people sell scrap copper, rates of copper theft—often involving metal taken from vehicles, plumbing, building roofs—may offer some clues. Craig Depken II, chair and professor of economics at the University of North Carolina, Charlotte, who has studied the phenomenon, says it tends to increase under two conditions.

The first is when the price of copper is increasing, much like it is now. The second is when people’s level of “economic distress” is high enough to make them believe the potential monetary benefits of stealing copper outweigh the risks of getting caught. (It’s legal to sell lawfully obtained copper everywhere in the US except Minnesota, which requires a state-issued license.)

On TikTok, it’s easy to find copper-themed accounts with hundreds of thousands of followers. On TikTok Shop, beginners can even buy their own wire stripper—the small tool that eats a cord and separates copper from its exoskeleton. The most popular option has sold more than 20,000 units globally at the time of writing.

The final price of recycled copper depends on a few factors, including its quality, grade, and whether it’s still “insulated” in plastic or rubber. Insulated copper can go for a fraction of the price of stripped copper, so many resellers remove it themselves to earn more. But even with a wire stripper, the process can be tedious. For some, sharing content about it appears to be a way to make the repetitive task more bearable.

People have posted about hosting a wire-stripping “girls night,” “movie night” and even “date night.” In one video from August, the creator @Payciencu.u strips wire on a bed near a man in a red sweatshirt. “How down bad are you?” reads the text overlay. “Well I’m stripping wire just to hang out with him sooo.”

Other creators treat copper scrapping as a full-on side hustle or hobby, posting time-lapses, tutorials, and lists of the “worst cables to strip for copper.”

Benjamin Stickle, a criminal justice professor at Middle Tennessee State University and author of the 2017 book Metal Scrappers and Thieves, identified three main types of legal scrappers: those trying to make ends meet, workers selling scrap metal they obtained on the job, and people raising money for a charitable cause.

Electricians, who are plentiful among wire-stripping TikTokkers, neatly fall into the worker category. Other creators are harder to classify, especially if they’re also earning money from TikTok itself.

The creator @Courtney2291, who did not respond to WIRED's inquiries, began documenting her efforts to strip enough copper to save $20,000 for a three-bedroom home earlier this year. She sourced wire and old machinery from family, her scrap yard, and Facebook Marketplace, stripping it almost daily. On October 10, a little over six months after she started, she announced she had reached her fundraising goal.

However, most of the money didn’t come from selling the copper itself. According to a scrap yard receipt Courtney shared, she earned just $99 selling 30 pounds of copper stripped over six months. One TikTok video, meanwhile, generated $1,446 after racking up 6.8 million views, according to an earnings screenshot she displayed in another video.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, Courtney says she plans to keep posting. “Once the house is mine, and it’s all set up and ready to be lived in, then I’ll continue pretty much the same kind of content,” she said. Her new goal will be to pay off the loan on the two acres of land where her home will sit.

Other popular copper creators, including King of Copper and Queen of Copper take a different approach, emphasizing scale and spectacle over personal storytelling. Their videos show them buying crates and trunks with hundreds of pounds of old wire, and then, little by little, stripping every piece

Neither claims to be affiliated with the other, despite their similar names and videos, but they occasionally repost each other's content. Both have amassed hundreds of thousands of followers, though how much they earn from TikTok and YouTube is unclear.

King of Copper is currently more than 70 days into a self-described quest to obtain, strip, and sell 1 ton of copper. In early September, 44 days into his journey, he claimed to have collected 271 pounds.

In one recent video, King of Copper spent five hours stripping wire, melting the copper in a black furnace, and then pouring it into six-ingot molds, producing what he claimed was about $90 worth of metal. In another clip, he spent two hours meticulously dismantling a 110-pound air conditioner and extracted roughly 7 pounds of copper, which he valued at $47.

His videos are mesmerizing to watch, but it’s unknown how much he might earn from them, and King of Copper did not respond to inquiries from WIRED.

Another way copper accounts can earn revenue is by marketing wire strippers and other goods for sale on TikTok Shop. When viewers purchase products through links in their videos, creators can earn a commission on each sale. Courtney, for instance, has advertised her own wire stripper. But many of these salespeople, like the creator @Dragonslayer_Industries, don’t speak much about copper outside of their ads.

“If you’ve got any scrap wire laying around, you need this wire stripper right here,” Dragonslayer Industries says in a January video before feeding a bright green wire through a machine linked for purchase. “This thing is basically just gonna print money for you. Probably pay for itself in the first hour you use it.”

“It's crazy, I only got this because my neighbor was stripping wire by hand,” he said in a June comment on one of his videos. “I got the free sample and thought l'd do 2 videos and give it to him. I thought it was such a niche thing that they probably wouldn't sell. It's been my biggest seller since l started doing affiliate videos. I can count on 5-10 of these a day selling.”

In May, he claimed to have helped sell thousands of wire strippers. It’s unclear how much he’s earned, since TikTok Shop merchants set their own affiliate commission rates. Dragonslayer Industries did not respond to WIRED's inquiries, but in a recent video he noted that it's not in his “best interest” to devote much time to wire stripping since he makes “more money” from doing other things.

But human salespeople like him face plenty of competition from AI-generated ads on TikTok Shop. Even in the specialized market for wire strippers, there’s a huge number of videos featuring AI-generated imagery and voice-overs, some using remarkably similar scripts about making “eight grand a week” from selling scrap copper.

Online, copper scrapping can seem like a solitary pursuit. Offline, however, scrappers sometimes form the kinds of communities that are less apparent on TikTok. Stickle tells WIRED that scrappers he spoke to often worked in small teams, split their profits, and looked out for one another. People struggling to get by, especially those who were experiencing homelessness, sometimes received donated metal from their fellow scrappers.

Stickle adds that they also would lean on friends who had trucks for help transporting large hauls. “If they came across a large amount [of metal] that they couldn't just dolly back to a place,” Stickle says, “they had somebody they'd call.”

Despite his family ties to Ivory Coast, he has shown a clear preference for the France national team, a choice he justifies partly through his career path and the cultural environment in which he grew up.

Ivory Coast will have to do without one of the most promising attacking talents of his generation. At just 20, the Premier League forward, whom Bournemouth priced at more than £85m (€100m) in the last transfer window, expressed an unequivocal preference for France in an interview with DAZN.

‘I have always played for France’s youth teams. I was born in France, I started playing football in France. I cannot see myself going to a culture I do not know. The France national team is my goal,’ the striker told DAZN.

The answer may upset some Ivorians, but his choice is hardly surprising. Born and trained in France, Kroupi has built his entire international career in France’s youth ranks. His development also puts him alongside Yan Diomande and Ayyoub Bouaddi in the race for the 2026 Golden Boy award.

His father, Eli Kroupi, is from Sassandra, in the Gbokle region of Ivory Coast. He moved to France at a very young age and built his entire professional career in the country, winning only two caps for Ivory Coast between 2001 and 2005.

Even so, the Bournemouth forward’s talent would be a significant boost for the Ivory Coast national team. His goal is now clearly defined: to convince Zinedine Zidane to open the doors of the France senior team.

It is a stated sporting and personal choice, although his international future is not necessarily set in stone until he plays an official match for the France senior side that binds him to the country under Fifa rules.

#478. Chinese scientists discover “D-rex,” a giant fungus with roots stretching back 65 million years

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists exploring the forests of Yunnan, China, have discovered an extraordinary giant fungus that is challenging previous assumptions about how large certain fungi can grow. The newly identified species, Dianjunus rex, produces upright reproductive structures that can reach 50 cm (20 inches) in height and weigh as much as 2.2 kg (4.9 pounds). These are the largest known fruiting bodies among fungi belonging to the phylum Ascomycota.

"This remarkable species represents a new fungal family, Dianjunaceae, and provides a rare window into the evolution of extreme fungal body forms," said Dr Indunil C Senanayake, an associate professor at the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species of the Kunming Institute of Botany in China and corresponding author of the study, published in Frontiers in Fungal Biology.

"The discovery illustrates how much fungus biodiversity remains to be discovered in East Asian forests."

Researchers from the Kunming Institute of Botany encountered the unusual fungus while surveying several forest environments in Yunnan. Their fieldwork included temperate coniferous forests near Damaidi and Yipinglang, along with tropical rainforest near Nanbang.

The team spotted unusually large, whitish reproductive structures, known as 'stromata', growing from the woody roots of Chinese cork oak trees. These structures emerged from the rhizosphere, the region of soil surrounding plant roots, and appeared to belong to an unidentified member of the ascomycete fungi.

Ascomycetes make up the largest major group of fungi, with more than 64,000 recognized species. Unlike familiar mushrooms, these fungi do not produce true mushroom structures. Instead, they generate spores within tiny sac-like structures found inside or on the surfaces of their fruiting bodies.

To investigate the discovery, the scientists collected samples and preserved reference specimens in the Kunming Institute of Botany's collections. They also cultivated spores under laboratory conditions to examine the fungus's physical characteristics and sequenced its genome, including its protein-coding genes.

Meet D-Rex, a Record-Breaking Fungal Giant

The genetic analysis, together with a close examination of the fungus's physical features, revealed that the discovery represented much more than a previously unknown species. It was sufficiently different from known fungi to require the creation of a new genus and an entirely new family (Dianjunaceae) within the order Xylariales.

The researchers named the species Dianjunus rex, drawing inspiration from its impressive size and the ancient Dian kingdom. Its nickname, 'D-rex', reflects its exceptional stature among related fungi.

Its closest known relative is Graphostroma platystoma, a fungus that grows on dead hardwood throughout the Northern Hemisphere. That species is the sole representative of its own family, Graphostromataceae.

Members of the order Xylariales are known for producing a variety of specialized chemical compounds and enzymes capable of breaking down complex molecules in plants. Researchers believe similar enzymes may allow 'D-rex' to decompose dead wood and obtain nutrients from it.

A 65-Million-Year Connection to Dinosaur Extinction

One of the most intriguing findings concerns the ancient evolutionary origins of the newly identified fungal family.

Using a technique known as a 'molecular clock', the scientists compared the fungus's DNA with genomic information from hundreds of other fungi whose evolutionary histories have been established. This analysis allowed them to estimate when the Dianjunaceae family first appeared.

Their results suggest that the lineage originated approximately 65 million years ago, roughly one million years after the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction. That catastrophic event eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs along with countless other animal and plant species.

The timing raises the possibility that the enormous environmental changes following the extinction helped create favorable conditions for the ancestors of this unusual fungus.

"This timing raises an intriguing possibility. After the mass extinction, forests experienced dramatic changes, including the sudden availability of large amounts of dead plant material," said Senanayake. "For wood-associated decomposing fungi, such environmental disruption may have created new ecological opportunities."

Another mystery is how and why Dianjunus rex evolved such an extraordinary size.

Before its discovery, the tallest known ascomycete fruiting bodies measured approximately 20 to 30 cm. The newly identified fungus can reach 50 cm, making it substantially taller than the previous record holders.

One possible explanation involves how the fungus releases and spreads its spores. By extending high above the forest floor, its reproductive structures may be better positioned to catch moving air and distribute spores through dense vegetation.

However, growing such a large structure also requires considerable energy. The researchers suggest that this unusual evolutionary strategy would be advantageous only in environments where the benefits of greater spore dispersal outweigh the resources needed for growth.

"A fruiting body which towers above surrounding vegetation and debris could have better dispersal of spores by wind in dense forests. However, such a large structure requires substantial energy investment, suggesting that this strategy likely evolved only where the ecological benefits outweighed the costs," hypothesized Senanayake.

China's Forests May Harbor More Undiscovered Fungi

The discovery also highlights how much remains unknown about fungal diversity in China's subtropical forests.

Researchers believe the region is particularly well suited to the evolution of unusual fungal species because it contains a wide variety of habitats, has experienced relatively stable climatic conditions, and supports intricate relationships among living organisms.

Despite these favorable conditions, many fungi in the region have received little scientific attention. Remote and difficult terrain complicates fieldwork, while the seasonal appearance and often hidden nature of fungal reproductive structures make some species especially difficult to locate. Historically, research has also concentrated heavily on fungi with commercial or practical value.

The scientists expect that continued exploration of these forests will uncover additional species, potentially revealing more unexpected forms and evolutionary histories.

"This region combines exceptional habitat heterogeneity, climatic stability, and complex biotic interactions that promote fungal diversification. Historically, however, fungal diversity in these ecosystems has been underestimated due to logistical difficulties in field sampling, the seasonal and cryptic nature of fungal fruiting, and a historical emphasis on economically important fungi," said Senanayake.

All four started topped up to 100%, with warmed batteries, factory-recommended tire pressures, and climate control running. They followed the same highway loop along U.S. Route 64 in North Carolina at a GPS-verified 70 mph. None of the vehicles wore their most range-friendly wheels. The BMW’s optional 21-inch wheels reduce its stated EPA range from 434 to 399 miles. The BMW had by far the largest battery. The testers quoted approximately 112 kilowatt-hours usable for the U.S.-spec iX3 (more than the European version rated at 108.7 kWh), versus roughly 88 kWh for Rivian and an estimated 84–85 kWh for Tesla. All four entrants cost $60,000-plus, with the well-optioned BMW being the most expensive at approximately $70,000.

Its observed average consumption of 2.8 miles/kWh beat the Rivian’s, but its smaller energy reserve limited its range.

The R2 reached halfway at 109 miles and 10% at 195 miles. It eventually managed approximately 213 miles, using 86.8 kWh at roughly 2.45 miles/kWh. Cooler conditions and all-terrain tires hurt compared with a previous range test, where it managed about 253 corrected miles in warmer weather on road tires.

The Model Y L was impressively frugal. Despite its smaller battery and extra seating capacity, the Model Y L traveled 286 miles, using 81.1 kWh. Its displayed efficiency hovered around 3.5–3.6 miles/kWh, comfortably ahead of everyone else.

The BMW, meanwhile, still showed 42% remaining after 192 miles. It reached zero at approximately 333 miles, then continued until 339.7 miles before losing highway speed. It used 112.7 kWh at about 3 miles/kWh, finishing roughly 60% ahead of Rivian and 19% ahead of Tesla’s indicated result.

It was also the quickest to replenish range thanks to its 800-volt platform (the only one in the test). The depleted BMW immediately accepted 122 kW from the mobile charger, effectively everything the equipment could supply. The Tesla initially charged much more slowly after depletion. This wasn’t a true charging comparison, but BMW’s advantage went beyond how long it kept driving.

The BMW’s win here was decisive. On DesignArena, Canva Craft is in fourth place overall for image models, trailing only OpenAI’s ChatGPT Images and beating bigwigs like Google’s Nano Banana and Microsoft’s MAI-Image models. Canva also ranks highly for generating portraits and product-focused imagery, though it trails in the marketing subcategory, which is where a lot of people use Canva.

The ability to edit AI outputs is a priority for Canva, and it’s an important one for anyone who uses AI tools. Often, no matter how perfect your AI prompt is, an image or video generator will spit out a result that is almost, but not quite, right. Canva has been tackling this problem with tools like its Magic Layers, which let you break down an AI design into its most basic elements and adjust them, a technologically complex process that solves a big problem for AI enthusiasts. Canva Craft builds on that foundation and lets you edit results.

Creative AI tools are controversial, to put it mildly. Photographers, illustrators and artists have been calling out concerns for years about how AI is allegedly trained on their work in violation of copyright protections, used to create abusive material and being offered up to executives as a replacement for real artists. Creative companies from Adobe to Canva have maintained that AI is just a tool they want to offer their creators.

In an apt analogy of the current state of generative media, Eric Wittman, CEO of the popular photo editing and sharing platform VSCO, replied to Canva CEO Melanie Perkins’ LinkedIn post about Canva Craft and said that real photographers, not AI, will always rank No. 1 for portraits.

While executives argue over the best way to incorporate AI, photographers have to deal with AI headaches on every design platform, including VSCO. As AI models improve, it’s also forcing real creators to consistently verify their work as human-made, not AI-generated. But digital verification methods are sporadic, creating an even more complex web for creators to navigate.
