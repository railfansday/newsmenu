Tottenham Hotspur’s victory in the howling wind and rain was forged in a single destructive intervention. Astronomers have spotted a remarkable gamma-ray burst (GRB) that's a real Incredible Hulk compared to other cosmic explosions of the same type.

What makes this GRB so special is the fact that the central engine powering it remained active for around 27 days, flaring in X-rays following the initial blast. That's 20 days longer than the longest previous GRB on record.

This record-breaking burst — which is designated GRB 220706A and was spotted on July 6, 2022 — is even more exciting because scientists don't know what the central engine powering it actually is.

"In a nutshell, GRB 220706A is an extremely late X-ray flare. X-ray flaring is common following GRBs, but previously it has always come within the first few days after we detect the burst," study team member Benjamin Gompertz, of the University of Birmingham in England, told Space.com.

"The fact that we see flaring at around 27 days in the GRB’s rest frame tells us that there’s an ongoing active process, and that’s unprecedented in the known population," Gompertz added. "The previous record for delayed flaring was about six days after the GRB."

GRBs are some of the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. They have been linked to the collapse of a massive star, which also triggers a supernova and creates a black hole. This results in a long-duration GRB, while short-duration GRBs are thought to be triggered when two stellar remnants called neutron stars collide.

GRB 220706A is an example of an ultra-long GRB, a rare cosmic blast of gamma rays in which the engine powering the GRB stays active for longer. They are incredibly rare, with their cause still unknown.

"We don’t know what the central engine driving this outburst is, and that’s what makes this such a compelling event," Gompertz said. "The fact that we see a supernova tells us that a star has collapsed, which is the standard power source for GRBs, but it doesn’t immediately account for the extremely late flaring or unusually bright supernova. We can interpret that through previous events to some degree."

Gompertz added that another ultra-long GRB detected in 2011, GRB 111209A, was associated with an unusually bright supernova (SN2011k) that had clear evidence for a central engine. The team behind that research determined that GRB 111209A is powered by a magnetar, a neutron star with a particular powerful magnetic field. In this case, the magnetic field is rotating at around 1,000 times per second.

"Our supernova looks a lot like that one, so a magnetar might be present here. That ties in nicely with super-luminous supernovas, which are also thought to be magnetar-driven," Gompertz said. "Another possibility could be that the GRB jet and the supernova are colliding with shells of dense material that were ejected towards the end of the parent star’s life, as it became unstable and began ejecting its outer layers. This sort of event is also seen in the super-luminous supernova population."

The team isn't quite sure how the central engine of this ultra-long GRB differs from that of others and why that resulted in 27 days of flaring.

"One possibility is that it’s a much bigger star than we usually see producing GRBs," Gompertz said. "A bigger star would take longer to fall in on itself and so provide a longer-lived source of energy to power the jet."

GRB sources may be members of a stellar population of massive, compact stars called Wolf-Rayet stars. These are 10 times wider that the sun, with around 10 times the mass of our star. However, even the collapse of such a star can't explain GRB 220706A.

"To power GRB 220706A, we estimated we need a star more like 1,000 times the radius of the sun, which would require a population of stars known as blue supergiants," Gompertz said. "On the other hand, it may be the remnant that’s different. If this star produced a magnetar, it may be that 'normal' GRBs just collapse to black holes."

The team thinks that the big question with GRBs like this one is whether they are members of the normal GRB population or a different kind of event. Having this second event, in addition to GRB 111209A, with an unusually bright supernova potentially adds weight to the idea that these are different stars. But the problem is not yet solved.

"The incredibly late flares also strain our models for how jets are powered," Gompertz said. "It will be fascinating to see how future theoretical work can incorporate a power source from a collapsing star that can last for a month!"

There could be even longer-lasting GRBs out in the cosmos that we have thus far failed to detect. Gompertz explained why such detections are tricky.

"GRBs this long are hard to detect because most of the satellites we use to find them are in low Earth orbit, which means the target is periodically blocked every 90 minutes or so," he explained. "That's fine for your typical 10- to 100-second-long GRB but becomes a nuisance when tracking much longer events. Long-lasting GRBs also seem to be more active in the X-rays rather than the gamma rays."

The University of Birmingham researcher added that newly launched missions like the Einstein Probe, which is more sensitive in X-rays than gamma rays, may be the perfect instruments to find more ultra-long GRBs.

"With any luck, we’ll get a few more of these to chase so that we can start trying to understand the incredible power sources that drive them," Gompertz concluded.

A pre-peer-reviewed version of the team's research is featured on the repository site arXiv.

UnitedHealthcare expands AI companion Avery to 21M members" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Earlier this year, UnitedHealthcare unveiled Avery, a generative AI companion aimed at supporting care coordination for its members.

At the time, the tool was available to about 6.5 million of its members in employer plans and 160,000 people enrolled in Medicare Advantage. Now, the insurer is expanding access to 21 million of its members, growing the tool's reach at a faster clip than was initially expected.

To date, the company has found that Avery has resolved 90% of its member interactions, and in the remaining 10% of cases, can complete a handoff to a human advocate to offer member support.

"As we're seeing some of the statistics and some of the feedback that we're getting from users around the success we've had within the early phases of Avery, it's just making sense to open up that application into more and more of the population," Craig Kurtzweil, chief data and analytics officer at UnitedHealthcare, told Fierce Healthcare.

He said that a key piece of what makes Avery work is that it's built on "ground truth systems" that create a critical data foundation for the platform as it sits on top of the data available through UnitedHealthcare. With that data layer powering Avery's interactions, members get better answers, Kurtzweil said.

That accuracy is critical to helping members build trust with the tool and use it most effectively. Avery can provide key details on deductibles, out-of-pocket maximums, provider networks, pricing and claims processing, Kurtzweil said.

"Making this as accurate as possible is a big piece of gaining that trust with the consumers," he said.

Avery also fits into an ecosystem where UnitedHealthcare is making a push to put more information in its members' hands. Plan designs like Surest, which offer variable copayments, give members power to choose the providers and care that works best for them and their wallets.

With Avery, members can then have an easier time navigating the experience and understanding their options, Kurtzweil said.

The team is also building out additional functionality for Avery, including an option for it to call providers and schedule appointments on the member's behalf. Smoothing out that process can eliminate yet another friction point that can prevent people from seeking the care that they need.

Kurtzweil said that UnitedHealthcare also intends to continue building out access to Avery across its membership, with the intention to ultimately ensure that all of its enrollees can tap into the tool.

"More and more people will be impacted by this," he said, "where we're going to start to reduce that friction and make life easier for those consumers."

#3607. The Fitbit Edge Is Everything the Charge 6 Should Have Been

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve been using the new Fitbit Edge for a little over a week, and am happy to report that it’s quite good at its job. It shipped with more features than I expected (Google Maps directions were there from the jump) and corrects the missteps that Google made with the Fitbit Charge 6. After running the Fitbit Edge through its paces, here’s my breakdown of what works well, what doesn’t, and what I think of the device overall.

When I reviewed it in May 2025, I liked a lot about the Charge 6, but found it had a few serious flaws. Tightening the strap messed up its GPS readings (weird but true), and some of its features required a subscription (like the music controls that only worked with YouTube Music). Its promised side button wasn’t really a button, and didn’t even always work.

I’m happy to report that all of my biggest gripes have been fixed in the Fitbit Edge. Or to put it another way: the Edge is what the Charge should have been in the first place.

Accuracy tests are always one of my top priorities in testing, but honestly I wasn’t expecting much from this device. A Fitbit can still be successful in my eyes even if its heart rate sensor is only “good enough.” Nobody should expect lab-level precision from a Fitbit.

But I was pleasantly surprised by just how capable the Edge is in this regard. It sometimes takes a few minutes to lock on to a heart rate signal (as most optical devices do), but after it does, it is spot-on—I think it might be in the running alongside the Apple Watch and high-end Garmins as one of the better heart rate wearables on the market. The Edge’s accuracy is notably better than the Fitbit Air's, even though they seem to share the same sensor hardware.

Location accuracy is also quite good, only deviating slightly from a Garmin I tested alongside the Edge. It may not be the best out there, but it’s miles ahead of what the Charge 6 was able to do.

The Fitbit Edge has real music controls, unlike the Charge 6. I can play something in Spotify or in Castro (my favorite podcast app) on my phone, and then control the audio from my wrist—and you no longer need to use YouTube Music or have a subscription to get this feature.

When the Fitbit Air and the Google Health app first launched, it was possible to pair both a Fitbit and a Pixel Watch to the same phone, but not two Fitbits. That has finally changed: I was able to connect the Edge to my phone without losing my connection to the Air. It also works to swap seamlessly between the two Fitbits. For example, one night the Edge was low on battery, so I slept with the Air on, and then swapped back in the morning once the Edge was charged. The app happily showed my sleep data from the Air and my activity data from the Edge without my having to do mess around in the app.

As a nice bonus, the charger for the Fitbit Edge is the same as for the Fitbit Air. (The chargers for the Pixel Watch and for the Fitbit Charge 6 are not compatible with the Edge.)

The Fitbit Edge is a similar size as the old Charge 6, but with noticeably more screen real estate. The strap fits pretty much the same way as a Pixel watch band—that is to say, normally, not with the strange hang-off-the-wrist stiffness of the Charge 6.

The Fitbit Edge also has a physical button—not a haptic nub like the Charge 6, but an actual, physical button. And it even works (to return you to the home screen) nearly every time I press it. The Charge 6’s nub was not as reliable.

Overall, I’m quite happy with the Fitbit Edge. It’s much better than the Charge 6, and adds accuracy and a screen to the things I already liked about the Fitbit Air. But there are a few things I don’t totally love.

One is that there is no NFC chipset, which means no option to use Google Pay. I don’t mind using my phone or card at checkouts (and I always carry my phone), so this isn’t a big deal for me, but I know a lot of folks love a wrist-based payment, and that’s missing from the Fitbit Edge. (Google reportedly said most Fitbit users didn’t actually use Google Pay, so they removed the functionality—presumably to fit in the other components while keeping the device small.)

The other downside is, I hate to say it, the Google Health app. It’s OK, but I still find myself arguing with the AI when it assigns me workouts I never asked for, and I have to agree with the redditors who are commiserating about the lack of a “yesterday” view. (On the bright side, Google recently added a feature that lets you turn off the AI insights, which helps a lot.)

Overall, the Fitbit Edge is a great fitness tracker. It measures heart rate and running/walking mileage accurately; it can control music in a sensible way; and it looks good, feels good to wear, and works smoothly. The price also tracks: It’s definitely worth the $20 premium over the Charge 6, and it’s not quite double the price of the Air, which I think is fair. For anyone who doesn’t want a smartwatch or a running watch and will be satisfied with just “a Fitbit,” this one is pretty much perfect.

Are you focusmaxxing today? Knowledgemaxxing? Maybe fibermaxxing? Dictionary.com has chosen “maxxing,” which can seemingly be attached as a suffix to any other English word, as its Word of the Year for 2026.

Dictionary.com defines maxxing as “the practice of pursuing an activity to the fullest or optimizing a certain aspect of your life.” It’s not a lot different from older expressions such as taking it to the max, maxing out a credit card or feeling maxed out. Words like max, maximum and maximize have their roots in the Latin maximus — meaning “greatest” or “largest.”

But maxxing takes it to another level. The word has an additional x and can be tacked onto the back of seemingly any word. Dictionary.com said that two of the most common of these in 2026 have been looksmaxxing and tokenmaxxing.

Looksmaxxing — maximizing one’s physical appearance — was the most popular internet search term in 2026 for derivations of maxxing, according to Dictionary.com. Certain internet content creators popularized the term and have gained large followings with their sometimes extreme approaches to improving their looks.

Tokenmaxxing is another frequently searched term. In the world of AI, token refers to a small bit of written text that AI language models process to formulate responses. The term tokenmaxxing generally refers to using AI as much as possible at work, as companies integrate AI more and more into workflows. It’s no joke: Some companies are tracking and evaluating their workers on how much and how effectively they use AI in their jobs.

Maxxing can also be used on its own, such as, “Have you been maxxing on anything lately?”

Dictionary.com said the popularity of “maxxing” in all its various uses indicates how much we value optimization, as does the rising use of prefixes super- and ultra- in 2025.

“Whatever we’re doing, we want to push it further,” Dictionary.com reports. “But its expanding uses also reveal how people are responding to that impulse. Some embrace it. Some exaggerate it for laughs. Others flip it on its head to describe stepping away from the relentless pursuit of more. The open question is whether this need for optimization has peaked or if we’ll push even harder next year.”

If you let your imagination fly, you can maxx on anything — dietmaxxing to lose weight, bookmaxxing to read more, bingemaxxing for all that streaming.

To identify its word of the year, Dictionary.com lexicographers analyze social media trends, news headlines, search term popularity and other data. The site, owned by educational platform IXL Learning, has chosen a word of the year since 2010, with “change” as the inaugural winner. Since then, words of the year have been tergiversate, bluster, privacy, exposure, identity, xenophobia, complicit, misinformation, existential, pandemic, allyship, woman, hallucinate, demure and 67. (Yes, we had to look up that first one. Tergiversate means to repeatedly change one’s attitude or opinions, or to equivocate.)

Dictionary.com’s shortlist of top words this year included affordability, data center (spelled as two words), odyssey and unc. Affordability has been one of the most common words in politics in the lead-up to the midterms — can people pay for what they need? Data centers are sprouting up all over the US and are either gateways to economic prosperity or harbingers of doom, depending on whom you talk to.

Unc, short for uncle, refers to someone who’s out of touch with Gen Z and Gen Alpha culture; and odyssey cracked the top five thanks to the popularity of The Odyssey, Christopher Nolan’s 2026 film adapted from Homer’s Greek epic about Odysseus’ journey home after the Trojan War.

#4687. This 83-Year-Old Has Finished Every 5-Borough NYC Marathon. He Has No Plans to Stop Running.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Dave Obelkevich will run his 50th consecutive race this year. He shares how to go the distance.

DAVE OBELKEVICH DIDN’T grow up a runner. He was more a music kid, who did chorus, orchestra, and band. But on a whim for his senior year of high school in Johnson City, NY, Obelkevich joined the track club. A few years before, he had completed a mile in under 6 minutes while his brother and best friend finished around the 8-minute mark, so he thought he might do well. He was humbled immediately.

“The coach would say, ‘Okay Dave, you start running around the track. We'll give you a 10-second head start,” Obelkevich tells MH. “They always caught me by the end of the loop.” He never finished dead last, though. Second to last, but never last place.

Obelkevich eventually moved to New York City to attend Columbia University in 1961. But it wasn’t until almost a decade later that he took his first steps to building one of the most notable reputations in the city’s running community. At 83, he’s the only person to participate in and finish every NYC Marathon since the course expanded to all five boroughs in 1976. (Before 1976, the NYC Marathon was four large laps around Central Park). This year, Obelkevich is taking on his 50th consecutive race.

He’ll lace up with custom-made orthotics in his Hoka shoes and an XL lucky shirt his friend Tucker Anderson made for him 15 years ago. The shirt has his name on top followed by big block text: FINISHER EVERY NYC MARATHON. The next line reads “1976-???” “I'll wear a long-sleeve shirt over it so I can take the long-sleeve shirt off,” says Obelkevich. “Unless it's really frigid, I'll run in shorts. If it's below 40, I may have to have my legs covered. But even as I get older and have less fat, it's easier to run with shorts on.” Obelkevich also likes to keep his hands warm and carries gloves with him just in case.

OBELKEVICH’S METICULOUS PREPARATION for the NYC Marathon is a stark contrast to his first race where he literally wandered on the course. In 1973, Obelkevich was in Central Park and noticed people running with bibs. It was a strange sight to see back then. Running wasn’t as popular as it is today and it was rare to find someone running on a weekday. “Nowadays, whether it’s two in the morning or two in the afternoon, there are always people running in Central Park,” he says.

Dave, along with roughly 17 spectators in the park, stood at 100th Street and Park West Drive, where he watched Tom Fleming (who later won the 1973 marathon) speed down the hill as leader of the pack. He remembers telling himself there was no way he could keep with that guy, even for one loop. But once the 75th person came around, Dave thought maybe it was possible. “Without having a number, it was very low key. Five guys passed me while I was in that loop for 43 minutes and I thought, ‘wow, that’s not bad.’”

The following year, Dave formally signed up for the marathon. His motivation was to see if he could do it and to honor his father, who had passed away earlier that year. Dave ran at 10 minutes a mile, racing down hills with a 16-year-old girl. “In the first three years of the New York City Marathon—1970, ‘71, and ‘72—a total of 12 women entered, and a total of five finished,” says Obelkevich. In 2025, he estimates about 19,000 women finished. “It’s a big difference in who has been running since 1970,” he says. He also recalls running with mayoral candidate George Spitz, who later came up with the idea to expand the race to the five boroughs.

His third year racing the NYC Marathon, 1975, was memorable because it was the only time he did not finish. About 10 to 15 miles in, Obelkevich felt dizzy. He lay on the grass for a few minutes and resumed the race, alternating between walking and running only to feel dizzy again. With roughly four to five miles left to go, Obelkevich admits he gave up because he didn’t think he would run faster than he did the year before. “Why should I finish? Fred, the race director, came by with his little Fiat and I said ‘Hey, Fred give me a ride,’” Obelkevich recalls. Fred told him to hop in the back and he’d drive him to the finish line. With a laugh, he says “It’s his fault I didn’t finish. He should have said ‘Hell no, Dave. You finish by walking.’”

As participation grew and the marathon expanded to the five boroughs, Obelkevich joined the Millrose Athletic Association to train—but mostly to meet up with runners he befriended in races. “I had so much fun making friends during races. I would carry a couple of business cards with my email address on them,” he says. “Some people never called me back, but a lot did.” Running became Obelkevich’s outlet for exercising, making friends, and even meeting his wife, Lynn, in the 1980s through the running club.

AS OBELKEVICH GETS older, his streak is actually what keeps him going. He only learned that he qualified as a marathon streaker in 2005, when New York Road Runners called to congratulate him for having 30 finishes in the NYC Marathon. At the time, he and his friend Tucker Anderson tied for the most consecutive races finished. “I got the seed planted that I’m on a streak,” Obelkevich says. The reminder of his streak is what has pushed him to persevere through the difficult moments, like when he had to start walking more of the hilly portions of the course or when he finally accepted that he could no longer run a marathon in three hours.

By his late 60s, Obelkevich’s mindset shifted toward completion rather than meeting a certain standard. “My goal now is to finish. I don’t care what my time is,” Obelkevich says. “Some use a stopwatch. I use a calendar. I just want to finish.”

New York might be his streak, but it’s also far from his only race. At 83, Obelkevich has completed 115 marathons—from Boston to Melbourne and London to Tokyo. And like his shirt says, he foresees no end date of when he’ll officially call it quits. He knows any one of these races could be his last. But he carries the words of his late friend Toshiko D'Elia (the first woman in the world over 50 to break three hours in a marathon) to heart: “I should keep running marathons until I can't. Don't decide that I'm going to stop here. There'll be a time when I can't run it, and that'll be when I stop.”

If Obelkevich could give one piece of advice on how to be a lifelong runner, he says to run with other people. He’s experienced the difference between running alone, where he tends to move at a slower pace, and running in a group. Once, Obelkevich wanted to break four hours in the NYC marathon. So he donned South African shorts that gave him luck during the Comrades Marathon (one of the oldest ultramarathons held in the KwaZulu-Natal province of South Africa). A stranger noticed his shorts and struck up a conversation. They ran together for an hour before Obelkevich finally checked his watch and saw the time flew by.

“If someone's going roughly your speed, start to chat with them,” he says. “Even if someone looks like they're very self-contained and focused, often they would love to have someone to chat with. It's a great way to meet people.”
