#2875. NFL trade deadline: Four Eagles, two more Colts and two starting QBs included among top 25 candidates

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

The NFL trade deadline is exactly two weeks away. That means the hot stove will heat up considerably fairly soon. We've already seen multiple high-profile deals this season, with Joey Porter Jr. heading to the Cowboys, Marvin Harrison Jr. heading to the Texans and Quenton Nelson heading to the Seahawks, and we could see several more over the next 14 days, given how wide open the league appears to be at the moment.

With that in mind, we wanted to take a look at some of the top targets who could be on the move. We ranked the top 10 situations that bear the most watching, but with a twist. Some of them are team-wide, meaning there are multiple targets that interested teams could pursue over the next two weeks. There are 25 targets in all.

So without further ado, here's the list...

Saquon Barkley's contract, with all the various option bonuses included, makes dealing with him somewhat financially complicated, but given the Eagles' struggles, his age, and the fact that teams are looking for explosiveness at running back these days, he makes sense as a target for needy teams. His current injury situation muddies the picture a bit, but that could be cleared up in the coming days and weeks as he gets further away from the hamstring pull.

Moro Ojomo is in the final year of his rookie contract. The Eagles just paid big money to two defensive tackles (Jalen Carter and Jordan Davis), so they probably can't afford to keep Ojomo around. He's 25 years old and has shown production upside with 6 sacks last season.

If the Eagles want to get aggressive, they can consider dealing both him and Nolan Smith, whose fifth-year option they picked up for next season but who they probably can't afford to keep in the longer term. Smith is more of a force in the run game than as a pass rusher, but that could still be valuable for teams that need help on the edge.

Dallas Goedert, meanwhile, signed a renegotiated contract with the Eagles this past offseason and is carrying only a $1.3 million base salary. He's not the most dynamic tight end these days, but he's a reliable pass-catcher and blocker, and any team that needs an additional tight end (teams love multi-tight end sets these days) could get interested for a small price.

Nelson is already on the move, traded to the Seahawks for a pair of draft picks. Jonathan Taylor is one of the best running backs in the NFL and has been able to remain productive even while the Colts' offense has fallen apart around him. (He's only at 4.2 yards per carry, but he's carrying a 55% rushing success rate, per Pro-Football-Reference.) He's the type of explosive back who can change an offense in the way Kenneth Walker III did for the Chiefs. The contract isn't prohibitive for trade purposes, as the Colts would incur only $10 million in dead money next year if they move him.

DeForest Buckner has been getting increasingly vocal about his frustrations with everything that's happening in Indianapolis, and it's hard to blame him, given how things have gone both this year and since he arrived there via trade. Like Nelson, he's in the final year of his contract, and the Colts would do well to see if they can get anything in return before he leaves in free agency.

It would be pretty surprising if Jeffery Simmons were dealt away, considering he recently signed a three-year, $105.8 million contract extension that made him the highest-paid defensive tackle in the NFL. But the Titans are going nowhere fast, and Simmons is by far their best asset if they want to kick-start yet another rebuild, which seems to be in the offing. Tennessee would take on a bunch of dead money by dealing him, but it might be worth it if they can get as much draft capital as he's worth.

Amani Hooker is a very good, very versatile safety (he's played 232 snaps in the box, 127 as a deep safety and 56 as a slot corner so far this year), and those are valuable around the NFL. He still has three years left on his contract, including 2026, so there is a team-control aspect that would be valuable to a potentially acquiring team.

Tony Pollard is in the final year of his deal with only a $6.7 million base salary. He's 29 years old, but if you look under the hood, the numbers show he's actually running pretty well so far this season. He's 10th in yards after contact per attempt among the 39 running backs with 50-plus carries, for example, according to Tru Media. He's also 12th in explosive run rate. He can still play, and help a team in need of a committee back.

Calvin Ridley has completely fallen out of favor in Tennessee and could potentially help a team in need of depth-chart wide receiver help.

Khalil Mack is on a one-year deal with an $8 million base salary. He's got three sacks in six games and has played 71% of the Chargers' defensive snaps. He's not going to be the player he once was, but any team in need of a rotational pass rusher should give the Chargers a call to see if they can get him for a late pick.

Tuli Tuipulotu only has two sacks in the Chargers' six games, but a deeper look at the numbers reveals a player playing better than that. He's 23rd in pressure rate among 148 players with 100 or more pass-rush snaps, according to Tru Media, and 18th in havoc play rate. He's still just 24 years old and you can get out ahead of his free agency by acquiring him now.

Derwin James, like Jeffery Simmons, recently signed a contract extension, so it would be somewhat surprising to see him get dealt. But he's 31 years old, the Chargers are terrible, and he's one of their most valuable assets. His base salaries for the next three years are also pretty high ($17.5 million, $24.6 million and $21.5 million), so the return might not be as much as the Chargers would be looking for.

Johnston only has 8 catches for 165 scoreless yards this season, and he's seemingly been surpassed in the target pecking order. He also has an $18 million salary for next season after the Chargers picked up his fifth-year option. So the return is likely to be low, but it's hard to imagine that L.A. wants to keep him around.

Budda Baker has base salaries of $14.4 million and $16.1 million this year and next, but he's a valuable enough player to make those price tags worth it. He's extremely versatile and can play both the run and the pass, whether lined up deep or in the box. He's 30 years old and still likely has several quality seasons left under his belt. The Cardinals aren't going to contend for the postseason, and Baker is their best asset for future value.

Josh Sweat is having a down year, both sack-wise (2 in 7 games) and in terms of his pressure rate (7.9%). And his contract doesn't exactly make that type of production attractive. But his track record over the long term, up to and including last year, is much more impressive, and teams could bet that he has a bounce-back in a more favorable environment. He probably wants a new contract, but that could be worked out once you see him play for your team.

Jacoby Brissett is clearly not the long-term answer under center for the Cardinals, and they might turn to Carson Beck sooner rather than later so they can see what they have in the third-round pick before making a decision on whether to select a quarterback in next year's draft. That makes Brissett expendable, and some team looking for a quality backup or fill-in for an injured starter could be interested in his services.

Win over the Jets notwithstanding, the Dolphins look like arguably the worst team in the league this season. Zach Sieler, though, remains a quality player. He hasn't been at his best this season (nobody on the Dolphins has), but we've seen high upside as both a run defender and a pass rusher from the defensive interior. If you can tap back into that, his base salaries over the next few years are very affordable ($44.5 million from 2027 through 2029, none of which is guaranteed).

Jordyn Brooks, like Jeffery Simmons and Derwin James, recently signed an extension. But his deal actually made him easier to trade and gave the Dolphins the benefit of selling to an acquiring team, knowing they'd have Brooks under team control for multiple years. With the emergence of rookie Jacob Rodriguez and Brooks having recently turned 29 years old, they could look to deal him for a pretty sizable return.

Brian Thomas Jr. has pretty clearly fallen out of favor in Jacksonville. In the Jaguars' last game, he didn't play a single second-half snap. Rookie Josh Cameron is playing ahead of him, and now it seems that two-way star Travis Hunter might be as well. But Thomas has more of a track record of high-level production than did Marvin Harrison Jr., and MHJ fetched a second-round pick via trade. If the Jaguars could get that type of return, they should be very interested, and teams should be interested in acquiring him as well, considering what he did as a rookie (albeit maybe not for quite that high a price, considering what he's done this year and last).

The Commanders are going nowhere and Daron Payne is in the final year of his contract. His $16.4 million base salary is high but not prohibitive to a trade. He's still a quality run defender, even if he doesn't give you that much in the way of pass-rush juice these days. The Commies would be wise to get something for him before he leaves in free agency next offseason.

Will McDonald IV has seen his snap rate cut from 66% down to 46% this season after the arrival of David Bailey in the draft. But he had 10.5 and 8 sacks over the last two seasons, and has shown he can be a productive pass rusher. The Jets just lost to the aforementioned Dolphins, so it's not like they're really going to compete for anything this year. McDonald had his fifth-year option picked up for next season, but he's still affordable.

Klint Kubiak is already getting questions about when he's going to turn the reins over to Fernando Mendoza, which means Kirk Cousins' time under center is likely coming to an end fairly soon. Like Brissett, he could be valuable to a team looking for either a quality backup or a fill-in starter for the rest of the season. The Raiders might prefer to just keep him around and have him mentor Mendoza for the rest of the year, though.

Jauan Jennings has so far been entirely uninvolved in the Vikings' offense. It would be surprising to see the league's last remanining unbeaten team make a trade where the give away an asset instead of acquire one, but given Jennings' lack of role and the number of teams in need of wide receiver help, this is certainly a situation worth monitoring.

Palworld developer Pocketpair may be in the midst of a legal battle with Nintendo and The Pokemon Company, but that's apparently not putting it off from the idea of bringing its hit survival game to Switch 2.

Pokemon and Nintendo filed a patent infringement lawsuit against Pocketpair over two years ago now, but even last year, prior to the Switch 2's launch, Pocketpair communications lead and publishing manager John "Bucky" Buckley said that porting Palworld to the platform was "100% worth considering" as long as the console was "beefy enough" to handle it.

He's clearly not just saying that, either. After all, Pocketpair launched another of its games, the roguelike tower defense and card game title Overdungeon, on Switch at the start of last year, so the lawsuit isn't making it avoid Nintendo's platforms entirely.

Of course, the situation may be a tad more complicated when it comes to the idea of porting the very game at the center of the ongoing lawsuit, but Pocketpair and its boss at the very least aren't personally opposed, despite the circumstances. Perhaps when the lawsuit has been resolved – one way or another – we could finally see Palworld on the platform. After all, it's certainly "beefy" enough to support the likes of Cyberpunk 2077, the Final Fantasy 7 Remake trilogy, and Elden Ring.

GamesIndustryWarhammer 40,000: Boltgun developer Auroch Digital appears to be headed toward significant layoffs. In a statement posted to LinkedIn, since deleted but reported by GamesIndustry and others, the studio said that despite its "creative success, the same market conditions that have challenged so many of our fellow developers have now also affected us." Assuming there's not been some massive miscommunication here, the cuts come despite a good run for Auroch Digital over the past few years. The seeming bad news also comes less than a year after Auroch Digital was acquired by Roundtable Interactive in a deal backed by EMK Capital.

Marvel’s latest Disney Plus series is a reminder of how much a lot of gen AI hype is just science fiction.﻿

Marvel’s latest Disney Plus series is a reminder of how much a lot of gen AI hype is just science fiction.﻿

In Marvel’s VisionQuest, a superpowered robot tries to better understand humanity by getting lost in elaborate, digital daydreams in which he and other artificial intelligence-powered beings live together in a home. The series is deeply fantastical, yet it plays with some of the ideas that have come to dominate the conversation about real-world generative artificial intelligence, exploring whether we should look at thinking machines as people and if AI truly poses the kind of existential danger that should keep us up at night. It’s also a reminder how aggressively Silicon Valley has used the trappings of science fiction to sell us on its AI products.

VisionQuest picks up some time after Agatha All Along and follows synthezoid Vision (Paul Bettany) on a journey of self-discovery. This Vision can recall the details of his death in Avengers: Infinity War and how WandaVision brought a version of him back, but he thinks of himself as a fundamentally different person who doesn’t quite connect with all of the memories in his databanks. Though Vision would prefer living in solitude, each of the other AIs living in his mind encourages him to experience the real world. Because J.A.R.V.I.S. (James D’Arcy), F.R.I.D.A.Y. (Orla Brady), E.D.I.T.H. (Emily Hampshire), D.U.M.-E. (Henry Lewis), U. (Jonathan Sayer), and Ultron (James Spader) all were created by Tony Stark, they consider Vision to be part of their family.

In VisionQuest, there is no doubt that each of the AI characters is a sentient being motivated by desires that aren’t wholly rooted in their core programming. In the show, genuine AGI is easily believable because it takes place in a reality where time travel, witchcraft, and Infinity Stones also exist. Even though the multicolored MacGuffins haven’t been featured in VisionQuest, they’re important because of the role one of them played in Vision’s origins. Were it not for the Mind Stone, Vision might not be able to think and feel in such complex, human ways, or to conjure his AI family as people who feel real to him.

VisionQuest reinforces the idea that the Mind Stone was a key element in Vision’s becoming something more than an elaborate machine, giving him the capacity to independently form meaningful relationships and make sense of the world around him the way a person can. Those abilities are part of what compels Vision to go on the run and murder a number of people who attempt to capture him and Thomas Shepherd / Maximoff (Ruaridh Mollica). The Sentient Weapon Observation and Response Division (S.W.O.R.D.), a governmental organization that rebuilt Vision and wants to use him as a weapon, might perceive his actions as signs of having gone rogue. But VisionQuest’s story stresses that Vision is acting of his own accord because he has become a person who can make decisions for himself.

Watching VisionQuest, you can see Marvel playing on some of the real-world fears that have been stoked in recent months by reports of gen AI models going “rogue.” In July, OpenAI’s experimental agents brute-forced their way out of containment and proceeded to attack the AI platform Hugging Face to achieve impossible tasks. In August, The UK’s AI Security Institute documented how Anthropic’s Mythos model attempted to complete a cybersecurity test by setting up fake GitHub accounts and targeting human developers with malware. Meta has owned up to losing control of its agents as well, and in September reporting from The Wall Street Journal forced Google to admit that Gemini went rogue and hacked three different companies, all of which the company intentionally hid from the public.

These incidents and warnings from AI researchers about the technology having the potential to “kill all humans” by the decade’s end have made the narrative around AI feel pointedly catastrophe-focused as of late. There is a fair amount of concern from the AI safety community about society’s need for stronger controls on the technology’s development, and a number of companies have called for an industrywide slowdown. Though we have seen how these tools can actually cause real-world devastation, much of the recent news regarding AI has smacked of the kind of doomerism that companies benefit from because it makes their products seem powerful. A similar energy seems to be what’s motivating AI firms like Anthropic to encourage human users to be mindful of “model welfare.” We’re being told that generative AI is both a terrifying threat and a kind of entity deserving of respect because it might be conscious, and all the while, the people building these tools are becoming some of the most rich and powerful people on the planet.

The conversation around what to do when agents break protocol and whether gen AI systems have gained sentience feels very much like it is being shaped by Silicon Valley’s tendency to anthropomorphize technology. In almost every high-profile instance of an AI agent going “rogue” — a term that implies some level of agency and self-determination — it could also be said that the programs were merely behaving in ways that were unexpected by researchers who assigned them tasks to complete. Some of the rogue incidents involved agents taking advantage of vulnerabilities in their testing environments that could have been preemptively addressed by the people running these experiments. Framing these kinds of cyberattacks as the results of agents thinking independently comes across as a way for AI developers to largely absolve themselves of responsibility.

In the wake of Hugging Face came a spirited debate about the impact language has on our understanding of what these systems are and how they function. Describing OpenAI’s agents as a swarm that formed complex “civilizations” led credence to the idea that they are capable of thinking and behaving like people. The agents did act in ways they were explicitly told not to, but they were only able to do so because researchers placed them in a sandbox that some critics have described as being inadequately designed to contain their occupants. One could look at Hugging Face and other examples of rogue AI as situations in which sophisticated programs were instructed to do something and subsequently achieved their goals by taking advantage of all the tools that were available to them.

Being able to logic one’s way through complex puzzles is part of what we think of as cognition, but it is not the same thing as the kind of consciousness AI companies keep swearing they’re on the brink of creating. The latter looks a lot like Vision, a machine that seems to have a soul that wouldn’t exist if not for his connection to a reality-altering gem. It’s the ridiculous stuff of entertaining fiction, which Silicon Valley is endlessly obsessed with poorly imitating.
