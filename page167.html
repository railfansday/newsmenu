The James Webb Space Telescope captured a spectacular star-forming region glowing 161,000 light-years from Earth in a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

The NASA/ESA James Webb Space Telescope has captured a breathtaking view of a majestic star-forming region shining 161,000 light-years from Earth in a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way known as the Large Magellanic Cloud.

The deep-space image reveals a 230-light-year-wide swathe of the glowing emission nebula N159, threaded with denser filaments of reddish molecular hydrogen that hide countless energetic young stars.

Ghostly blue clouds of ionized hydrogen gas (H II) glow in the center of the image, having been energized by the ultraviolet radiation pouring out from nearby stars, whose winds helped create the chaotic structure of the vast stellar nursery.

The ethereal pockets are surrounded by roiling orange clouds of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — a type of organic molecule that may account for 30% of all carbon in the universe, according to the U.S. Department of Energy.

N159 is estimated to be less than 3 million years old, making it one of the youngest star-forming regions in the Large Magellanic Cloud. It also has a markedly different composition than the stellar nurseries that spawn stars in our Milky Way, boasting a lower metal content that could mirror environments that existed in the early universe.

By pointing JWST at N159, astronomers open a window to the distant past. They can compare the characteristics of stars at varying stages of evolution, including pre-main-sequence stars that are still in the process of forming, or accreting from the vast interstellar cloud.

#5448. Volkswagen gets L3 autonomous driving test permit in China, eyes 2027

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Volkswagen Group China now has a permit to test Level 3 autonomous driving on public roads in Hefei, and it says testing is already underway. The permit also covers large-scale driving data collection in the city.

It’s the latest step toward VW’s goal of L3 technology readiness in China by 2027. And the timing is good as the group holds its first China Technology Day in Hefei on Wednesday.

VW says the permit was issued under China’s strictest L3 framework and that its setup meets the country’s latest mandatory safety design standards. It didn’t say which authority issued it, how many vehicles are in the fleet, or which roads they can use.

Hefei makes sense as a base. It’s home to the Volkswagen China Technology Company (VCTC), the group’s largest R&D center outside Germany, and to its Volkswagen Anhui production site.

The software comes from CARIZON, VW’s intelligent driving unit in China, which started as a joint venture between CARIAD and Horizon Robotics. Its driver assistance system is called Hyper Sense 8 (HS8), which VW describes as its first “full-scenario” ADAS.

Behind it is GAIA 2.0, a world model VW built itself. VW says it runs simulation equal to 5 million km of safety testing per day, using about 30 million real-road scenario clips, and it wants to get that to 10 million km per day. VW is also developing its own system-on-chip, the C7H, which it says can support up to Level 4.

Ralf Brandstätter, chairman and CEO of Volkswagen Group China, kept the message on safety. He said the group “will never advance technology at the cost of safety” and called the permit an important step on the road to L3 readiness (our translation from Chinese).

This is a road test permit. VW can’t sell L3 cars to customers with it.

China’s MIIT granted the country’s first L3 product approvals in December 2025 to two sedans: a Changan model capped at 50 km/h on designated Chongqing expressways, and a BAIC Arcfox model capped at 80 km/h on three Beijing routes. GAC’s Hyptec and XPeng already hold L3 road test permits too. So VW isn’t leading here. It’s catching up to Chinese brands on their home turf.

In July, VW, CARIZON, and Horizon Robotics said they would deepen their partnership, giving CARIZON “white-box” access to Horizon’s AI foundation model, and targeted the start of L3 deliveries in the second half of 2027. VW’s L2++ system is rolling out on seven new NEV models from its Chinese joint ventures this year.

VW also leans on XPeng in China. It bought a roughly 5% stake in XPeng in 2023 and co-developed its China zonal electrical architecture, CEA, with the startup.

Outside China, the only Western automaker with a certified L3 system on sale is Mercedes-Benz, whose Drive Pilot works on approved highways in Germany and in California and Nevada. Mercedes also got approval to test Level 4 driving in Beijing back in 2024. Tesla’s FSD remains a Level 2 system everywhere it’s sold, China included.

L3 is where liability moves. When the system is engaged, the automaker is responsible for the driving, not the person in the seat. That’s why so few companies have shipped it, and why the ones that have keep it on a short leash: specific roads, low speeds, mostly traffic jams.

China has been serious about building a framework for automakers to bring this techonology to market, and through it, we are seeing who is interested in this version of autonomy.

Not everyone is. Many automakers, including Tesla, have been talking about “skipping” level 3 and going straight to level 4.

#5583. Clean energy groups, states oppose MISO’s large load-generation study plan

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The grid operator’s proposed fast-track study process for large loads with generation lacks ratepayer protections and favors vertically integrated utilities over independent producers, critics said.

MISO proposed the 120-day LARS process earlier this month, proposing to offer it to loads larger than 200 MW and generation that is within the same local resource zone.

There are 10 local resource zones in MISO’s footprint, generally aligning with state boundaries. Under the proposal, MISO would study the planned load and generation at the same time.

MISO’s proposed LARS process is partly in response to FERC’s mid-June finding that its rules related to large loads are inadequate, including for integrating “proximate” loads and generation onto the grid, according to the grid operator’s application.

But the Organization of MISO States told FERC that a majority of its members have “significant concerns” about the proposal, including a lack of protections against transmission cost shifting to existing customers, and potential effects on competition and MISO’s generator interconnection queue.

“LARS cannot become a general alternative to the existing [generation interconnection queue] or provide preferential access to transmission capacity without adequate safeguards for existing queue participants and transmission customers,” OMS said, adding that any LARS-like program should end after five years.

Eleven state utility commissions supported OMS’s comments opposing the proposal. State commissions in Arkansas, Louisiana and Mississippi voted against the critical comments, and filed comments at FERC supporting MISO’s plan.

Meanwhile, in separate filings, Google and a coalition that includes the Sierra Club and the Natural Resources Defense Council said the proposal would lead to existing customers paying for network upgrades that are needed for any new loads and generation.

“The lack of cost shifting protections is especially concerning because, despite the benefits — especially speed to power — that LARS would promote, it is structured in a way that threatens Google’s ability to meet its foundational commitment to ensuring that other retail customers do not bear the costs of serving its data centers, including through the Ratepayer Protection Pledge,” the company said.

MISO plans to file a cost allocation framework for LARS projects next month, but “the details of that agreement and how it will protect against cost shifts are open questions, which the Commission cannot simply assume away,” Google said.

Ratepayer advocates representing six states also opposed MISO’s proposal, partly because of concerns about cost-shifting.

Under the proposal, MISO would only accept LARS applications from its transmission customers — load serving entities, which are mainly vertically integrated utilities.

The American Clean Power Association and other groups said that requirement would give utilities the ability to favor their own generation at the expense of independent power producers.

“Even an IPP with a direct offtake agreement with the large load must be sponsored by the [load-serving entity’s] application; there is no path for an IPP to deal directly with MISO for purposes of accessing the generator interconnection queue,” ACP and the Southern Renewable Energy Association said in a joint filing.

Even if a LSE agrees to include an IPP in a LARS application, the IPP faces stricter eligibility requirements compared to utility generation, according to the trade groups.

The Solar Energy Industries Association and Advanced Energy United also asked FERC to reject the proposal, which they said “is unduly discriminatory because it allows LSEs to decide if their competitors may participate in the LARS process.”

The Data Center Coalition generally supported MISO’s proposal, but said it is concerned about giving utilities sole access to the process.

“In MISO’s primarily vertically integrated footprint, establishing the LSE as the sole LARS application facilitator narrows the pool of supply that can gain access to expedited Large Load Generation Interconnection Agreements ... to only those projects that are effectively approved by the LSE,” the trade group said.

“The LARS proposal is a narrowly tailored but valuable tool that enables the accelerated evaluation and integration of electrically proximate large load and generation pairs and the related identification of more efficient network upgrades,” a group of MISO transmission owners told FERC.

As a permanent plan, the LARS proposal will provide a long-term market signal for pairing new large loads and generation for at least 15 years, the utilities said.

Entergy and Cleco Power urged FERC to approve the plan.

“Making LARS a permanent process is appropriate because it provides needed, long-term regulatory and financial certainty required to site and finance large load-and-generation pairings, and it includes structural limits that keep the process bounded in every cycle,” they said in a joint filing.
