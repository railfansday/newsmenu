#3683. 3 Types of Investors Who Should Avoid SpaceX and Tesla Stocks as the Companies Explore a Potential Merger

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Space Exploration Technologies' (SPCX +1.14%) third-quarter earnings call on Oct. 20, CEO Elon Musk revealed that the company and his other megacap, Tesla (TSLA +4.04%), are contemplating a merger.

Not surprisingly, many investors have taken a strong interest in a possible SpaceX-Tesla merger. The combined entity would be one of the world's largest AI companies.

Although many investors would likely own stakes in that company, such a stock is not suitable for everyone. In particular, these three types of investors should probably not rush to buy it.

Even for those who know nothing about investing, both SpaceX and Tesla might look like attractive choices on the surface. Both are growing their revenues thanks to technologies that increasingly interest the public, and Musk's past successes add to their appeal.

However, most investors do not own individual stocks. Instead, they tend to own long-term passive mutual funds and exchange-traded funds held in their retirement accounts. That is a sensible strategy, particularly for investors who don't have the time or willingness to dig into the details of individual businesses' competitive advantages, risks, and financial metrics. Many of these funds may include Tesla and SpaceX among their numerous other holdings.

Moreover, if one wants to learn how to invest in stocks, most experts recommend starting with companies that have well-known brands, solid balance sheets, and long track records. SpaceX and Tesla are both well-known, but SpaceX doesn't even have a full year under its belt as a public company, and Tesla only went public in 2010 -- that's not a terribly long track record either.

Moreover, objectively speaking, these companies' financials may not be the easiest to understand. Blue chips such as Home Depot or Chevron, which have existed for decades and operate in more straightforward businesses, may make better sense as one's first stock picks.

Another aspect of investing that both novices and veterans alike must account for in their choices is their risk tolerance.

Risk-averse investors would rather accept less growth potential in exchange for a lower chance that they'll actually lose money on a stock. As such, they are better advised to buy slower-growing stocks in stable businesses that are less likely to fall in the long run. Even in bear markets, they tend to decline less than cutting-edge tech companies that get valued heavily on the future potential, and those slow-and-steady stocks often pay dividends.

In contrast, risk-tolerant investors are willing to accept a markedly higher possibility of stock price declines if it increases the likelihood of rapid growth. Almost by definition, this requires them to be comfortable holding on through significant short-term ups and downs.  Not surprisingly, SpaceX and Tesla typically attract this type of investor despite being among the largest companies trading on the market.

Additionally, both are bets on the artificial intelligence megatrend. Assuming their AI technology thrives in niche markets, either of their stocks or the combined entity could earn considerable returns. However, failures in this area could mean investor losses. Moreover, since both trade at extremely high and speculative valuations, investors today face a greater risk of overpaying for these stocks.

Not all investors will feel comfortable with these risks. Those who don't should probably not own these companies directly.

Given the other two reasons to not own these stocks, telling some growth investors not to buy SpaceX or Tesla does not seem to make sense. Tesla has historically experienced impressive periods of rapid growth in its publicly traded history, and both appear well-positioned to outperform the S&P 500.

Unfortunately, investors also must temper their expectations given the companies' sizes. Growth investors have likely read stories about how those who invested a few thousand dollars in Home Depot or Amazon around their IPOs and held on now have positions worth millions.

The difference is that Home Depot or Amazon were small-cap companies at the time of their IPOs. At this point, Tesla and SpaceX are megacaps. Currently, Tesla's market cap is about $1.5 trillion, while SpaceX's is at $2 trillion -- just about where it was early on the day of its June IPO.

This means that the combined company's size would be at least $3.5 trillion. A 100-fold gain in such a stock would take it to $350 trillion. Considering that no company has yet reached $6 trillion, and the entire value of the U.S. stock market is around $75 trillion, even a 10-fold gain would seem hard to imagine.

Admittedly, AI development is driving rapid growth. If a combined SpaceX and Tesla succeeds, a $10 trillion or even $20 trillion market cap is possible in the foreseeable future. Still, a $20 trillion market cap would be close to a sixfold gain. That would be impressive, but it's still well short of the 100-fold or more return potential that extreme growth investors might seek.

Although a combined SpaceX and Tesla holding could be desirable, that prospective stock would not be suitable for everyone. Both companies are likely to be among the top AI companies. This could mean they will outperform the market regardless of whether the merger happens.

Nonetheless, before buying such a stock, one has to know what kind of investor they are. If they are not knowledgeable, cannot tolerate significant risk, or have extreme growth expectations, they may not want to buy shares of this prospective future company.

#3633. ‘The Mosquito Bowl’ Review: ‘Friday Night Lights’ Meets ‘Five Came Back’ in Peter Berg’s Formulaic Netflix Movie About a World War II Football Game

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There have been any number of movies about the hell of fighting in World War II, the most visceral of which have been made in the years since “Saving Private Ryan” provided a very enduring template for how modern filmmakers could render that carnage with both the spectacle of a blockbuster and the horror of a memory. Peter Berg’s brutal and concussive “The Mosquito Bowl” is definitely one of them! No one could ever say that it’s not.

What differentiates this unsparing epic from the scores of other stories about the Greatest Generation, however, is that some of its characters are good at football. Indeed, these All-American college boys are so good at football that, on Christmas Eve 1944, the soldiers are ordered to play a morale-boosting game of two-hand touch on the beach where their units are preparing for the Battle of Okinawa. Related Stories ‘The Gilded Age’ Review: Season 4 Is Team Bertha’s Time to Shine ‘I Play Rocky’ Wins Virginia Film Fest Audience Award as Peter Farrelly’s Stallone Drama Readies for Theaters

And for the span of the eight-minute montage during which “The Mosquito Bowl” actually makes good on its title, it’s easy to picture how the famous scrimmage — fought between a roster of impossibly beautiful athletes in their primes — might have been used to backstop a story that accentuated the senseless waste of sending thousands of young men to their deaths. Alas, for the rest of this 126-minute film, that waste is better expressed through Berg’s failure to make anything more of their lives, as the “Spenser Confidential” auteur’s latest Netflix project does almost nothing else to distinguish itself from a genre so well-worn that it’s seemingly run out of ways to blow up a human body.

Why “almost”? I was hoping you wouldn’t ask. For starters, there’s the decision to cast thin-skinned ESPN commentator Pat McAfee as an R. Lee Ermey-inspired drill sergeant. “The Mosquito Bowl” also stands out for how confusingly it conflates the thrill and teamwork of combat sport with the violence and camaraderie of armed conflict; war is presented as both a natural extension of what these boys have been trained to do on the gridiron (to the point that the soldiers briefly diagram their plan of attack as if it were a football play), and also the catastrophe that robbed them of a more innocent purpose. (Berg loves the troops as much as he hates the fact that generals and presidents so wantonly sacrifice their lives.) Someone declares that “The Marine Corps is the greatest offensive line in history,” and yet the film’s characters don’t seem to have any special advantages on the field of battle, as it would be glib to suggest that even the country’s best college running back might be able to slip free of a mortar blast. It’s one thing to argue that football is war, and another, much more asinine thing to argue that war is football.

Perhaps that’s why the Mosquito Bowl itself only took up a single page of the Buzz Bissinger book on which Berg’s film is based, and why that source material used the game as a lens through which a light could be shined on the semi-forgotten men who found their way into the lineup that day. It’s been suggested that Bissinger played up the sports angle in order to capitalize on the success of his “Friday Night Lights,” and given that Berg adapted that book into a film as well, it stands to reason that his “Mosquito Bowl” should do the same.

The problem is that, in stark contrast to Bissinger, Berg and co-writer Mark L. Smith only seem to care about their characters as a means to an end. The project’s anti-war ethos is solely conveyed by reducing collegiate legends into cannon fodder, as the movie is considerably less interested in what bonds these boys together than it is in the shells that blast them apart.

Perhaps that explains why I’ve been able to write almost 650 words without bothering to mention any of their names. To the extent that it matters to a film so blandly de-saturated that everyone in it looks half-dead from the start, Nicholas Galitzine stars as Brown University legend John McLaughry — the only character we know for certain will survive the assault on Japan. (The movie opens with a sustained flash-forward of John staring longingly at an empty football field as “Friday Night Lights” veterans Explosions in the Sky layer their post-rock shimmer over the soundtrack). He’s got sad eyes, a square jaw, and a brunette girlfriend, which is more than you can say for Bob Baumann (Bill Skarsgård), who has a devil-may-care attitude that disappears real quick once his unit makes land on Tawara. The only real source of contextual friction is owed to the rivalry between Purdue fullback Tony Butkovich (a glinty and magnetic Ray Nicholson) and the straight-laced mama’s boy he beat in a big game before the attack on Pearl Harbor (Tom Francis as the stoic and decent Dave Schreiner).

Individually, Butkovich is the only one of these characters who has the chutzpah to carry the movie on his back. Incidentally, he’s the only one of these men who was heading to the pros before the war got in the way. Still, it’s engaging to see them all blitz through life on their home turf, and “The Mosquito Bowl” benefits from Berg’s handheld and ruggedly naturalistic approach to how the players comport themselves off the field (read: they say “fuck” a lot and generally aspire to feel more like real people than they do patronizing impressions of what men were like in the ’40s).

But then the war comes to Hawaii, a match-cut turns a flock of birds into some fighter jets, and these beloved football stars all find themselves rendered nearly anonymous by the sea of shaved heads that are waiting for them at book. Little is made of their civilian celebrity, and even less of their individual decisions to enlist. “This is my rifle — there are many others like it, but this one is mine.”

Berg takes militaristic uniformity as all the excuse he needs to avoid deviating from expectation, and nearly everything that happens between basic training and the big game on the beach near Okinawa feels like it could have happened in several dozen other World War II movies, and almost certainly did. Defiant warriors curdle into terrified kids, bullets whizz through ocean waves, snipers pick off predictable characters at unsuspecting — or very suspecting — moments, and even the most hardened of marines find themselves staring blankly into the middle distance as their friends are decimated on all sides. Berg has always had a flair for the frenetic, and while there’s a perversely fetishistic veneer to the attention he devotes to entrails and enemy screams, it serves the film’s purposes that war has seldom seemed less survivable than it does here. (The film has no interest in humanizing the Japanese, but tries to compensate for that by presenting a few of the Americans as unrepentant monsters).

Still, for all of its supposed verisimilitude, “The Mosquito Bowl” seems less indebted to the reality of war than it does to Hollywood’s idea of it, and its abject refusal to flesh out its characters — a dereliction of duty, if ever there was one — makes it feel more contrived rather than less. I’m happy for Berg that he found a way to combine all of his favorite things into a single project (football, the military, and massive fireballs are all well-accounted for, even if Mark Wahlberg’s absence makes the whole movie feel like it’s forgotten something important), but “The Mosquito Bowl” accomplishes very little beyond violently illustrating that war is worse than football. It does that with such fervor that it loses all sight of what those things might really have in common.

#3476. Google's 'Health Guardian' Is Now Available for Fitbits

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google’s “Health Guardian” feature set rolled out in late September to Pixel Watches 3, 4, and 5, and is now coming to Fitbit Air users in a new update. Google says the new features include reports on your blood pressure trends and on insulin resistance, which is a big claim for a wearable. Here’s what we know about Health Guardian, including how to set it up and what to expect.

Insulin resistance is associated with type 2 diabetes, and can be an early sign that you are heading toward pre-diabetes or diabetes. When your body’s insulin receptors stop responding as strongly to insulin, you’re more likely to have higher blood glucose.

Insulin resistance is normally measured with bloodwork. For example, your fasting glucose levels and fasting insulin are combined into a score called HOMA-IR. If you’re concerned about insulin resistance or pre-diabetes, it’s best to talk to an actual healthcare professional to find out what tests they recommend, and what to do with the results.

The insulin resistance feature coming to Google Health is, Google notes, “not intended for medical purposes” and “not a pre-screener for diabetes.”

Google published a paper describing some of the testing that went into developing its insulin resistance algorithm. In the study, the researchers had people use their own Fitbits or Pixel Watches to record a variety of metrics, including heart rate, HRV, and activity. It’s not clear from this paper exactly how the algorithm works or whether it’s likely to be useful in a general population, but apparently Google is betting on it being an interesting factor to track in your Google Health app.

Google also published a paper on a blood pressure algorithm, although it is not peer-reviewed. Instead of explaining how the algorithm works, it describes how the researchers tried to figure out what their AI-based model might be looking at. According to this paper, Google's model works about as well as clinical blood pressure measurements, but it's not clear how useful it would be in the real world. For example, the study excluded people with heart disease, but I'd imagine plenty of people with heart disease would be interested in their blood pressure scores.

Google said last month in a press release that Health Guardian, which includes insulin resistance and blood pressure trends, is available for free for people who use a Pixel Watch 3, 4, or 5. Fitbit Air users, they added, would get blood pressure and insulin resistance trends “with a Google Health Premium subscription later this year.” "Later" has arrived, as the feature for Fitbit users is in Google Health 5.11, available on both Android and iOS.

So it’s free if you have a Pixel Watch, but not if you only have the Fitbit Air: Google Health Premium is $9.99/month. It’s unclear what happens if you own both devices; presumably, having a Pixel Watch will get you free access. In any case, other devices, including older Fitbits, do not seem to be included.

The Pixel Watch got Health Guardian in the 5.09 release of the Google Health app for Android. The Fitbit got Health Guardian in the 5.11 release for both Android and iOS.

You can set up your blood pressure and insulin resistance tracking as soon as you get the new version of the Google Health app. I was able to set up insulin tracking by going to the health tab and then tapping Metabolic, and I set up blood pressure under Heart. If you go to Respiratory, you can also set up sleep-breathing quality. (Some European users will get a feature that can call for help in a "breathing emergency," but that is not available in the U.S.)

Google said that the insulin and blood pressure features will give you a monthly report on the first of each month, with the first report coming Oct. 1, if you have given the watch enough data in the previous month. If you're a Fitbit user and are just setting up the feature now, you'll get your first report on Nov. 1. Here is what the app requires:

For blood pressure reports: You need to wear the watch for five consecutive days in the previous month (so, in October to get your November report). You don't have to wear the watch at night.

For insulin resistance: You need to wear the watch for seven days and nights in the previous month, but they don't need to be consecutive.

I set up both features as soon as I got the update for the Pixel Watch, but that timeline didn't allow me a full seven days and nights before the October report. That means I don't have an insulin resistance result. I do have my blood pressure trend result: "Not High." As a reminder, a watch cannot know your actual blood pressure, so the watch is making estimates based on data that may not correspond with your actual blood pressure. That said, my blood pressure tends to read in the normal range at doctors' visits, so at least this wasn't surprising.

What's more confusing is what you're actually supposed to do about the results. If your watch says your blood pressure or insulin are fine, would you delay seeking care or abandon a treatment or lifestyle change that was supposed to help you? If your watch says something is wrong, would you believe that and maybe spend unnecessary time and money getting checked out? Pixel Watch users are discussing their results online, with a variety of reactions. One redditor says: "I awoke to find out that my blood pressure is too high and apparently the insulin in my body does nothing. This despite that my most recent doctor's visit and blood work were all perfectly normal." Another said: "I got this high insulin result this morning. I have really changed my diet the last four months and I have been going to the gym regularly. I have lost about 40 pounds this year. It's a bummer to see this. I just wonder if it's accurate." I wonder that, too.

#3385. This old Nokia phone got an AI Upgrade it was never designed for

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The GOAT of dumb phones gets a new lease on life.

Nearly 20 years ago, Nokia released the Nokia 6300. Despite arriving the same month Steve Jobs would introduce the iPhone at MacWorld 2007, the 6300 would go on to become one of Nokia's best-selling phones, thanks to its understated but attractive design, affordable price and easy-to-use software. In 2026, however, the original 6300, not to be confused with the recent 4G rerelease, is a device on borrowed time.

For one, it's a 2G phone, and finding a carrier that still operates its old 2G network is becoming more difficult with each passing year. Then there's the phone's browser, which Nokia never updated to work with any internet security protocols beyond TLS 1.0. After Apple, Google, Microsoft and Mozilla came together to deprecate the standard in 2020, less than an estimated 25 percent of all websites support TSL 1.0 connections as of mid last year, meaning the 6300 essentially can't access the modern internet. There's not much that can be done about the former, but one coder has devised a pretty clever solution to get around the 6300's dated browser using the latest chatbots.

In late September, Turkish entrepreneur Emir Karşıyakalı took to X to show off Claude S40, an app he vibe coded to make his old Nokia 6300 a bit more useable in 2026. In its original form, his app app allowed the 6300 to connect with Claude over a Java and Go backend. Since then, Karşıyakalı has updated the software to make it work with all of the major chatbots, including ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok, and renamed the app Aikon — that's Nokia spelt backwards — to reflect the greater compatibility.

Outside of a phone running either Nokia's S40 or S60 operating systems, you'll need a Go server to hold the API key from Anthropic, Google, OpenAI or SpaceXSI. This part of the equation is essential since it's the server that communicates with Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Grok, and truncates their responses into 8kB blocks your old phone can read. It's also worth mentioning, since you're going through each company's API, you'll need to set up token billing.

For what is ultimately a gimmick, Karşıyakalı's app is quite robust and thoughtful. There's an reading mode, with a dedicated keypad shortcut, to make using a chatbot on the 6300's 240x320 display a bit more manageable. Provided you a memory card, you can also save responses to the 6300 for offline reading. If you want to send photos to your favorite chatbot for analysis, you can do that too. It's even possible to send voice messages up to 30 seconds long, with an OpenAI model handling the speech-to-text conversion. Over on the server side, Aikon will hold messages for up to 30 days, and the app supports English, Spanish, Portuguese, French, German, Russian, Indonesian and Turkish. Karşıyakalı has open sourced the app so others can continue building on the app.

Now, before you dig out your old Nokia phone, just know if you live in the US there's no way you'll get this app running. That's because the nation's last 2G network went offline this summer when T-Mobile shut down its old GSM network in August. If you live outside of North America and want to try Aikon out for yourself, head over to Github and follow the installation instructions.

#3127. Evaluating the 49ers’ defense during the bye week

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The San Francisco 49ers will enjoy their bye week with a 6-1 record and leading the NFC West. A loss to Atlanta was frustrating, but all things considered, the 49ers are in a great spot with one loss through seven weeks. The loss itself isn't the end of the world, but it was frustrating, as the 49ers had every opportunity to leave Atlanta with a win.

Nobody expected the 49ers to go undefeated, and they will lose more games this season. If someone told you the 49ers would be 6-1 heading into their bye week, you would take that 10/10 times.

The team will return impact players next week against the Raiders, potentially including Nick Bosa. Let's take a look at the 49ers defense during the bye week.

The 49ers rank fourth in the NFL, allowing 299.3 yards per game and 13 touchdowns on the season. Some would attribute the fine start to the opponents, but it's clear this defense is a step or two above last season. Although Sunday was rough for the defensive line and pass rush, the overall body of work is impressive, with the 49ers on pace to shatter last season's sack total.

Defending the pass has the 49ers slipping to number ten with 210.3 yards per game and ten passing touchdowns allowed. Still, a top-ten passing defense is excellent, and the secondary has room for improvement. Sunday saw Atlanta attack underneath more and take advantage of soft cushions and Cover 3 zones.
