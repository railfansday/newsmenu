#271. Jordan attacks militants in Syria in retaliation for church attack

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Jordanian air force says it carried out strikes against ‘terrorist groups’ tied to killing of nine during funeral

Jordan has carried out airstrikes against armed groups in Syria in retaliation for Friday’s attack on a Greek Orthodox church in the southern Jordanian governorate of Karak that killed nine people, the Jordanian air force has said in a statement.

It said the operation “resulted in the killing and wounding of many members of these terrorist groups and the destruction of a number of their vehicles”. It did not specify who the groups were or the precise location of the strikes.

Syrian state media reported that civilians had been killed in the strikes. The director of health in the southern province of Daraa, where the strikes took place, told the Syrian national news agency, Sana, that at least five civilians had been killed, including women and children and a 65-year-old man.

Jordan’s armed forces said the operation was conducted in coordination with Damascus, but Syria’s government gave a contradictory account, saying it had launched an investigation into the ⁠Jordanian attacks, and stressed in a statement the importance of field and security coordination in border areas.

In the aftermath of the strikes, Syria said it had closed the ⁠Nassib border crossing with Jordan, suspending ⁠traffic in both directions. Nassib is Jordan’s only passenger and commercial border crossing with Syria.

Earlier on Monday, Jordanian authorities arrested three men for last week’s attack, which also wounded more than two dozen people. The arrests followed a dawn shootout, and all three men were wounded, officials said.

The attack happened during a funeral at St George’s Greek Orthodox church in Hamoud, Karak governorate, on Friday. Karak is home to a minority Christian population.

The attack shocked the nation, and Jordan declared three days of mourning for the victims. No group immediately claimed responsibility for the attack; the attackers’ motive remained unknown.

Later on Monday, Jordan’s Public Security Directorate identified the three arrested men as brothers Hazzah Zaidan Mohammad and Mishal Zaidan Mohammad, and their brother-in-law Ahmad Sami Hussein. All three are Jordanian; they were taken into custody in Karak, about 55 miles (90km) south of Amman.

The agency’s chief described the gunmen as “takfiri”, a term used for Sunni militants, but said they were not tied to any organised group.

“They only represent themselves,” Maj Gen Obaidallah Abd Rabbo Al-Maayta told a news conference. He also said a large cache of munitions was found in the abandoned house where Jordanian forces apprehended the men.

Jordan’s interior minister, Mazen al-Faraya, said the attack by the three men was the result of “a terroristic, extremist way of thinking”.

“If this way of thinking is not resolved, we will see incidents like this again,” he told reporters.

Jordan’s King Abdullah II had ordered authorities to respond with “an iron fist” against what he called the outlaws of Islam and anyone attempting to undermine the unity of the Jordanian people.

“We vowed the terrorists would not escape punishment, and no proponent of dark ideologies that divide Jordanians will be tolerated,” he said in a statement on Monday.

Relatives of the victims said the arrests brought them some relief.

“Despite the profound and immense tragedy, what happened today has eased our grief, and the grief of the Jordanian people – not just for us, the families of the martyrs,” Khaled al-Borqan, himself a survivor of the attack and a relative of three of the people killed, told Al-Mamlaka.

“Despite the sadness and pain, the news brought us joy,” Fares Halaseh, an uncle of one of the victims and a survivor of the attack, told the TV station.

But the pain remains, he added. “We will not forget this agony for years to come.”

This article was amended on 27 October 2026. The attacked church is located in Hamoud, in the Karak governorate, not in the city of Karak as stated in an earlier version.

#3700. James Gunn and Peter Safran’s DC Experiment: What’s Working, What Isn’t, and What’s Next

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Four years ago this week, James Gunn and Peter Safran were handed one of Hollywood’s most coveted, and impossible, jobs. When they were named co-CEOs of Warner Bros.’ DC Studios label, they received complete oversight of comic book characters like Superman, Wonder Woman, Batman* (more on that later), Green Lantern, the Flash, and countless others. With a seemingly blank canvas to make as many movies and shows as their hearts desired, the implied mandate was clear: get DC Comics out of second place.

The studio’s DC division spent much of the 2010s as a laughingstock while its Marvel competitors seemed to never miss. Despite having a better roster of characters — 20 years ago, mentioning the Guardians of the Galaxy in the same breath as Batman would have been laughable even to the biggest comic book nerd — Warner Bros. couldn’t figure out how to turn its characters into must-see cinema. The incredible cultural moment of the Christopher Nolan Batman movies gave way to one misfire after another: Zack Snyder’s “Man of Steel” gave way to the “Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice” debacle, Patty Jenkins’ acclaimed “Wonder Woman” movie led to a critically panned sequel, and the studio’s attempt to launch its own “Avengers” with “Justice League” was bad enough to leave fans arguing about different cuts instead of wondering where the story went next. Related Stories ‘I Play Rocky’ Wins Virginia Film Fest Audience Award as Peter Farrelly’s Stallone Drama Readies for Theaters Gotham Film Awards 2026 Nominations Spread the Love from ‘All of a Sudden’ to ‘The Invite’

It was the kind of turnaround project that entitles its leaders to a long runway. After Gunn and Safran accepted the job in late 2022, they began announcing projects in early 2023. Then they had to shoot and edit them, meaning that we didn’t really see the vision take shape until this year. But now we’ve seen Gunn and Safran release three major theatrical films, two live-action shows, and multiple animated ones — more than enough of a sample size to start grading their creative direction. Even if we’re still in Phase One of their long-term plan, the pros and cons of their studio approach are becoming clear.

Gunn and Safran kicked off their DC tenure with the safest and smartest film they could have possibly made: a “Superman” movie written and directed by Gunn himself. Putting DC’s signature character in the hands of a filmmaker with an unimpeachable ability to deliver smart popcorn fare was a no-brainer. And in many ways, “Superman” delivered.

The film wisely skipped the obligatory origin story that everyone already knows, checking in with the man formerly known as Kal-El after he had already assumed his dual role of Superman and Clark Kent on Earth. Gunn brought his signature blend of heart, humor, and needle drops, delivering an entertaining blockbuster that stood in clear contrast to the excessively dark DC films of the 2010s. This movie was colorful and fun, even in its heaviest moments. Unsurprisingly, it grossed over $600 million worldwide — though it still didn’t tell us much about Gunn’s ability to run a studio. His bona fides as a comic book writer/director were never in doubt, but we still hadn’t seen him supervise other filmmakers.

Those opportunities came in 2026, and the results were more polarizing. The studio’s second film, “Supergirl,” was one of the year’s most public flops. Milly Alcock’s eponymous character appeared briefly in “Superman,” but Craig Gillespie’s film traded its predecessor’s grounded Earth drama for a space western set across a series of “Mad Max”-esque planets. The confusing mythology and grating visual effects felt like a departure, and the film received mixed reviews and only made $100 million worldwide despite costing nearly twice that. Equally concerning were reports about creative clashes between Gunn and Safran and Gillespie, with two competing cuts being screened against each other months before the movie opened.

Then came “Clayface.” James Watkins’ origin story for an obscure Batman villain was branded as a horror movie that just happened to exist within a comic book universe. The gory, goopy story of an actor who sacrifices everything to regain his skin-deep beauty wasn’t for everyone, but it represented a real creative swing. And it was made on a responsible $44 million budget, which removed the pressure to be universally appealing.

It’s easy to see a world where this DC universe thrives with a combination of major tentpoles directed by Gunn (who is already hard at work on a “Superman” follow-up dated for July 2027) and smaller, weirder films based on minor characters. But we still haven’t seen Gunn and Safran oversee a blockbuster that connects with mainstream audiences with another filmmaker in the director’s chair. “Supergirl” may end up being an anomaly, but right now it still represents one-third of their theatrical output. That sadly has to count for something.

Gunn and Safran’s overhaul of DC’s TV output technically began before they took over as CEOs, as “Peacemaker” premiered on HBO Max nine months earlier. Created (and largely directed) by Gunn, the raunchy action comedy saw John Cena reprising his role from Gunn’s “Suicide Squad” movie as an antihero willing to kill as many men, women, and children as he needs to achieve his goal of world peace. Gunn’s sensibilities were still there, but the HBO brand let the show include far more nudity and profanity than you’d ever find in a four-quadrant film. A hit with fans and critics, “Peacemaker” reminded audiences what was possible when you had access to a premium cable brand to complement your theatrical films.

Their first official project after taking the reins was also a TV show. The animated “Creature Commandos” debuted on HBO Max in December 2024 with an inoffensive six-episode Season 1 that showcased Gunn’s favorite themes about weirdos who assemble their own found families. The business stakes were low — fairly or not, the average consumer isn’t watching animated superhero shows, and the diehard fans who do tune in are unlikely to ever stop supporting DC’s other projects. But the series has one notable wrinkle: Gunn cast its voice actors with the intention of reprising their roles in live-action projects, a tactic he says he plans to use on all future animated projects.

“Peacemaker” also concluded with a well-received Season 2, but the real test of the DC Universe’s TV output came this fall with “Lanterns.” Chris Mundy, Damon Lindelof, and Tom King’s series about DC’s intergalactic police force more than delivered on its promise to be “True Detective” with Green Lanterns. The film uses classic police procedural tropes — the show centers on an older, set-in-his-ways Green Lantern who reluctantly partners with a talented young partner he won’t give the time of day to — in a dark, season-long murder saga set in middle America. The show itself received rave reviews, but its actual contents matter less than what it represents: a unique, auteur-driven project that fits into Gunn and Safran’s storyline while allowing other filmmakers to showcase their own voices. So far, it’s the ultimate example of what the CEOs have always said they wanted their DC Studios to do.

Perhaps the biggest variable facing Gunn and Safran’s DC Studios is largely outside their control. Matt Reeves’ “The Batman” had already opened in theaters a few months before they took over, and Warner Bros. announced that the Robert Pattinson-led series would continue separately from the new DC Universe. A second film is in production, with Steven Soderbergh helping to complete principal photography, and HBO shows like “The Penguin” and the recently announced “Poison Ivy” continue to expand the mythology.

Gunn and Safran have some involvement in these “Batman” projects and publicly support them, but the films and shows exist outside the overarching storyline they’re trying to build. And while it’s not an insurmountable problem — multiple artists working with the same characters in different timelines has been a trademark of comic books from the very beginning — it’s also not ideal to have one of your franchise’s two biggest characters tied up outside your purview.

Nothing’s stopping Gunn and Safran from bringing in their own Batman, and it seems inevitable that it will happen soon. “Clayface” takes place in Gotham City, and once you start introducing Batman villains, you need to give audiences something to fight them. Gunn and Safran previously tapped Andy Muschietti to direct a Batman movie called “The Brave and the Bold,” which was still in development as of 2025, but no cast or release date has been announced.

Many fans and Warner Bros. shareholders believe this DC Universe won’t reach its full potential until it introduces its own Batman. The kind of major team-up movies with billion-dollar box office potential, like a new “Justice League” or a “World’s Finest” team-up between Batman and Superman, all feature the Dark Knight. And assuming their Batman isn’t Robert Pattinson, they’ll inevitably be faced with audiences who wonder why or see theirs as a second-tier product.

It’s not fair to grade Gunn and Safran on this problem, since they didn’t create it, but they still have to navigate it. Again, it’s not insurmountable, but Kevin Feige never had to deal with this at Marvel.

Gunn and Safran’s overarching DC story is still in its infancy, but their development process has become clear. And it’s arguably their best asset. In many ways, their DC Studios can be described as the anti-MCU. Whereas their competitor (and Gunn’s former employer) became famous for seeing everything as part of a larger puzzle, DC Studios seems much more focused on treating each film as a standalone project. Films are greenlit based on the quality of their individual scripts, not the Easter eggs that connect them. Nobody would ever tell you that a brand-new DC cinematic universe should release “Clayface” as one of its first films, but Gunn has openly said Mike Flanagan’s script stood out among the many other projects they were developing. The script was ready; therefore, they made the movie.

Prioritizing scripts over corporate synergy should be music to the ears of cinephiles burned out on Marvel’s assembly-line approach to storytelling. That means continuity will never be as tight as what you find in the MCU, and plenty of projects will be announced without ever coming to fruition. (We’re still upset about James Mangold’s “Swamp Thing” and Luca Guadagnino’s “Sgt. Rock” falling through the cracks, but it’s better than rushing out movies that aren’t ready.) Comic book fans used to following these companies like sports teams might need to adjust their expectations, but movie lovers who care less about those details should get richer viewing experiences. It’s also better for the creatives who get a chance to pitch their own original ideas and put their own stamp on beloved characters.

The only thing holding this back from an “A” grade is the fact that ideals that sound good on paper still have to be executed. I deeply admire Gunn for publicly declaring that no DC Studios movie will receive a release date until everyone is happy with the script. But I also saw “Supergirl” and wondered what this mysterious great script could have possibly looked like, and why it apparently didn’t make it into the finished movie.

Startups have never been under more pressure to grow quickly. To do that, many of them must land large enterprises, the deep pockets of the IT world, as customers. But as any company that sells to enterprises will tell you, it’s not easy to win such contracts.

One critical mistake most younger startups make is believing that their tech will practically sell itself, says Arsalan Tavakoli-Shiraji, co-founder and senior vice president of field engineering at Databricks, which helps businesses store and analyze huge amounts of data.

Yes. The tech must work well. But that’s not enough, he warns. Databricks today is an AI and big data storage giant, valued at $190 billion, with more than 70% of the Fortune 500 as customers. But it was once a tiny startup, and Tavakoli-Shiraji was responsible for helping bring in its first big deals.

“Everybody when they’re selling to enterprises at startups … we think it’s all about the tech and having the best tech,” Tavakoli-Shiraji told attendees at TechCrunch Disrupt last week. But he learned the hard way that “people and processes matter the most.”

He remembers securing a meeting with a prospect in the early days, only to discover that it was the same person who signed a mega-deal with his major competitor. “He has no interest in listening to me because he bet his career on that competitor,” Tavakoli-Shiraji said. He also remembers early deals fizzling because the startup wasn’t prepared for legal, contractual, or security questions.

He offered his top tips for startups to win big customers:

When Databricks was young, it tried and failed to score a meeting with UBS, the large Swiss bank. Then it did “a partnership with Microsoft,” becoming an Azure cloud partner. That meant sales agreements would be covered under Microsoft’s terms.

But it also meant that Microsoft’s sales folks actually booked the meetings and advocated for the startup. “All of a sudden, that meeting wasn’t hard to get,” the founder recalled. “It was a huge boon to us that we were able to partner with somebody who had that access and distribution.”

With Microsoft’s sales team helping, “we started getting pulled everywhere.” That created new problems, like prospects wanting features for countries Databricks hadn’t expanded to, but they were good problems to have.

Today, Databricks is the big company that works with startups, selling their products under Databricks’ own terms and cybersecurity setup. Examples include Hightouch, which provides AI-powered marketing and customer-data tools (Databricks has also invested in it); Addepar, which offers data tools for investment professionals; and Tecton, a machine learning startup that Databricks later acquired.

Tavakoli-Shiraji groups enterprises’ main concerns and objections into four categories he calls the four Cs: context, choice, cost, and control. Context means an AI that can be trained “like bringing on a new employee,” he says, learning the company’s terminology, processes, priorities, and so on.

Choice is the freedom to switch AI models rather than be tied to a single provider. Cost is about controlling how much the AI product will cost to run. Control involves the safeguards that govern the AI’s behavior and protecting sensitive information.

Tavakoli-Shiraji jokingly asks CIOs where they stand with AI on a scale of one to five. A one means employees merely have access to ChatGPT, and a five means the company has adopted new AI capabilities that have changed its business processes.

“And everybody loves picking, ‘I’m three leaning towards four.’ But 99% of the ones I talk to are in bucket one and two.” Enterprises still haven’t figured out how to deploy AI agents in ways that create value beyond allowing engineering teams to write code. “It’s going to take many, many years before people can actually properly deploy AI in a very meaningful way in the enterprise.”

One big opportunity for startups to bring that value is to modernize expensive legacy systems. “If you go to any bank today, they still have [an IBM] AS/400 and mainframes,” he described. “Nobody wanted to touch it because nobody knew what that code did.”

But today, AI agents “can go in and understand and actually decompose what that code does.” That, he says, opens the door to products that do everything from adding modern interfaces for those ancient systems to replacing them altogether.

#5210. Manhattan Luxury Market Booms as Wealthy Buyers Shrug Off Pied-à-Terre Tax

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Manhattan luxury market is booming despite the Big Apple's looming pied-à-terre tax on second homes valued over $1 million.

Last week, 36 contracts were signed at $4 million and above, eight more than the previous week and the most since the first week of June, according to the Olshan luxury report.

The Upper East Side took the crown for the most popular area, and condos outsold co-ops—as they normally do. But this time, the gap between them was considerably smaller, with 19 condos sold, only six more than co-ops.

Co-ops not only tend to be a lot older than condos, but require an often-onerous board approval process. However, they are typically cheaper. The average asking price of a co-op at the luxury level last week was $6.8 million versus $9.4 million for a condo.

The top-selling property was a six-bedroom penthouse condo at 255 East 77th St. with an asking price of $23.75 million, or $4,303 per square foot.

A major lure here is no doubt the sweeping 36-degree views of Central Park from nearly all the rooms and the two terraces totaling 363 square feet. A six bedroom condo at this size—5,520 square feet—is rare, and the unit took only five months from listing to contract.

The Robert A.M. Stern Architects–designed building includes amenities such as a door attendant, concierge service, lounge, children's playroom, golf simulator, library, and automated parking system. The fitness center boasts a yoga room, spa with steam room and sauna, massage/treatment room, and the crown jewel: a 75-foot swimming pool.

Alexa Lambert of Compass held the listing.

Last week's second top sale, a sponsor condo at 53 W. 53rd St., had an asking price of $21.53 million. The 3,762-square-foot, three-bedroom unit, with north-facing views of Central Park, was sold on the same day it was listed, indicating it could have been an off-market deal.

The strong luxury market belies warnings that it would collapse after the pied-à-terre tax became effective on July 1.

For secondary-home condos and co-ops with a fair market value of $1 million to $3 million, the surcharge rate is 4.00%. For $3 million to $5 million, it is 5.25%, and for $5 million or greater, it is 6.50%. (Currently, the tax is collected annually and set to end on June 30, 2031.)

The rollout has been fraught with delays and legal challenges, and about 55% of those hit with a tax bill have thus far successfully appealed, according to The City.

Michael Reisor of the Compass-affiliated Reisor Team, who recently sold a $4.5 million loft condo at the Schumacher complex on Bleecker Street in Noho that will be used as a pied-à-terre, says the upcoming levy isn't stopping the wealthy from investing in Manhattan.

"Every type of product in Manhattan is only going to be getting more expensive as time goes on," he tells Realtor.com®. "New York will always be New York. If you're waiting out news on the pied-à-terre tax, you're going to be waiting too long, and you won't be able to catch up with the market."

Nor is New York City the only area seeking to increase taxes on the rich. Whether it's California's proposed Billionaire Tax or Washington's Millionaires Tax (effective January 2028), states looking to top off their coffers by taxing the wealthy in one form or another are increasing, leaving elite buyers with less choice on where to live if they want to avoid higher taxes.

"We're not seeing people not purchase the product they want because of fears of the tax, and the numbers this week reflect that," says the broker. "No one is going to throw out New York because of taxes."

Reisor says buyers might adjust their budget a little. But most affluent buyers would rather not dial down their lifestyle (and major asset), preferring instead to adjust their budget in other ways if needed.

He points to one client, owner of a pied-à-terre townhouse, who looked for something less expensive due to the new tax, but decided against it, preferring a property that met his wishes and standards.

Wealthy buyers "kind of poke their heads above water, look around, and go, 'Eh, I'll deal with the tax,'" says Reisor. "There's a price to being in Manhattan, one that people deem worth it."

Michelle Griffith of Douglas Elliman, who brought the buyer the city's second top sale two weeks ago, tells Realtor.com that luxury agents "are all sort of surprised by the strength of the luxury market."

Griffith puts the robust market down to two factors: lack of inventory and the exorbitant rental market.

"This is lack of inventory across all price points," she says. "You couple that with rents that are through the roof. That takes people who are living and working in Manhattan, with the kids in school, and pushes them into buying scenarios."

She says high-end apartments that would have previously rented for $25,000 a month are now entering bidding wars and going for near double that. Those who don't want to pay crushing monthly rents are forced into a buying market with slim inventory.

"For the longest time, people were saying 'Let's wait for more inventory to come on the market,'" she says, noting it is down almost 30% this fall. "We don't see new inventory."

She adds: "We're saying to our clients, 'Listen, there is no perfect apartment. If it checks 7 out of the 10 boxes, you need to move forward with it.'"

The broker also notes that buyers who had previously held out for turnkey apartments have changed their minds as their dream apartments failed to materialize.

"Those [turnkey] properties have disappeared," she says. "A lot of buyers are now saying, 'I'm not afraid to renovate. I'll take on a project.'"

Buyers are also willing to put in the renovations for their desired neighborhood, specifically the Upper East Side.

The high number of prewar co-ops that moved last week underscores that buyers are willing to take on renovations to get what they really want: prime location, layouts over 4,000 square feet, and at least three bedrooms.

The highest-selling co-op, a 1925 five-bedroom at 860 Park Ave. with an ask of $10,950,000, is described as "impeccably maintained," and co-ops at the ultraluxury (top 1%) tier, which in the borough starts at approximately $5.6 million, generally will not need much, if any, work.

But Griffith says high-end buyers on the whole are more willing to take on a project, provided the property checks a certain amount of boxes. For the right size, layout and location, they'll hire that contractor.

"It's showing long-term commitment to Manhattan," she says.
