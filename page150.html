#684. Damning data: stats that show Michael Carrick’s luck was bound to run out | Andrew Beasley

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Favourable conditions and the rub of the green were behind Manchester United’s successful finish to last season

Manchester United have been the third‑best team in the Premier League with Michael Carrick as their head coach. It is some effort for his job to be so under threat when only Arsenal (57 points) and Manchester City (56) have been more productive than United (46) during the 45-year-old’s tenure.

The tally obscures pertinent facts. United topping the Premier League during his 17‑match interim spell last season is irrelevant when they sit 16th with no win in eight games in all competitions this season. Losing narrowly against City is one thing, but three-goal defeats by Como and Bournemouth will surely spell managerial change.

Carrick can point to a degree of misfortune this season. Conceding stoppage-time equalisers to Everton and Tottenham was costly, although they also required an 89th-minute goal to avoid losing at Fulham, who were winless at the time.

Opta’s expected points model suggests United have earned 3.8 points fewer than their underlying statistical performances merit in 2026-27. Only three clubs are further adrift of where they can reasonably expect to be.

Equally, United’s expected position of 10th is hardly befitting a club whose net transfer spend this summer topped all those outside the Premier League bar Real Madrid, even before we consider their annual revenue and illustrious history. Their par level remains uninspiring.

If they have been a touch unlucky with results, it was almost an inevitable outcome. United outperformed their expected points by 12.7 in Carrick’s share of 2025-26, 7.6 more than any other club. Such fortune was never going to last, as any back office paying the remotest attention to data would know. Poor recruitment in the summer increased the likelihood of a slump.

The run of results appeared as unsustainable as it has proven. Eight of United’s 12 league wins under Carrick last season were by one goal. In that time, only Napoli (nine) had more such victories in Europe’s big five leagues. The expected goal margin was no more than 0.6 in United’s favour in five of those eight victories and they lost the xG by about 0.6 in their draws at West Ham and Sunderland. A lot of coin-flips went in their favour.

United’s woes are not simply the result of luck turning against them. They are incapable of breaking down teams when dominating the ball. Something that seemed insurmountable for Ole Gunnar Solskjær has also hampered Carrick. Meet the new overpromoted club legend, same as the old overpromoted club legend.

In Carrick’s first game, United won a Manchester derby despite having 32% possession. They were similarly ball-averse in victories against Liverpool (38%), Chelsea (40%) and Arsenal (43%) last season. Yet they have drawn blanks against Hull and Bournemouth in 2026-27 despite having 72% and 63% possession respectively. They were also unable to break down City when having a man advantage for three-quarters of the contest and 55% possession.

United lead the division for shots, but no team have had a higher proportion of their efforts from outside the penalty area, which explains why their non‑penalty expected goals per shot average is the second‑worst in the Premier League.

It is therefore fair to question Carrick’s tactical blueprint, as Bruno Fernandes did after defeat by Como. “Whatever system you play, whatever tactics you play, if you don’t run, and you don’t run forward, and you don’t run back, you ain’t going to win,” was Carrick’s response to his captain’s comments. A look at United’s physical data justifies his concern.

It is important to remember there is little correlation between distance covered and success. Arsenal lead the league for kilometres per match this season (119.4) but Fulham (119.0) are second. United are third-bottom, though their 112.3km per game average is up on the 102.2km they posted for Carrick in 2025-26.

Far more notable is the shift in sprints. Data from Gradient Sports shows United’s outfield players are averaging fewer sprints per 90 minutes than they did under Carrick last season. Youri Tielemans is not just below his figure for Aston Villa in 2025-26, he is barely at the level Casemiro offered despite being five years his junior.

The increase in average possession from 49.1% under Carrick last season to 56.7% may partially explain why United are sprinting less. However, City lead the Premier League for possession (64.7%) and average marginally fewer sprints than United, while Arsenal have the second-most ball (61.1%) and sprint more often. It seems unlikely United’s sprinting decline is entirely by design.

Have the players stopped trying for Carrick? Or do they simply no longer have the advantage of free weeks thanks to the Champions League qualification his success brought? Wherever the truth lies, intensity has dropped. The favourable circumstances of last season have gone and Carrick does not look up to the task of navigating a far more challenging campaign.

#290. Google Fitbit Edge Review: I Still Prefer the Air (2026)

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Earlier this year, the Google Fitbit Air won me over and made me wonder why I ever wanted a display on my fitness tracker in the first place. Of course, not everyone is ready to quit the screen cold turkey, especially if your wearable doubles as your everyday smartwatch. Google Fitbit hasn’t introduced a new tracker with a screen since 2023, and the older models are starting to fall behind on health features and hardware. That deters both newcomers who want a screen and longtime users who have to choose among aging models.

At $180, the new Google Fitbit Edge costs $20 more than Fitbit's Charge 6 ($160) and $80 more than the screenless Air ($100), but far less than Google's Pixel Watch 5 ($400). After 12 days of testing, I’d recommend it to Fitbit owners looking to upgrade. Personally, though, the Edge hasn’t given me enough reasons to give up my Fitbit Air.

The Edge is comfortable enough to wear throughout the day and overnight. Its dimensions are slightly larger than the Charge 6’s (39.2 x 25.5 x 11.5 mm versus 36.73 x 23.09 x 11.20 mm), but I found the capsule shape sat more naturally on my wrist than its predecessor’s rectangular build. It feels like a better option for people with slimmer wrists and those who might find the Pixel Watch or Apple Watch too bulky to sleep with.

As with any smartwatch, comfort also depends on the band. The Edge comes with Google’s Active Band, which is fine for workouts but isn’t my favorite of the available options. Google also offers the HNBR (high-performance synthetic elastomer) Active Sport, the breathable Performance Loop, the silicone Magnet band, and the Metal Links band.

I found myself alternating between the Metal Links and Performance Loop. I love the look of the former for a night out or a more formal setting, and the latter is best for everyday wear. The Performance Loop’s micro-adjustable fit makes it easy to get a precise fit, but it's just as easy to pull it a little too tight. I also noticed that the band occasionally popped loose while I was adjusting it. The tracker was still attached from one side, so I wasn’t worried about losing it, but the fastening mechanism didn’t feel as secure as it should. I eventually noticed that the locking button was stuck and had to fiddle with the mechanism to fix the issue.

The Fitbit Edge in brushed silver with Metal Links band (left), in Canyon with Active band (middle), and in Fog with Performance Loop band (right)

The 1.34-inch display is the Edge’s obvious advantage over the screenless Air. I noticed the difference most during workouts. On outdoor runs, I could check my metrics and control my music without slowing down. The display also reaches roughly twice the Charge’s maximum brightness, making it easier to read in direct sunlight. With Google Maps integration, I could follow turn-by-turn directions from my wrist while running or cycling, without having to pull out my phone.

I do prefer Wear OS’s more colorful interface; Fitbit’s watch faces also feel a little elementary by comparison. But Fitbit’s software offers up to seven days of battery life, which is a feature the Pixel Watch can’t match. The Edge is also compatible with both iOS and Android, unlike the Pixel Watch. Plus, in a first for Fitbit, the Edge is Google’s first repairable fitness tracker, with modular construction including a replaceable battery, matching the Pixel Watch. This is likely due to battery regulations set to take effect next year in the European Union, which will require removable and replaceable batteries in smartphones, smartwatches, and similar devices starting February 18, 2027.

The Edge is a significant upgrade over the Charge 6 when it comes to health and fitness capabilities. Google has added more detailed workout metrics and a few hardware upgrades that make the Edge a more capable fitness tracker.

Some of the additions are aimed at long-term health tracking. Like the Pixel Watch 5 and the Air, the Edge offers monthly blood pressure and insulin resistance trends. Its SpO2 readings now include nightly averages, ranges, and changes over time. There’s also 24/7 heart rate tracking, cardio load metrics, and an updated ECG app with atrial fibrillation (AFib) detection. Alongside nutrition logging, if you use a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), you can view glucose insights.

Like any Google wearable, most features are locked behind Google Health Premium, which costs $10 per month or $100 annually. Without the subscription, you still get the core metrics and scores, but the deeper analysis and AI coaching are inaccessible. In other words, the Google Health Premium membership is essential to the experience. (Buying any new device gets you a three-month trial.)

The hardware has also caught up to Google’s latest models. A barometric altimeter measures elevation changes and floors climbed. The Edge also finally gets an IP68 rating for dust and water resistance. Its display brings more of these metrics to your watch face, including live workout data and visual guidance.

Fitbit Edge bands (left to right): Olive Active Sport, Champagne Gold Metal Links, Obsidian Performance Loop, Mahogany Magnet, and Obsidian Active.

Google adds a gyroscope that improves automatic activity recognition, but it may have overcorrected a little. During outdoor runs, the Edge auto-paused when I stopped and resumed when I started moving again. At times, it was too sensitive; even taking a single step forward while catching my breath sometimes restarted my workout. It’s not a major downside, but worth noting that I sometimes had to correct the Edge’s decisions.

The Charge 6 supports contactless payments, but the Edge doesn’t, which feels like a regression for a newer model. If you want to use the Edge as a smartwatch, the lack of NFC technology is disappointing. I didn’t miss it much myself because I rarely leave home without my phone, but it’s a beloved feature that shouldn’t have disappeared without good reason.

The Fitbit Edge is a robust smartwatch, and Charge 6 owners will be happy with the improvements. I’m less convinced it’ll win over newcomers. The Fitbit Air remains the most appealing value in Google’s lineup, and the Edge doesn’t offer much more to change that. Without NFC for contactless payment, it feels like a smartwatch with one too many compromises.

#4988. Why Prediction Markets Got the Brazilian Election So Wrong

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Why Prediction Markets Got the Brazilian Election So Wrong

Prediction markets got the results of Brazil's presidential election wildly wrong, with Polymarket putting Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's chances at just 20% before election day, when he ultimately won out.

Experts say the whiff is the result of Brazil banning prediction markets in the country and a wave of retail investors diluting the expertise of the money on the line.

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, commonly referred to as Lula, beat right-wing rival Flávio Bolsonaro in the Brazilian presidential election on Sunday. Bolsonaro came out ahead after the first round of voting at the start of October, but hadn't reached a majority, and a second round of votes was required. That second round is what Lula won, with a majority this time, and he will serve his fourth term as president.

Going into election day, Lula hovered around a 20% chance of victory with Bolsonaro sitting at 80%, per Polymarket. Even once polls and results started to roll out, one Polymarket whale told Decrypt that the market was slow to push up the odds.

"Sharps [a term for professional, high-stakes predictors] knew the market was mispriced, probably closer to 30-40% on [event] day. Mispricing was most evident during live trading, Lula was nearly a lock by 6:15 pm ET when Lula ‘Yes’ was 60c," pseudonymous Polymarket whale FhantomBets said. "Retail traders were so against Lula that you could get unlimited size at 60-80c when Lula had [essentially] already won."

Prediction markets settle at $1, with prices below that—such as $0.60 on a “yes” bet for Lula in Brazil’s election—providing implied odds of that event occurring.

The Polymarket trader added that many whales, including himself, were selling off long-term positions to get liquid capital so they could add more size to the Brazil market. Essentially, FhantomBets is claiming that retail traders with less prediction market experience inflated Bolsonaro's chances right until the very end.

Luke Nolan, senior researcher at CoinShares, pushed back on this notion and said Polymarket results reacted as polling results were released throughout the night.

"I don’t think more users are inherently a problem. They can bring liquidity and different perspectives, including local knowledge. What matters is whether participation brings different information, and whether traders can act on it," Nolan told Decrypt. "I would be wary of explaining the result simply as professionals being right and retail being wrong."

Instead, Nolan suggested Brazil’s ban on prediction markets is likely a bigger reason why the market was so harshly mispriced. Einar Knobel, CEO and co-founder of sportsbook and prediction market firm TxODDS, agreed.

"The price was set mostly by people outside the country," Knobel told Decrypt. "They were trading the first-round result, poll headlines, and the story built around them."

Brazil issued a sweeping ban against prediction market platforms in April, citing investor protection concerns and the rise of gambling addiction in the nation. Finance Minister Dario Durigan said the platforms violated betting regulations and lacked proper regulatory oversight.

Knobel added that liquidity was also relatively thin, with the market attracting just 4.5% of the volume that the 2024 U.S. presidential election garnered. "With fewer participants and almost no local knowledge in the book, little was there to correct the price," Knobel said.

Nolan believes predictors put too much weight on Bolsonaro's first-round performance, underestimated the increase in voter turnout for the second round, and assumed how voters would transfer based on the eliminated candidates.

"That said, Lula was priced at around 20% before the vote," Nolan said. "That is still some form of meaningful chance of winning. I think there are good reasons to question whether the market underestimated him. Ultimately, something that has an 80% chance of happening does not happen 20% of the time."

The mispricing of the Brazilian election was a significant upset, but Nolan cautioned against seeing it as a massive failure. He said prediction markets continue to price things better than traditional betting platforms—pointing to Betfair giving Hillary Clinton an 83% chance the day before the 2016 U.S. election.

"Across enough events, outcomes given a 20% chance should happen roughly 20% of the time. We should also compare their performance with other forecasts available at the same time," Nolan told Decrypt. "The concern would be a persistent pattern of overconfidence or a failure to incorporate relevant information, rather than an individual upset."

NASA has officially ended the mission of its Swift space telescope, after a private satellite failed in its bold bid to rescue the aging spacecraft.

That rescue mission was led by the Arizona company Katalyst Space Technologies, whose LINK spacecraft tried to meet up with Swift this summer to boost its orbit.

That didn't work out, however, so Swift continues its fall back toward Earth. The telescope is expected to reenter Earth's atmosphere "in the next few weeks," NASA said in a statement on Friday (Oct. 23).

That statement served primarily to announce the end of the Swift mission after more than 20 very productive years.

The telescope launched to Earth orbit in November 2004, tasked with studying gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) — the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. Swift was designed to operate for two years, but it continued studying the high-energy universe for more than two decades.

"In that time, Swift played a major role in advancing our understanding of the changing universe and was part of a revolution in astrophysics that showed how critical rapid-response observations are to the entire community," Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of the Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington, said in the statement.

NASA had hoped to keep Swift going even longer. In September 2025, the agency awarded a contract to the Arizona company Katalyst Space Technologies to rescue the observatory, which was falling back to Earth thanks to atmospheric drag.

Swift has no thrusters capable of counteracting this drag. So Katalyst built a refrigerator-sized spacecraft called LINK, which aimed to rendezvous with Swift, grapple it with three robotic arms and boost it to a higher orbit, allowing the observatory to continue its science work.

NASA and Katalyst stressed from the start that success was far from guaranteed. After all, LINK's mission was unprecedented: No private spacecraft had ever captured and boosted a satellite that wasn't designed to be serviced in this way. And the rate of Swift's orbital decay demanded a rapid timeline: LINK launched on July 3, a little over nine months after Katalyst won the $30 million NASA Swift-rescue contract.

LINK was not able to execute its very challenging mission. About three weeks after liftoff, it suffered problems with its propulsion system and orientation-maintaining reaction wheels, which caused it to start spinning at an unexpected rate. These issues prevented LINK from capturing and boosting Swift, but the private craft did manage to get within 7.5 to 9.3 miles (12 to 15 kilometers) of the observatory and photograph it.

"From the beginning, this was a high-risk, high-reward mission," Domagal-Goldman said in a statement on Sept. 25, the day that LINK fell back to Earth.

"And while we'll be sad to see Swift's mission come to a close, we knew this boost effort would be worthwhile either way — advancing U.S. spacecraft servicing technology, challenging us to meet unprecedented mission timelines, and testing how we operate satellites to extend their time in low Earth orbit," he added.

Swift — which was officially renamed the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory in 2018, after its first principal investigator, who died a year earlier — gathered its last scientific data on Oct. 20, according to NASA's Friday update.

During its long and storied career, the space telescope observed more than 2,000 GRBs. And it did other work, too, studying more than 1,400 supernova explosions, as well as eyeing asteroids, comets and black holes and conducting an all-sky survey in X-ray light. The mission's data has contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers to date, according to NASA officials.

And that number is sure to keep rising far into the future.

"Swift leaves us with an extraordinary archive of multiwavelength observations that will fuel new discoveries for decades more," Domagal-Goldman said in the Oct. 23 statement.
