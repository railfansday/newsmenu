Honda’s built a reputation on engines that usually outlast its owners. The Shea family’s 2017 Honda Pilot had covered just 56,000 miles and was diagnosed with rod bearing failure last year, just out of warranty. The dealership quoted nearly $25,000 for a replacement engine, more than what it would cost to just buy another Pilot of the same year. Honda declined to help, even though the issue is linked to one the NHTSA is investigating over 1.4 million Honda and Acura models with V6 engines for. But when a couple of elected officials and a television news team got involved, the story flipped: Honda finally picked up the bill for the Shea’s new engine.

The pressure started with 7News, which reported on the family’s predicament last year and stayed on it. Senator Chris Van Holler and Representative Jamie Raskin then contacted Honda and the NHTSA. Raskin sent the automaker six questions about the engine failure and its decision not to cover the repair. Honda then replied, saying it would recover the engine and put in a new one at no cost. Honda’s statement to 7News said inspecting the engine could help its ongoing investigation, which isn’t really an admission of fault either.

The NHTSA is investigating connecting rod bearing failures across roughly 1.4 million Honda and Acura vehicles, and seven crashes or fires have been documented. No recall has been issued yet. A 2023 NHTSA recall covering about 250,000 vehicles from the 2016, 2018, and 2019 model years left 2017 out, which is exactly where the Shea family’s Pilot sat. Had the family paid for the replacement engine, they would have been out $25,000 for a failure regulators were already studying. Congress may need to legislate tighter investigation timelines so that other owners who aren’t so lucky don’t end up paying for a company’s mistakes.

#3551. Prediction: This Artificial Intelligence (AI) Memory Stock Will Go Parabolic on Oct. 30

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As technology companies race to build data centers, demand for the memory chips that make artificial intelligence (AI) computing possible has skyrocketed. One of the leading manufacturers of memory chips for AI hyperscalers is Micron Technology (MU +2.77%).

So far this year, Micron stock has soared 225% -- making it the third-highest performer in the Nasdaq-100. Nevertheless, Micron's shares have experienced considerable volatility over the last couple of months and trade about 24% below their all-time high.

I think this disconnect is about to reverse. On Oct. 28 and 29, Alphabet (GOOGL +1.19%) (GOOG +0.84%) and Amazon (AMZN +1.61%) are scheduled to report quarterly earnings. While investors will undoubtedly focus on their respective AI-related capital expenditures (capex), I think another topic could emerge as a major discussion point: the global memory shortage. If my prediction is accurate, Micron could begin to rally just in time for Halloween.

For most of the AI revolution, investors primarily associated infrastructure with graphics processing units (GPUs) designed by Nvidia and application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) designed by Broadcom. However, GPUs and custom silicon cannot operate efficiently without sufficient memory. Micron manufactures dynamic random access memory (DRAM) and high bandwidth memory (HBM), both of which are critical components of the AI chip stack.

Interestingly, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy already highlighted the growing importance of memory during the company's last earnings call. Jassy explained that Amazon planned to increase its 2026 capex budget to $220 billion -- up from its previous forecast of $200 billion -- specifically because of higher memory costs.

Meanwhile, Alphabet raised its 2026 capex forecast to $195 billion to $205 billion during its second-quarter earnings call. Management attributed the increase to accelerating infrastructure investments and capacity supply constraints. Although Alphabet did not explicitly identify memory as the culprit, its comments reinforce the underlying idea that demand for AI compute is insatiable.

As both companies prepare to report earnings this week, investors should pay close attention to any additional commentary surrounding memory availability, pricing, and infrastructure spending.

Amazon and Alphabet aren't the only companies confronting the memory shortage. Earlier this month, Elon Musk took to X (formerly Twitter) to discuss the bottlenecks facing AI infrastructure build-outs. Musk specifically identified RAM supply as a constraint, supporting the idea that memory availability is becoming just as important as access to advanced processors.

This distinction matters because the AI infrastructure boom is moving beyond simply procuring as many GPUs as possible. AI models need memory to support training and inference. Training involves teaching AI to recognize patterns, while inference occurs when those models are deployed to perform an actual task rather than just answer a query. Both activities require substantial memory, particularly as applications become more complex across autonomous systems, robotics, and agentic AI.

A prolonged memory shortage could create favorable conditions for Micron. When demand for a product exceeds available supply, manufacturers can command higher prices. For Micron, this is already happening and translating into accelerating revenue growth, expanding profit margins, and earnings visibility.

One of the biggest headwinds dragging on Micron stock right now is skepticism about the semiconductor industry's boom-and-bust cycles and whether hyperscalers can maintain the pace of their infrastructure spending. I think Amazon's and Alphabet's upcoming earnings reports will help answer that question.

If leadership explicitly emphasizes the memory shortage, rising component prices, or the increasing demand for memory-intensive AI applications, investors may begin to view Micron's growth as more durable than anticipated.

My prediction is that earnings season will become a turning point for Micron stock. If the world's largest AI developers stress the industry's memory shortage, investors should recognize that Micron's opportunity extends well beyond this year. With shares still trading meaningfully below their all-time high, positive commentary could provide a much-needed catalyst for a new rally.

Scientists are still learning about a cosmic "string of pearls," a unique celestial relic left by a supernova explosion that humanity witnessed around 850 years ago.

That is thanks to the most detailed observation of the dandelion-shaped chains of gas and supernova debris that comprise Pa 30, the only supernova remnant of its type observed in the Milky Way. What makes Pa 30 even more unique is that it has a rare "zombie star" at its heart.

Supernovas like the one that created Pa 30 are called Type Ia supernovas; they happen when a dead star called a white dwarf overfeeds on a companion star like a cosmic vampire. This overfeeding triggers a runaway nuclear explosion that usually destroys the white dwarf. But on rare occasions, the supernova doesn't completely destroy the star, and a shattered stellar body remains, called a zombie star. These events are called Type Iax supernovas.

Pa 30 was first identified in 2013 by a citizen scientist in 2013, but scientists soon linked it to a cosmic explosion recorded independently in the year 1181 in Japanese, Chinese, and Arabic historical documentation. That makes this one of only five supernovas seen before the invention of the telescope.

"Reliable historical records of stellar explosions extend back only about a thousand years. For all we know, there might well be similar remnants still lurking in the dark in our own galaxy," team member Ilaria Caiazzo, assistant professor at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria (ISTA), said in a statement.

"Today, we are lucky to study an intriguing object we know was observed by astronomers who made three independent historical records of the event centuries before the invention of the telescope."

Pa 30 is a good laboratory to study supernovas because, at around 7,500 light-years away, it is relatively close to Earth on cosmic scales. For instance, the heart of our galaxy is around 26,000 light-years away. "We can see this much detail because of Pa 30's proximity to Earth," said Caiazzo. "This proximity makes Pa 30 uniquely valuable for study."

It is also an important subject of study because it is the only supernova remnant in which we can see a zombie star.

Using the Gemini North telescope, Caiazzo and colleagues zoomed in on Pa 30, discovering knots along the strands of gas radiating out from the site of the cosmic explosion, like pearls on a string.

These pearls are each wide enough to encompass the orbit of Neptune, the most distant planet from the sun, ten times over.

"The planetary region of our solar system could fit in each knot about ten times with room to spare," said Caiazzo. "The knots are quite strikingly uniform. We are excited to try to model them."

The team also discovered that the zombie star sits almost perfectly at the heart of Pa 30. When these cosmic explosions happen, they should provide any debris, including zombie stars, with a kick. The fact that this "living dead" star still sits dead center of this wreckage suggests that when this explosion happened, it was roughly symmetrical and the kick was relatively light.

"Explosions conserve momentum. If the explosion itself was perfectly symmetric, the ejecta should also be symmetric, and the surviving star should not receive a substantial kick," Caiazzo said. "We were able to measure a precise upper limit of the remnant star's transverse kick speed, showing that it was surprisingly small. We practically don't see the star moving away at all."

While there are still many mysteries surrounding Pa 30 and its zombie occupant, one of the most exciting prospects emerging from this research is the possibility it will help identify the wreckage of other Type Iax supernovas in our galaxy.

"While we can see Type Iax supernovas in distant galaxies as point sources of light, resolving the remnants of such explosions is only possible in our own galaxy and in very few nearby ones," Caiazzo concluded. "Eventually, we plan to use Pa 30's distinctive properties to search the Milky Way and nearby galaxies for similar objects."The team's research was published in The Astrophysical Journal.
