#4878. Women With Enlarged Breasts More Likely to Experience Migraine, Sleep Apnea

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

  • A new study found that women with macromastia (enlarged breasts) had higher odds of chronic migraine, neck pain, and obstructive sleep apnea than women without the condition. 
  • The findings suggest that macromastia may lead to more than physical discomfort, potentially contributing to multiple health conditions that affect quality of life. 
  • Women with macromastia experiencing frequent headaches, neck pain, or poor sleep quality should talk with their doctor to learn about their treatment options. 
  • General macromastia is often treated with breast reduction surgery, but other strategies, such as a supportive bra and physical therapy, may help alleviate discomfort.

Enlarged breast tissue, known medically as macromastia or breast hypertrophy, is relatively common among women.

The exact prevalence of the condition is not fully understood, but some research estimates that macromastia affects around 1–5% of women.

This estimate climbs after menopause, with around 18.6% of women experiencing an increase in breast size, which may be linked to weight gain and hormonal shifts.

However, macromastia can occur during other life stages, such as puberty or pregnancy.

Women with disproportionately large breasts often experience discomfort and pain, and may turn to breast reduction surgery for relief. 

Now, new research suggests that macromastia is associated with more than just physical pain.

A cross-sectional study found that enlarged breasts are associated with higher rates of chronic migraine, neck pain, cervical radiculopathy (a pinched nerve in the neck), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

The findings, published September 22 in Headache: The Journal of Head and Face Pain, add to a growing body of research examining how macromastia may affect overall health and quality of life. 

“Many people think of enlarged breast tissue primarily as a cosmetic issue, but our findings suggest it may be associated with a broader range of women’s health concerns, including chronic migraine, neck pain and sleep-related breathing disorders,” lead study author Kristyn Pocock, MD, assistant professor of neurology at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, said in a news release.

“By better understanding these connections, we can help ensure women receive more comprehensive evaluations and care for symptoms that may affect their daily lives.” 

Macromastia linked to 40% higher prevalence of chronic migraine

To better understand how macromastia may impact quality of life, researchers reviewed medical records from 687 females treated at headache and neurology clinics affiliated with Stanford Medical Center from 2017 to 2024. 

The cohort included 347 females with macromastia and 340 without.

After accounting for factors including age, body mass index (BMI), depression, and anxiety, researchers found that those with macromastia were:

  • 40% more likely to have chronic migraine (a headache on at least 15 days per month for more than 3 months)
  • roughly twice as likely to experience neck pain
  • 54% more likely to have obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)
  • more than twice as likely to have cervical radiculopathy, a condition caused by irritation or compression of nerves in the neck

The study was a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis, meaning researchers reviewed existing medical records rather than following participants over time.

This means the study does not prove that macromastia causes migraine, neck pain, or sleep apnea, warranting further investigation.

“These findings raise important questions about how enlarged breast tissue may contribute to painful symptoms for women,” Pocock said. “More research is needed to determine whether treating macromastia could help certain women find relief and enhance quality of life.” 

Why macromastia may affect the neck, head, and sleep

Researchers don’t yet have an explanation for why macromastia is associated with these health conditions.

One possibility, Pocock previously suggested, is mechanical strain. 

Larger breasts can place additional load on the neck, shoulders, and upper back, which may contribute to musculoskeletal pain and nerve irritation. 

Pocock has also discussed how other factors may play a role, such as:

  • inflammation
  • hormonal effects
  • mood changes
  • physical activity
  • sleep quality

Further studies are needed to determine the exact role these cofounding factors may play in the possible health effects of macromastia.

Neck pain may drive breast reduction treatment decisions

The researchers examined breast reduction surgery among people with macromastia. 

Of the symptoms studied, neck pain was significantly associated with undergoing breast reduction surgery, known as a reduction mammoplasty. 

Migraine, sleep apnea, and cervical radiculopathy were not significantly associated with this surgical treatment. 

This finding raises questions about whether some symptoms associated with macromastia may be overlooked when people seek treatment, suggesting another avenue for further research.

Could breast reduction improve symptoms?

Preliminary evidence indicates that breast reduction surgery may help some people with headaches, but the evidence is still limited.

In a small 2025 prospective pilot study, which was also co-authored by Pocock, 34 women with symptomatic macromastia and headaches underwent breast reduction. At follow-up, 79% reported at least a 50% reduction in monthly headache days. Headache frequency, severity, and related disability also improved. 

Another pilot study led by Pocock published in 2026 found improvements in headache frequency, severity, disability, and sleep apnea risk after surgery. The sample size for this study was also small, with 34 participants, highlighting the need for further research.

The researchers also emphasized that breast reduction should not yet be considered a treatment for headaches because larger studies are still needed. 

Sherry Ross, MD, co-founder of Oneself, Intimate Skin Care, and the Pair-a-Docs Podcast, said that women with macromastia experiencing chronic pain may wish to consider breast reduction surgery.

“In my 30+ years as a practicing OB-GYN, I have never met anyone who regretted having breast reduction surgery,” she told Healthline. 

Talking with your doctor about macromastia

If you live with macromastia and experience frequent headaches, persistent neck or shoulder pain, numbness or tingling, loud snoring, or significant daytime fatigue, it’s important to discuss all of your symptoms with your doctor. 

A comprehensive evaluation can help guide you to the treatment that you need, whether it’s breast reduction surgery or other methods. 

Your healthcare team may recommend a more supportive bra, physical therapy, or an NSAID or other pain reliever to help you find relief. 

“The most important step in improving your comfort and health is to get a properly fitted bra,” Ross said. 

“There are dedicated bra stores that can properly fit larger-breasted women with full-coverage, well-supported bras, with and without underwire… both for everyday wear and for exercise.”

Ross emphasized the importance of proper breast support for regular physical activity, a first-line defense against other health conditions like obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. 

She recommended exercises that are better suited to larger breasts to avoid excessive breast bouncing and discomfort, such as: 

  • swimming
  • cycling
  • walking
  • strength and resistance training

“Strength training that focuses on the upper body and core is helpful as well,” Ross said. “Select workout clothing that is comfortable, breathable, and moisture-wicking to avoid excessive sweat and moisture buildup.”

Lastly, Ross advised maintaining a healthy weight and eating a well-balanced, plant-forward diet to support overall health and longevity.

#4870. NBA Star Steph Curry Didn’t Realize His Blurry Vision Was a Sign of an Eye Disease

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

  • NBA All-Star Stephen Curry is opening up about living with keratoconus, an eye condition that can cause blurry or distorted vision.
  • Curry was diagnosed after former Golden State Warriors general manager Bob Myers noticed him squinting during a game and encouraged him to get his eyes checked.
  • Keratoconus can worsen over time, but early diagnosis and treatment can help correct vision and may help prevent the condition from progressing.

Twelve-time NBA All-Star and two-time MVP Stephen Curry is known for leading the Golden State Warriors to four NBA championships.

In addition to basketball accolades, being part of the Warriors dynasty made him aware of an eye condition he was living with called keratoconus (KC).

The condition causes the cornea to thin and bulge outward into an irregular cone shape instead of staying round.

This can cause blurry or distorted vision, increased sensitivity to light, glare and nighttime halos, frequent or rapid changes in eyeglass prescription, and eye irritation or redness.

“I didn’t know anything about it before I went to the doctor, nor did I really know that I had truly blurry vision or, like, distorted images and stuff,” Curry told Healthline. “It was kind of just the way I saw things, and for whatever reason, I didn’t ask the questions or go have an eye screen and all that until our general manager for the Warriors, his name’s Bob Myers, [pointed it out].”

In 2018, Myers was watching Curry play on TV and noticed that when the camera zoomed in on Curry sitting on the bench, he was squinting as he watched his teammates and looked at the Jumbotron. Myers took a photo of Curry squinting on the TV and texted it to him after the game.

“He’s like, ‘Hey, you were doing this the whole time they had you on camera. Have you gotten your eyes checked?’” said Curry. “And then that was the first idea of like, ‘Wait, is there something off about the way, you know, my vision was?’ Thankfully, I had access to go to an eye doctor and got a screen. And that was the first time I heard the word keratoconus.”

How treatment for keratoconus changed Curry’s vision

At 29 years old, Curry was diagnosed with KC and learned that it’s a progressive eye disease that can worsen over time.

“[There’s] a certain time window of your life [when it becomes] an irreversible sight-threatening condition,” he said.

Randy McLaughlin, OD, associate professor of clinical ophthalmology at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said the condition can worsen if not treated and may make obtaining a driver’s license or working difficult.

He said people with the condition are usually fitted with rigid gas permeable contact lenses, which are firm lenses made of breathable plastic and silicone that allow oxygen to pass directly through to the cornea. Some people get larger scleral contact lenses, which are wide enough to vault over the cone-shaped cornea and rest on the white of the eye.

“Patients may be treated with a medical therapy called corneal cross-linking, which hopefully stops the progression of keratoconus,” McLaughlin told Healthline.

Once Curry figured out corrective lenses, he said, “I could actually see clearly and stop squinting, stop having those weird little headaches that would come, that I didn’t associate with my vision.”

While it took some trial and error to get his lens prescription correct and feel stable enough to play basketball, once he did, he couldn’t believe the difference in his vision.

“[I] was the most annoying person probably that first two weeks, ‘cause any time I walked in the room, either I’d be awkwardly staring at you, ‘cause I can see you clearly, or making a comment [like], ‘I can see all the dust in the air, like, this is incredible,” Curry said.

On the court, he could see his teammates better.

“[It] was just a whole new world, for sure,” he said. “[I] realized for the longest [time] I was just adapting and adjusting to what I saw.”

Curry continues to have eye screenings twice a year to monitor the condition.

Keratoconus symptoms and why early diagnosis matters

KC is most often diagnosed during puberty and the teenage years and progresses during a person’s twenties. Delayed diagnosis may increase the risk of irreversible vision loss.

McLaughlin said KC is easily diagnosed at an eye doctor’s appointment with an instrument called a keratometer. However, additional testing may be required.

Your eye doctor may test for it if they notice sudden or severe changes in your astigmatism or nearsightedness, if your vision hasn’t improved with standard eyeglasses, or if you have an irregular corneal reflection.

Although the condition is not inherited in most cases, it can occur in some families.

“Family history of every patient is important, including any family history of keratoconus,” said McLaughlin.

To spread awareness about keratoconus and the importance of annual eye exams, Curry partnered with Glaukos for its Could it be KC? campaign.

Because KC disproportionately affects Black and Hispanic populations, he especially hopes to reach these communities.

“[The] reach that I have and the authentic story of what this has kind of been like for me is the reason I’m doing it,” Curry said. “The work that me and my wife are doing in our foundation in Oakland — the kids that we’re serving — this is a message that they need to hear.”

To connect with more young people, he took part in an educational community event at Madison Park Primary in Oakland on Wednesday, September 23, 2026. At the event, he introduced a new picture book, “Eye on the Ball,” inspired by Curry’s own experience living with KC, and designed to make conversations about eye health and KC more approachable.

“You want to give a visual, especially for kids, to kind of destigmatize what it means to go to a doctor, ask uncomfortable questions, be in that environment where it can be overwhelming at times for sure,” said Curry.

He empathizes with children and people of all ages who are living with undiagnosed KC.

“I can only imagine [because] the way that I saw the court, sometimes struggling to see the screen to watch film, like, little things that you just adapt to and figure out, for younger kids, high school kids, college, [those] in the workforce, you realize…that’s energy exerted,” Curry said. “It’s a hurdle that shouldn’t necessarily be a thing that you have to deal with, but it’s only going to get corrected if you have the awareness to go get it checked.”

He encourages people to see an eye doctor if something doesn’t feel right.

“[There] were plenty of moments for me in that respect, where… things are designed a certain way to be able to see them, and if you can’t, you have to make adjustments to do it, or you have to ask somebody, ‘You know, what does that say up there?’”

Curry hopes that sharing his story encourages more people to schedule eye doctor visits and that more people with KC get the care they need.

“[Hopefully their] outcome is similar to mine, as well, where it’s just a matter of getting checked and getting the right answers for what your situation is,” he said.

#4853. Simone Biles Thought Her Itchy, Flaky Scalp Was Dandruff. It Was Eczema

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

  • Olympic gold medalist Simone Biles is opening up about living with eczema.
  • After experiencing itching and flaking on her skin and scalp, Biles saw a dermatologist who diagnosed her with atopic dermatitis and seborrheic dermatitis.
  • Biles hopes that sharing her experience will help others with eczema feel less ashamed and encourage them to seek care.

Olympic gold medalist Simone Biles is the most decorated gymnast in history. But the beloved icon is taking a break from talking about the balance beam and vault to discuss a personal health condition that she lives with alongside 31 million fellow Americans: eczema.

“A couple years ago, I started noticing flaking and itching on my scalp, especially on the crown of my head. And at first, I thought it was a lot of dandruff, and then I was worried it could be something contagious,” Biles told Healthline. “I’ve also dealt with patches of dry skin on my arm, but it comes and goes.”

Recently, she noticed her symptoms getting worse, especially when stressed from training.

“I would definitely be in the gym, finding myself waiting for bars or for beam and just scratching and scratching and [people] would be like, ‘Simone, can you stop scratching?’ and I’m like, ‘Oh, yeah, my bad, sorry,’” said Biles.

When her hairdresser noticed scaly patches on her scalp, Biles began searching online for answers and trying home remedies, including combs, shampoos, and oils.

“[Self-fixes] weren’t really helping, and I didn’t go to the dermatologist immediately because I was busy in the gym training,” said Biles. “I also felt ashamed and embarrassed, so I started speaking out about it at Paris Olympics.”

After making a TikTok video about her symptoms, she was inspired to see a dermatologist who diagnosed her with the skin conditions seborrheic dermatitis and atopic dermatitis (also known as eczema).

“[A] lot of what she is communicating really mirrors what we’re seeing in patients and what we know about these conditions,” Angela Lamb, MD, Bile’s dermatologist, told Healthline.

While eczema is a group of chronic inflammatory skin conditions, Lamb said stress can trigger flares, as well as extremes of weather, especially for those with atopic dermatitis.

“And then there’s just individual triggers, so patients really need to listen to their bodies, find out what moisturizer would work well for their skin, find out if you have seborrheic dermatitis on your scalp,” said Lamb. “[Know] what are the products that tend to be flaring it or what you do that makes it better or worse.”

How Biles found relief from her eczema symptoms

Biles says she found relief for her symptoms with Zoryve, a once-daily, steroid-free, topical prescription medication that comes in both foam and cream forms. Biles uses both forms: the foam for her scalp, and the cream for her skin.

“I feel like it fits in easily to my lifestyle…you can apply it to your scalp, and it’s really, really lightweight, and then for my eczema, it’s a cream, and it’s non-greasy,” said Biles.

In addition to helping her feel more comfortable, she said that receiving effective treatment eased the embarrassment she experienced around the appearance of her skin.

“[Going] on a lot of shoots and a lot of sets, [I was] definitely more self-conscious about my parts or if I was wearing braids — the flaking, what that looks like on camera — so I would just try to cover it up with oils and all of that stuff,” she said.

To share her journey, Biles partnered with Zoryve’s maker, Arcutis Biotherapeutics, to launch its Skin to Believe In campaign. The initiative aims to break the stigma around chronic skin conditions that can make people feel self-conscious, isolated, or embarrassed.

“I know people are going through similar things, thinking it’s dandruff or thinking it’s contagious,” Biles said. “[You] don’t have to silently suffer. Own it. Go see a trusted professional, and get the relief that you deserve.”

Because every medication has potential side effects, Lamb said it’s important to talk with your doctor about a medication’s potential risks and benefits.

“This one, the side effects are in a very low percent of patients, which can include some nausea, headache, as well as an allergic reaction, but you’ll discuss that with your physician, and in this case, dermatologists are the main ones writing for Zoryve,” said Lamb.

She said it’s encouraging that nonsteroidal medications exist as options for patients with skin conditions. She explained that Zoryve contains roflumilast, a PDE4 inhibitor that helps reduce inflammation involved in eczema.

“So I’m very excited [because] I think the landscape of how we’re treating many conditions is really changing as we understand the science and the core of what drives it better,” said Lamb. “I think that’s been the largest breakthrough in the last many years in healthcare…understanding root cause is better, which can lead us to better treatments.”

How Biles reduces stress to help manage her eczema

Because Biles believes stress can contribute to her eczema flares, she practices a wellness routine that includes spending time with her girlfriends to get their nails done or a massage, and taking baths at night to wind down.

“[But] honestly, without training every single day, I don’t feel as stressed,” she said. “[Everyone] can find different ways to alleviate their stress. And it looks different for everyone.”

As she approaches her 30th birthday, Biles says she’s learned to prioritize her physical and mental well-being.

“I feel like now I know that there is help out there if I do get stuck or I notice something, and I feel like maturity has helped a lot with that,” she said.

“And I just feel like being an ambassador and having awareness for so much has opened up the doors for me to say, ‘Hey, I’m not feeling okay. Can I please do this instead?’ Or, ‘Oh, I need to speak to my therapist twice a week because I feel like I’m stuck in a rut right now.’ So I feel like [aging] has given me space and… confidence to do that.”

#4763. Oral GLP-1 Orforglipron (Foundayo) Achieves Weight Loss for Women of All Ages

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

  • A new analysis of previous studies found that orforglipron promoted weight loss in women regardless of their menopausal status.
  • This is important since the decline in estrogen after menopause can make weight loss more difficult.
  • Menopausal women also tend to gain weight around their midsection, which is associated with health risks.
  • The fact that the new GLP-1 comes in a pill form offers a convenient option for many women.
  • However, doctors stress that it is also important to select the treatment that is the most effective for you.

A new GLP-1 pill called orforglipron (Foundayo) has been shown to help women lose weight, regardless of menopausal status, according to new research.

A group of researchers, including senior author Hunter Thomas Hoffman, PharmD, a clinical research scientist affiliated with the drug’s manufacturer, Eli Lilly, analyzed data from two prior trials, ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2, to arrive at this conclusion.

These studies reveal that this medication works equally well for women who have not yet gone through menopause, those who are currently experiencing it, and those who are postmenopausal. 

The findings were presented at the Annual Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD), held in Milan, Italy, from September 28 to October 2.

This is important because menopause often causes weight gain due to lower estrogen levels, making weight loss more difficult for many women. 

Orforglipron offers a promising new option for managing weight in women with obesity or overweight, especially those with type 2 diabetes.

In April of this year, Eli Lilly’s Foundayo received FDA approval as a weight-loss drug, making it the second GLP-1 pill for weight loss to enter the U.S. market after Novo Nordisk’s Wegovy.

Examining menopause and weight loss with orforglipron

The ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies involved women who were either obese or overweight and who had at least one medical condition related to their weight. 

Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher, and overweight as a BMI of 27 or higher. 

The participants were divided into groups based on their menopausal status: premenopausal (before menopause), perimenopausal (during the menopause transition), and postmenopausal (after menopause). 

Each group included between 142 and 225 females who either received orforglipron or placebo, an inactive pill with none of the drug in it.

The ATTAIN-1 study focused on people who were overweight or obese but did not have diabetes. ATTAIN-2 included participants who had both overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes. 

The purpose of analyzing these two groups separately was to understand how orforglipron works in people with different health conditions.

The researchers measured several outcomes over a period of 72 weeks. They examined changes in body weight, waist circumference (the distance around the waist), and the waist-to-height ratio, a measure used to assess body fat distribution. 

They also tracked how many women reached specific weight loss goals, such as losing at least 5%, 10%, 15%, or 20% of their body weight, compared to those who took the inactive pill.

By studying these factors, the researchers were able to determine not only whether orforglipron helped women lose weight but also how much weight loss was achieved and how it affected their body shape.

Up to 14% weight loss with orforglipron regardless of menopause status

The results showed that women who took orforglipron experienced significant weight loss regardless of their menopausal status. 

In the ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies, premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women all lost an average of up to 14% of their body weight. 

This means that the medication was effective for women at different life stages, including those experiencing menopause, when weight gain is often a challenge.

In addition to losing body weight, women in the orforglipron groups also saw meaningful reductions in waist circumference. Waist measurements decreased by up to 12.5 centimeters (about 5 inches) across all menopausal groups in both studies. 

This reduction is important because greater visceral fat is associated with higher risks of heart disease and diabetes.

The waist-to-height ratio also improved for many women taking orforglipron. More participants treated with the medication moved into lower waist-to-height ratio categories than those who took the placebo. 

This indicates a healthier distribution of body fat and suggests a lower risk of heart disease.

When looking at the number of women who reached specific weight loss milestones, orforglipron performed much better than the placebo. Up to 83% of women on orforglipron lost at least 5% of their body weight, while 30% of women on placebo reached that same level of weight loss. 

Losing 5% or more of body weight is often considered clinically meaningful, as it can lead to improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels.

Overall, these findings suggest that orforglipron is a promising new medication for weight management in women with obesity or overweight, including those who also have type 2 diabetes. 

This can be especially helpful for those navigating the challenges of menopause while trying to manage their weight.

Oral GLP-1 offers convenient weight loss option for women

James J. Chao, MD, FACS, Chief Medical Officer and co-founder of VedaNu Wellness, wasn’t involved in the research, but said the significance of these findings for postmenopausal women.

“The 14% is what’s grabbing headlines,” he told Healthline, “but up to 12.5 cm from the waistline is what I’m interested in.”

According to Chao, as females enter postmenopause, they tend to store more fat around their midsection.

“This is the type of fat that can cause heart disease,” he said. “So a pill that can help a 38-year-old woman lose the same amount as a 62-year-old breaks that myth.”

Additionally, in contrast to most GLP-1 drugs, which are injectable, it’s a once-a-day pill. You can also take it any time, Chao explained, making it more convenient.

“No needle, no refrigerator, and up to 83% of participants lost at least 5%,” he said. “Sometimes it’s the simple things that make a difference.”

Luca Moretti, MD, a medical doctor at Sapienza University of Rome, who wasn’t involved in the studies, added that convenience isn’t the only thing to consider when deciding whether to use orforglipron.

Obesity management should begin with an individualized plan that includes nutrition, physical activity, and the management of other risk factors.

“When these measures are not sufficient, adding pharmacological treatment can be considered together with a physician,” he told Healthline.

Moretti further advised speaking with your physician about any potential side effects, your existing medical conditions, other medications you are taking, and your treatment preferences.

“So the most useful question to ask a doctor is not simply, ‘Is Foundayo more convenient?’ but rather: ‘Among the available treatments, which one offers the best balance of effectiveness, tolerability, and practicality for me?'” he said.

#4758. Detached Nails More Than 4 Times Higher Among GLP-1 Users, Study Finds

GLP-1 users have reported various unusual side effects like “Ozempic feet,” “Ozempic butt,” and “Ozempic breasts.” These changes have been largely tied to rapid weight loss.

In the study, which researchers presented in October at the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) Congress 2026 in Vienna, people taking GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide — Ozempic, Wegovy — and tirzepatide — Mounjaro, Zepbound — reported nail detachment and nail discoloration more often than people who didn’t take the drugs.

Nail changes reported among GLP-1 users

The average treatment duration was 18.5 months, and researchers tracked weight loss over three months.

They also looked separately at people who had lost weight, to help tell whether the drugs or the weight loss explained the changes. However, the abstract didn’t specify which GLP-1 drugs participants took.

The researchers have submitted the findings to peer-reviewed dermatology journals, where they are under review, Taïeb told Healthline. Here’s what they found.

Nail separation found in 39% of GLP-1 users

Taïeb told Healthline that the study asked about “nail detachment” because patients, not doctors, filled out the questionnaire.

Nail detachment was the strongest signal among the five nail changes the researchers asked about, Taïeb said.

According to the abstract, 39% of GLP-1 users reported nail separation or detachment (onycholysis), compared with 9% of nonusers.

Nail discoloration is a change in a nail’s usual color, such as white, yellow, or greenish-black, according to the AAD.

According to the abstract, 46% of GLP-1 users reported discoloration, compared with 17% of nonusers. Ridges or grooves and fragile nails were also more common among GLP-1 users.

Mir B. Ali, MD, a bariatric surgeon and medical director of the MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Los Angeles, said that while he prescribes GLP-1s, nail detachment hasn’t come up in his practice. Ali wasn’t involved in the research.

“I haven’t had anybody complain of that. It’s not something we really see,” he told Healthline. He said his patients more often mention brittle nails or minor changes in nail color.

Weight loss alone didn’t explain GLP-1 nail changes

Weight loss alone didn’t appear to explain the nail findings, the researchers said in the press release. The differences between users and nonusers held up when they looked only at people who had lost weight.

The researchers also cautioned in the press release that the study can’t show the drugs caused the changes. They noted that about half of GLP-1 users had nail problems before starting treatment.

Ali said the total amount of weight a person loses plays a bigger role in hair and nail changes than the rate at which they lose it.

GLP-1 hair loss and scalp changes

The U.S. labels for Wegovy and Zepbound list hair loss as a side effect reported in clinical trials.

As such, researchers have been examining GLP-1-related hair loss.

  • Unusual hair loss: 50% of users versus 34% of nonusers
  • Loss of hair volume or density: 42% versus 19%
  • Hair loss that started after treatment began: 26% versus 12%

Scalp redness was about three times as common among users, and most users with hair loss also had scalp symptoms such as itching, redness, or scaling.

Scalp inflammation may play a role in hair changes among GLP-1 users, in addition to the shedding that often follows rapid weight loss, one of the study’s authors stated in the news release.

Experts have told Healthline that hair loss on GLP-1 drugs is often temporary and tied to weight loss.

“Hair loss, we see commonly, not only in patients who are on GLP-1 medications, but also in our surgical weight loss patients,” Ali said.

“When you lose weight, especially in women, it releases hormones. You’re getting metabolic changes, and all these things can lead to hair loss and some changes in the nails.” Ali said he tells patients that “as the weight stabilizes at a healthy weight, the hormones stabilize and the hair tends to come back to where it was before.”

How to protect your hair and nails while using GLP-1s

Nail detachment and discoloration don’t appear as adverse reactions in the current U.S. prescribing information for Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound.

“I would not recommend stopping the GLP-1 medication simply because nail detachment occurs,” Evans said. She advised people to tell their doctor about any new changes in hair, scalp, or nails.

Treatment for nail detachment or discoloration depends on the cause, Evans said. A dermatologist can check for an infection, an inflammatory nail disorder, a nutritional problem, an injury, or another explanation, she said.

A detached nail recovers as a new nail grows out from its base, Evans said. That takes about 4 to 6 months for a fingernail and 9 to 12 months for a toenail.

Lastly, the AAD recommends having a board certified dermatologist examine any nail that’s lifting.

The AAD also recommends seeing a dermatologist if skin, hair, or nail side effects don’t improve, as they can help determine whether symptoms are due to the medication or another condition.

“The important message is don’t panic, but don’t ignore persistent nail changes either,” Evans said.

#4733. Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: Lung Cancer Can Look Like Anyone

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

  • Up to 1 in 5 lung cancer cases in the United States occur in people who’ve never smoked, but our picture of the disease hasn’t caught up.
  • Healthline Editor-in-Chief Robin Hough talks with Gini Harrison, a psychologist and his friend of nearly 30 years, whose only symptom was a sore shoulder blamed on breastfeeding. Her stage 4 EGFR-positive lung cancer diagnosis took nearly nine months.
  • They discuss testing, catching lung cancer sooner, and the documentary film, Our Lungs: Time to Breathe, which follows filmmaker Sarah Li and the family of Alex, a 24-year-old who died weeks after diagnosis.

Picture someone with lung cancer. Now picture a 24-year-old soccer player in the prime of his life. A filmmaker in her early 40s with a camera in her hand, doing what she loves best. A busy mom of two, with a shoulder so sore she can’t pick up her newborn, yet struggling to get a diagnosis. 

The gap between the picture in our heads and the people actually being diagnosed is costing time. And time is the one thing with lung cancer you cannot get back. 

We know that lung cancer is the most common cancer worldwide and the leading cause of cancer death. And while lung cancer incidence rates are falling in many countries as fewer people smoke, lung cancer in people who have never smoked is on the rise globally, both as a proportion and in absolute numbers. 

According to the CDC, as many as 20% of lung cancers diagnosed in the US are in people who have never smoked. Data suggests that non-smoking lung cancer is now among the most common causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide.

The numbers are stark. Lung cancer can affect anyone. And that includes the young – like Alex, one of the subjects of a powerful new short documentary commissioned by the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK for their Our Lungs: Time to Breathe campaign. 

Alex was a 24-year-old soccer player who died just weeks after receiving a diagnosis of Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) positive lung cancer, following several misdiagnoses born of the fact that no one suspected lung cancer in one so young, fit, and with no smoking history. The film was made by filmmaker Sarah Li, who herself was diagnosed with Stage 4 EGFR+ lung cancer in April 2024, just a month after Alex died from the same mutation of the disease, and charts her journey with survivor’s guilt, what an earlier diagnosis may have meant for Alex, and what that fact means for her own future. 

Ahead of Lung Cancer Awareness Month in November, I sat down with my friend, Gini Harrison, to talk about the film, her own journey, and why catching lung cancer sooner matters so much. Gini is a Professor of Psychology at The Open University, a trustee of EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK, and is living with stage 4 EGFR-positive lung cancer. She’s also the mom at the top of this piece, whose sore shoulder was initially attributed to breastfeeding posture.

This interview has been lightly edited for clarity and length.

EGFR-positive lung cancer: Warning signs

Take me back to the beginning. What were your initial symptoms, and what were you told?

Gini Harrison: I’d had my son, Michael, in February 2021 and was on maternity leave. My daughter, Emily, was five. I think the shoulder pain started around March, but that was it. No cough, no breathlessness… nothing that made me think about my lungs.

Initially, they gave me painkillers, which helped a bit. But the pain kept getting worse. Eventually, I started losing movement and strength in my arm, and it got to the point where I couldn’t pick up my son anymore.

This was all happening during COVID, so my appointments were over the telephone. Eventually, after months without being examined face-to-face, I paid to see a shoulder specialist. As soon as he touched my shoulder blade and I practically leaped out of the chair, he knew something wasn’t right. He ordered an MRI, which showed a tumor in my shoulder blade and another in my upper right lung. In December 2021, I was diagnosed with stage IV lung cancer.

What do you remember about that period, and how did it feel to be reassured when your instinct said otherwise?

Gini Harrison: I wasn’t thinking, “This must be cancer.” I was just thinking, “Why isn’t this getting better?”

And I think that distinction matters. Patients shouldn’t have to identify their own disease before they can get help. You should be able to say that something is wrong and be listened to.

What went through your head when you heard “lung cancer”? Did you have any of the assumptions you now push back against?

Gini Harrison: It was devastating. So many of my first questions were about the children: “How are we going to tell them?” “Am I going to be here to see them grow up?” “Will they remember me?” Suddenly, there was a question mark over a future I’d taken for granted.

I was also completely shocked by the type of cancer. If someone had said breast cancer, I don’t think I would have been anywhere near as surprised. But lung cancer?! I’d always associated it with smoking, and I’d never smoked.

I hadn’t imagined that this particular cancer could ever happen to me. So yes, I had some assumptions that I now challenge. I understand how deeply rooted they are because I had them too.

What lung cancer ‘looks like’

When people learn you have lung cancer, what do they say? What’s the first question you get asked?

Gini Harrison: People are often shocked, and one of the first questions is whether I smoked.

And they often look healthy. That doesn’t mean everything is easy, or that there aren’t side effects. It just means you can’t tell what someone is dealing with by looking at them.

I think we’ve given lung cancer such a narrow public image that people outside it can become almost invisible.

Has anyone ever asked if you smoked? How do you answer, and how should it land?

Gini Harrison: Oh, yes! Once, I spoke to a doctor about a breathing problem after radiotherapy. He asked, “How much did you smoke?” When I said I’d never smoked, he replied, “Well, how did you get lung cancer then?”

On another occasion, I went to a hospice for help managing side effects, and someone there asked, “How are you still alive?” That’s quite a thing to hear when you’ve come looking for support!

When people ask about smoking, I try to explain that I’ve never smoked and I’m certainly not alone. Never smoking doesn’t make you immune. But I’m also very clear that nobody deserves lung cancer, whether they’ve smoked or not. We shouldn’t be creating a hierarchy of who deserves compassion.

Smoking history matters medically. But in a personal conversation, “How are you doing?” is a much kinder place to start.

Genetics and biomarker testing 

Explain biomarker or genetic testing as if I’m a friend (which I am!) who’s never heard of it. Why does it matter?

Gini Harrison: Lung cancer isn’t one single disease. Two people can both have lung cancer, but different changes in their cells may be driving it.

Genes contain the instructions that tell our cells how to work, including when to grow and divide. Changes in those genes (called mutations) can cause cells to keep dividing when they shouldn’t, forming a tumor. In my case, there’s a mutation in a gene called EGFR.

EGFR-positive lung cancer is a type of non-small cell lung cancer caused by a genetic mutation in the EGFR gene, which keeps the EGFR receptor switched “on,” leading to uncontrolled cell growth and enhanced cancer cell survival.

Biomarker testing looks for these changes, as well as other features of the cancer that can guide treatment. If we know what’s helping the cancer grow, there may be a medication that specifically targets it.

The word “genetic” can sound scary, particularly when you have children. But these changes usually develop in cancer cells over a person’s lifetime. They aren’t necessarily inherited.

Why early diagnosis is so important 

What would you say to someone reading this who has a symptom that won’t go away?

Gini Harrison: Please get it checked. And if it persists, worsens or doesn’t improve with treatment, go back. Explain what’s changed and how it’s affecting your life. Ask, “What happens next if this doesn’t get better?”

The symptoms don’t always look like lung symptoms. Mine was shoulder pain.

Most symptoms will turn out to be something other than cancer. But when a symptom continues to worsen, the original explanation needs to be reconsidered. Lower risk doesn’t mean no risk.

And I don’t want all the responsibility to fall on patients to keep pushing. Reassurance should come with a next step.

Our Lungs: Time to Breathe – Alex and Sarah’s stories 

How did you get involved in advocacy, and what was the hardest thing about being part of it?

Gini Harrison: Once I had recovered from my initial treatment, I realized that I wanted to help others in my situation, so I became a trustee of the charity EGFR Positive Lung Cancer UK. Through this work, we produced a film, Our Lungs: Time to Breathe. At the heart of it, it challenges the perception of what someone with lung cancer looks like.

The hardest part is how close to home it feels. Alex was so young, and that worked against him. Watching his family talk about what happened is heartbreaking. It’s just so unfair.

What did you hope people would see in Sarah’s and Alex’s stories?

Gini Harrison: Sarah’s story shows life continuing alongside lung cancer. She’s a filmmaker with ambitions, people she loves, and things she wants to do.

Through Alex’s family, you see the enormity of the loss. I hope people feel that, rather than simply hearing another statistic.

Lung cancer and the bigger picture

What’s changed since you were diagnosed, and what hasn’t?

Gini Harrison: For me, the biggest thing is that my children are growing up and I’m here to see it. Michael is now five (the age Emily was when I was diagnosed), and Emily is about to turn ten. I can’t tell you how grateful I am to be here with them.

It wasn’t about big bucket-list things. It was about being here every day. Seeing Michael start school. Seeing Emily reach double figures. Those milestones are enormous when you’ve wondered whether you’d get to see them.

After chemotherapy and radiotherapy, I’ve had no evidence of disease since May 2022, and my scans remain stable. But a big change has been seeing the progress in available treatment. It gives me more hope about what might be available if I need treatment again.

The public image of lung cancer still needs to catch up. We need better recognition of symptoms, access to appropriate testing and treatment, and support for the whole person. As a psychologist as well as a patient, I’m particularly aware that psychological well-being and family life need attention alongside the cancer itself.

What do you want people to do after reading this?

Gini Harrison: I’d love for people to share the film and help get it in front of those who wouldn’t normally think lung cancer has anything to do with them. And take something from it into everyday life: book the appointment you’ve been putting off, ask your doctor about testing, or respond with kindness when someone tells you about their diagnosis.

Just being willing to rethink your assumptions could make a difference.

What do you wish more people would say to someone in your position?

Gini Harrison: “You don’t have to put a brave face on with me. How are you really doing?”

When you look well, or your scans are good, people can assume you’re “fine now”. But I’m not sure the fear ever completely goes away. Every scan is terrifying. It feels like there’s a sword of Damocles hanging over my head, and part of me is waiting for the bad scan. I also still have lasting side effects from treatment, and probably always will.

My life has fundamentally changed. Some of that has brought good things, but it doesn’t make the difficult parts disappear. It helps when someone gives you room to be honest about both, without expecting you to reassure them or turn everything into a positive story.

Gini and I have been friends for nearly 30 years, and, while she’d probably cringe to hear me say it, I remain in awe of the way she approaches life with cancer. I am so grateful for her sharing her experiences with me for this column. What Gini asks of us is simple: look again, ask the question, listen, and act on your instincts. Our Lungs: Time to Breathe is where that starts for her.   • brain fog
  • concentration 
  • working memory 
  • verbal memory
  • processing speed
  • problem solving

“Estrogen plays a well-documented role in protecting the brain, yet there are still no approved type and dose of treatment that specifically targets the cognitive symptoms so many women experience during menopause,” Rhonda Voskuhl, MD, neurologist, member of the Comprehensive Menopause Center at UCLA Health, and senior study author, said in a press release. 

“Women are often told to simply live with brain fog, when in fact there is a neuroscience basis for it, and potentially a way to address it. That gap in care is what motivated us to look more closely at estriol,” Voskuhl said. 

This was a small, preliminary case series without a placebo group or randomization. More research is needed to confirm the benefits for cognitive symptoms. 

Key points

  • Brain fog is a common symptom of menopause with no approved treatment. 
  • A recent study found that hormone therapy with estriol and progesterone may help improve cognitive symptoms during menopause. 
  • More research is needed to confirm the effects of hormone therapy on cognitive symptoms. 

Hormone therapy may help with menopause mood changes

A study published September 9 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society, showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes in women in midlife. 

The researchers noted that mood disturbances, such as depression, irritability, anxiety, and mental exhaustion, affect up to 68% of women during menopause. 

“The erratic changes and loss of estrogen associated with this hormonal transition can lower serotonin and dopamine levels, which [are] associated with depression and cognitive changes, including poor concentration, brain fog, and memory loss,” said Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

The study included 260 individuals with an average age of 52 years. Just over half of the participants had a history of anxiety or depression, and 24% were using an antidepressant medication at the start of the study. 

Each participant completed the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) both before and after initiating hormone therapy for FDA-approved indications, such as hot flashes and night sweats. 

The average follow-up during the study was 4.5 months. Between the baseline and the follow-up, there was a significant reduction in the MRS psychological scores. The percentage of participants with severe mood symptoms decreased from 62.3% to 24.6%. 

Treatment response didn’t appear to differ by age, menopausal stage, psychiatric history, or antidepressant use. These findings suggest that hormone therapy may help improve mood symptoms in midlife, especially for those with higher severity of symptoms before treatment. 

“This study supports the evidence that starting hormone therapy for the typical disruptive symptoms of menopause may bring collateral benefits, including improvement in mood,” Ross said. “Communicating with your healthcare provider is always best to manage your symptoms and prepare yourself properly for this next stage of life.” 

Key points

  • A recent study showed that hormone therapy may help improve mood changes associated with menopause. 
  • The biggest improvement was seen in those with more severe symptoms before beginning treatment. 
  • Improvement with hormone therapy was seen despite age, menopause stage, and psychiatric history. 

Menopause hormone therapy not linked to dementia

For decades, women have been warned about the serious potential risks associated with menopause hormone therapy (MHT). This included an FDA “black box” warning citing complications like cardiovascular disease, cancer, and dementia. 

However, a recent meta-analysis commissioned by the World Health Organization (WHO) that included data from more than a million women, and was published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, found that MHT neither increased nor decreased dementia risk. 

“The World Health Organisation currently provides no guidance on MHT and dementia risk, leaving a critical gap for women, clinicians, and policymakers. Our work will help inform upcoming WHO guidelines on reducing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia,” Melissa Melville, a PhD candidate and aging researcher in the Department of Clinical Health Psychology at the University College London, and first author of the study, told Healthline in a previous interview. 

In the same interview, Melville stressed that the importance of this work is twofold: for clinicians, there is an urgent need for higher-quality research, and for patients, decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

Key points

  • A new meta-analysis has helped to ease long-standing fears regarding the association between menopause hormone therapy and dementia. 
  • The analysis reviewed data from more than a million women and found that MHT neither raises nor decreases the risk of dementia. 
  • The researchers noted that decisions about MHT should be guided by symptoms and well-established benefits and risks.

#4652. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Not Be Linked to Colorectal Cancer

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

  • Researchers report that consumption of ultra-processed foods doesn’t necessarily increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.
  • They note that there is a wide range of foods considered ultra-processed, and each may carry different risks.
  • Experts say people should adopt a healthy diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and calcium-rich foods to lower their risk of colorectal cancer.

Ultra-processed foods as a group do not increase the risk of colorectal cancer, according to a new study.

Researchers from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) report that they found no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer incidence among the more than 400,000 adults they studied.

They said their findings are important because ultra-processed foods now account for more than half of adults’ energy intake in the United States. They published their research on October 15 in PLOS Medicine.

The study comes amid recent research that has connected ultra-processed foods to an increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.

However, the link between colorectal cancer and ultra-processed foods has been less clear.

A 2025 study reported that ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of colorectal cancer in females under 50.

A 2022 study found that males who consumed high rates of ultra-processed foods had a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.

NCI scientists noted in their new study that ultra-processed products represent a wide range of foods.

They pointed out that foods such as whole grains and dairy products were associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer among study participants, while foods such as processed meats were associated with a higher risk.

“We did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence,” said Erikka Loftfield, PhD, a former investigator with the NCI, in a news release. “However, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention.”

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the NCI scientists appear to be on the right track.

“While the study did not find that overall ultra-processed food intake was associated with colorectal cancer risk, the findings suggest that simply classifying foods by their level of processing may not tell the whole story,” said Marissa Shams-White, PhD, a senior principal scientist in population science at the American Cancer Society.

“One of the challenges with studying ultra-processed foods is that the category includes a very wide range of foods and beverages, and we don’t yet know whether they all relate to colorectal cancer risk in the same way,” she added.

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, agrees.

“Just because it is ultra-processed doesn’t mean it is inherently bad or good for you,” he said. “The source of the food and the additives like preservatives, flavorings, sugars, and pigments that are added to increase palatability probably play a much larger role in increasing cancer risk.”

“A whole grain cereal and a hot dog are both UPF, but they push risk in opposite directions,” Thanki noted.

Ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer risk

In their study, the National Cancer Institute researchers analyzed data from 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 years who enrolled in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study between 1995 and 1996.

The participants recorded their eating habits using a food frequency questionnaire. Researchers then used that data to estimate each person’s intake of ultra-processed foods. The median consumption of ultra-processed foods among participants was 43% of daily median intake.

The participants were then followed until 2018, for a median of nearly 19 years. The scientists reported that 10,075 cases of colorectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed during that period.

The researchers reported no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and the incidence of colorectal cancer.

However, they said their findings varied by food type. Higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium derived from UPF sources was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence, while meat from UPF sources was associated with higher incidence.

Researchers also found that replacing 10% of gram weight from UPF intake with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2% lower incidence of CRC.

The researchers acknowledged limitations to their study.

They noted that the participants were primarily older, white Americans. In addition, UPF intake was only measured once at the beginning of the study period, so the findings cannot account for changes in eating habits or the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods.

Nonetheless, Ashkan Farhadi, MD, a gastroenterologist at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in California, who wasn’t involved in the study, said he found the results “very interesting.”

“The definition of ultra-processed foods is so broad,” he told Healthline. “It’s important to designate what type of ultra-processed foods you’re talking about.”

“You shouldn’t just go by whether a food is designated as ultra-processed,” Farhadi added. “You should go by what’s on the [ingredient] label.”

Shams-White shared a similar approach. “When it comes to diet, it is important to follow an overall healthy dietary pattern,” she told Healthline. “Emphasize a variety of fruits and vegetables and whole grains and limit red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and refined grain products.”

“Eating a diet rich in fiber and whole grains, dairy and calcium, and limiting processed and red meat and alcohol is what really reduces your risk of developing cancer,” added Thanki.

Risk factors for colorectal cancer

The American Cancer Society estimates there will be about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer and nearly 50,000 new cases of rectal cancer in the United States in 2026.

The incidence rate is slightly higher in men than in females.

The organization reports that the rate of colorectal cancer cases in the United States has declined since the mid-1980s, but they note that most of that decrease has occurred in older adults.

The incidence rate among people under 50 increased by nearly 3% from 2013 to 2022. Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of death from cancer for U.S. adults under the age of 50.

Colorectal cancer occurs when cells in the large intestine grow out of control. About 70% of cases begin in the colon.

There are a number of symptoms of colorectal cancer. Some of the common ones include:

  • constipation
  • diarrhea
  • blood in the stool
  • bleeding in the rectum
  • abdominal pain

Diet is considered one of the risk factors for colorectal cancer. However, there are other considerations.

Farhadi said genetics, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and a sedentary lifestyle can also increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.

Shams-White noted that the American Cancer Society Guideline for Diet and Physical Activity for Cancer Prevention recommended achieving and maintaining a healthy weight, being physically active, following a healthy eating pattern, and avoiding alcohol and tobacco.

Thanki points out that there are risk factors for colorectal cancer that can be modified and others that can’t.

“Among non-modifiable factors, the biggest are age, a personal or family history of colorectal cancer or advanced polyps, inherited syndromes such as Lynch syndrome, and longstanding inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s colitis),” he told Healthline.

“On the modifiable side, the evidence is strongest for obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, alcohol, and diets high in red and processed meat and low in fiber, whole grains, and calcium,” he added.

Colorectal cancer treatment outcomes improve if the disease is caught at an early stage. The 5-year relative survival for localized colorectal cancer is 91.3%. The 5-year relative survival rate for colorectal cancer caught at various stages, including cancer that has spread, is 65.4%.

Shams-White points out that the American Cancer Society Colorectal Cancer Guidelines recommend that people at average risk of colorectal cancer start getting screened at age 45.

“Screening can help prevent cancer by finding abnormal cells and polyps so they can be removed before they can become colorectal cancer, as well as detect cancer at an earlier stage,” she said.

Thanki had similar advice. “Get screened starting at 45, or earlier if you have a family history or other risk factors, because colonoscopies can prevent cancer by removing polyps,” he said. “And never ignore symptoms like rectal bleeding or a change in bowel habits at any age.”

Breast cancer awareness has never been higher. Every October, the world turns pink and the key messages—that early diagnosis saves lives and that knowing the risk factors reduces the risk—are hard to miss. 

Breast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death amongst women in most countries, with an estimated 694,000 deaths annually and around 8.2 million women globally living with a breast cancer diagnosis, according to 2024 data from the World Health Organization (WHO)’s Global Health Observatory. 

But when October ends, and the wall-to-wall campaigns move on, what has awareness actually changed for those impacted by breast cancer?

For Marisa Weiss, MD, the answer is that there is always more to be done, and she’s uniquely placed to hold that view. 

Dr. Weiss has treated breast cancer patients for more than 30 years. In 2000, she founded Breastcancer.org, a not-for-profit that provides expert-reviewed medical and lifestyle information on breast health and breast cancer to millions of people globally.

Sixteen years ago, she was diagnosed with stage 1 breast cancer, becoming, in her own words, a “dual citizen” in that moment, with lived experience on both sides of the doctor’s desk. 

I had the pleasure of sitting down with Dr. Weiss as part of Healthline’s Breast Cancer Awareness Month coverage to talk about how awareness has changed care for the better for millions of people around the world, but why critical gaps remain, and how access to trusted information and community can help close those gaps. 

“It’s good that awareness is high, but knowledge remains low,” she said. The misconception she sees most is the belief that breast cancer is something that happens to other families.

“Most people think they’re off the hook because it doesn’t run in their family,” Dr. Weiss explained. In fact, 85%-90% of people who get breast cancer have no known family history and no known gene in the family, “so we’re all at risk,” she warns. 

For most, a diagnosis arrives as a shock. “They’re completely surprised, flattened by it. Their life is upside down, and they scramble to find what they need to know,” Dr. Weiss noted.

Breast cancer care: Be your own advocate

That aftershock caused by a diagnosis is at the core of why Breastcancer.org exists. Because the person whose life is at stake is increasingly expected to direct their own care and advocate for their needs, access to accurate and empowering information is critical in that process. “Decisions aren’t being made around the patient and for the patient in a bubble,” Dr. Weiss said. “Decision-making is supposed to be shared.”

With appointments hard to get and clinicians stretched, she said patients have to do more of the work.

In practice, Dr. Weiss says that means preparing by writing down your family history on both sides and making sure your care team knows who you are. “What’s important to you? Who’s in your life? Do you want more children? Are you planning a vacation? All those decisions can have an impact on the care you receive.”

Gaps remain even with the best care

Where Dr. Weiss sees the widest gap is after treatment ends. Speaking from personal experience, she said: “I had the best care possible, and there were still gaps. You can find yourself alone with lots of questions and fears.”

Understandably, a great deal of focus and attention is centered on diagnosis and treatment, she said. Then it stops. “When your life is supposed to go back to normal, it never does. You have to find a new normal. That quiet phase can be really lonely and difficult.”

For Dr. Weiss, the questions that fill that silence are often as practical as they are emotional: “How do I know my treatment is working? How do I tell leftover side effects from new ones? How do I go back to work, and who do I tell? How do I talk to my kids? What about that new treatment I heard about on TV last night?”

That post-treatment period can also be the first time anyone has really had a chance to stop, take stock, and start asking “what next?” 

“You might think, what happened to me? Who do I want to be moving forward? How do I have a long life full of wonderful things, and remain as independent as possible?” 

What breast cancer patients don’t say in the clinic

Some of what patients do post-care never reaches their doctor at all, with Dr. Weiss pointing to a growing amount of personal care happening outside the hospital: GLP-1s, peptides, hormonal therapies, supplements, medical cannabis, to name a few. Breastcancer.org’s annual Pulse survey shows that people reported being interested in all of those things and more, and that they tend to learn about them online rather than risk the stigma of bringing them up with their doctors.

“They don’t really trust that their doctor knows much about these things,” she said, “and they don’t want a conversation that makes them feel judged. Some patients worry about what ends up in their medical record and then everyone’s going to know.”

Dr. Weiss says the stakes are real, because some of those choices can interact with cancer medications with potential for adverse or unexpected side effects. “That is why people turn to Breastcancer.org with questions that are personal, private, and important, including “what do I need to share with my doctor, and who’s on a need-to-know basis?”

Peer communities fill part of that gap. “Breastcancer.org’s members share medical information, tips, and practical advice with people who have just had the call, or are about to start treatment and don’t know what to expect,” Dr. Weiss shared, and said that while she values that kind of support system hugely, it does come with a caveat:

The same community can also be intrusive. Dr. Weiss describes standing at a party, drink in hand, when someone asks what kind of cancer you had, what you did, and whether it came back. Her advice to patients, which she took herself: “Put up your hand and say, ‘thank you so much for caring, but I cannot hear another story right now.’”

Information you can trust in the age of AI 

On the topic of AI and its impact on treatment and care, Dr. Weiss is cautiously optimistic. On the treatment front, she points to tools that help radiologists read mammograms more thoroughly and assess future risk as game changers in the fight against breast cancer. 

Breastcancer.org also uses AI for tasks such as finding citations and interpreting pathology reports. But like many, she is wary of misinformation. “When you’re fearful, you look for reassurance, and you don’t want to be reassured by misinformation. When it comes to health information, every word matters.”

Her final advice is for the appointment itself, which she says she has even struggled with. “When they start speaking quickly in complex medical language, you can get lost really fast. The risk is you kind of fake it. That must not happen.” 

“Do your research in advance. Write things down. Bring someone you trust as a second set of ears. Ask direct questions. Don’t worry if it sounds funny or silly. It’s always a smart question when you are dealing with your health.” Just be prepared for the questions to multiply from there. “Once you get a certain set of questions answered, that raises more questions. So just write it all down.”

#4616. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Increase Dementia, Stroke Risk

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

  • Healthy adults who consume high amounts of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) may be more likely to show signs of vascular brain injury, which has been linked to stroke risk and cognitive decline.
  • UPFs account for more than 50% of daily energy intake in high-income countries such as the United States.
  • People with the highest UPF intake had higher body fat levels and greater odds of vascular brain injury than those with the lowest intake.
  • You can reduce UPF intake by making simple swaps toward whole foods, such as fruits, vegetables, and grains prepared at home.

Healthy adults who eat high levels of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) may be more likely to show signs of damage to the brain that could increase the likelihood of stroke and future memory problems, according to a study published October 15 in JAMA Network Open. 

UPFs are often defined using the NOVA classification: These foods typically have 5 or more ingredients and include substances not typically found in culinary preparations. This category of foods includes packaged snack foods, sugary cereals, soft drinks, and frozen ready-to-eat meals. 

UPFs account for more than 50% of daily energy intake in high-income countries, including the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom. 

Previous research has linked these foods to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. This new study adds to growing evidence linking high UPF intake to adverse health outcomes.

High UPF intake may increase the risk of vascular brain injury

This was a cross-sectional analysis of 8,783 adults between the ages of 35 and 69 without cardiovascular disease. The participants were all enrolled in the Canadian Alliance for Healthy Hearts and Minds (CAHHM) and Prospective Urban and Rural Epidemiology-MIND (PURE-MIND) cohorts. 

The researchers used MRI scans to evaluate the participants’ brains. They also set out to analyze the cross-sectional relationship between UPF consumption, body fat, vascular brain injury (VBI), and cognitive performance. 

They measured the participants’ habitual UPF intake using a food frequency questionnaire. Intake was expressed as servings based on a 2,000-calorie-per-day diet. 

UPFs accounted for an average of 25% of participants’ daily diets. For the individuals with the highest intake, this increased to 45% of everything on their plates, compared to 16% for those who ate the least. 

The researchers also noted that UPFs were not just an added part of individual diets. These foods appeared to replace whole foods.

Among people with the highest UPF intake, fruits, vegetables, grains, and other minimally processed foods accounted for 41% of their daily food intake. Among those with the lowest UPF intake, minimally processed foods made up 68% of their daily intake. 

When the researchers compared the highest intake to the lowest, they found that those with a higher intake of UPFs had 1.7% more body fat. They also found that people with a higher UPF intake had higher odds of VBI. 

“These findings are concerning, but it is important to remember that an association does not prove cause and effect,” said Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“From my perspective as a preventive cardiology dietitian, these findings reinforce the importance of nourishing our blood vessels to support both cardiovascular and brain health.” 

Although researchers found no significant differences in cognitive performance, the signs of vascular brain injury observed in the study have been associated with an increased risk of stroke and cognitive decline.

These findings suggest a possible link between UPF intake and signs of brain changes. However, participants were only assessed once and weren’t followed over time. This means the researchers were unable to determine whether UPFs directly caused the changes. 

“While we can’t conclude from this study that limiting UPFs would improve brain health, we have decades of literature demonstrating that higher intakes of green leafy vegetables, brightly colored fruits, whole grains, and lean protein sources are good for brain health,” said Suzanne Judd, PhD, Director of the UC College of Medicine’s Center for Public Health, who wasn’t involved in the study.  

“So focusing more on what can be brought into the diet rather than what should be eliminated might be a good way to start,” she added. 

Why might UPFs affect the brain?

The preservatives [in UPFs] may have a direct impact on the vascular system, while the loss of antioxidants results in a less robust response to inflammation in the body. 

“Additionally, sugar or salt is often added to make the food a bit tastier so people consume it in higher quantities. Over-consumption of food can lead to weight gain and diabetes, which can negatively impact brain health,” Judd said. 

Tips for reducing your UPF intake

Judd told Healthline that it is virtually impossible to completely avoid UPFs. Even some “health” foods, like protein powder and some yogurts, are considered ultra-processed. 

It is more important to look at diet overall and emphasize consuming foods as close to their natural state as possible, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, and meats purchased raw or frozen and then cooked at home.

Routhenstein recommended starting simply with one manageable swap rather than trying to overhaul your entire diet. 

She said that you should identify an ultra-processed food you eat regularly and consider a more nutrient-rich alternative. It is important to account for convenience, food preferences, budget, and lifestyle so that the change feels realistic and is sustainable over the long term.

“I want people to feel empowered, not fearful, when making food choices,” said Routhenstein. “Healthy eating does not mean having to cook everything from scratch. What matters most is building consistent, nourishing habits that support both heart and brain health.”
