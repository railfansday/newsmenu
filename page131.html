#3141. John Harbaugh defends Cam Skattebo's helmet-lowering technique

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

On Sunday, Giants running back Cam Skattebo drew a rare flag for impermissible use of the helmet. On Monday, Giants coach John Harbaugh defended Skattebo’s technique.

“I’m quite sure [Skattebo is] not trying to use his helmet as a weapon, as a battering ram,” Harbaugh told reporters, via Jordan Raanan of ESPN. “It’s not the way he’s thinking. He’s leading with his shoulder. That’s what he’s trying to do. . . . So, I didn’t have a problem with the technique personally at all, but we haven’t heard from the league. Maybe they’ll be able to enlighten us a little bit on it in terms of what they want us to teach, and that would be great.”

The language from the rulebook is as clear as it can be. Under Rule 12, Article 10(a), it’s a foul if a player “lowers his head and makes forcible contact with his helmet against an opponent.”

The NFL adopted that rule in 2018, as a proposal that was cobbled together, presented to ownership, and passed without any advance discussion or scrutiny from the media. Infractions are almost always fined after the fact. Skattebo’s violation was flagged.

In Week 6 alone, the NFL imposed five fines for impermissible use of the helmet. In Week 5, six such fines were levied. For the year (including the preseason), 31 fines for impermissible use of the helmet have been imposed.

It’s only an issue for coaches when it results in 15 yards of field position; the fines are paid by the players. If the league wants to truly get rid of the maneuver, flags need to fly. And then coaches will be incentivized to come up with a plan other than allowing players to use their helmets like a battering ram.

For now, Skattebo has vowed to keep doing it. If he does, the fines will increase. Eventually, he’ll be looking at a suspension. And the officials will surely be watching him more closely for further violations — and possibly throwing more flags.

#5419. NHTSA Proposes Cyan Lights For AVs, And Other Conveniently Tesla-Specific Changes

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) has announced more proposed changes to Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 108, which governs vehicle lighting regulations. The latest changes, announced Oct. 23, include establishing equipment specifications for vehicles with automated driving systems, including displaying cyan lights to signal when an automated driving system is operational, and removal of the requirement for autonomous vehicles (AVs) with no driver controls to have manually operated turn signals, hazard lights, and headlight beam switching controls or telltales.

This goes beyond the announcement NHTSA made earlier this month that it would modernize U.S. headlight regulations, which addressed headlight glare on other road users, improving performance, updating testing and materials, and modernizing lighting technology with rules for LEDs, dusk sensors, auto high beams, and replaceable light sources.

The Tesla Cybercab is the only car on American roads that does not have physical driving controls like a steering wheel or pedals, and it's currently the subject of a safety probe because of that lack of traditional controls. These proposed changes to Standard No. 108 almost certainly have absolutely nothing to do with the fact that Tesla CEO Elon Musk has donated over $400 million to Republicans this election cycle.

Mercedes-Benz got approval to use turquoise lights on its cars equipped with Drive Pilot, the company's Level 3 autonomous driving technology, in 2023. Mercedes made this decision to let other drivers and law enforcement know that a person is allowed to be distracted while their car is legally in control.

The new proposal will not require autonomously driving cars to have these cyan lights, but it legally establishes what NHTSA calls "an 'if equipped' standard" that regulates light color, as well as setting mounting and visibility rules for companies that choose to use them. If a vehicle is equipped with Automated Driving Systems marker lamps, at least one should be visible from each of the front, sides, and rear of the vehicle, at a proposed minimum height of 15 inches, and the lights can't be brighter than the parking lights.

Other proposed changes include a removal of the headlight testing requirements that currently require headlights in all vehicles to meet performance parameters when tested at 12.8 volts. It also aims to eliminate the current regulation that forces the uppermost light in a vertically oriented headlights to operate as the standard beam, since NHTSA has tentatively concluded that it's possible to provide adequate illumination for safe driving without requiring the low beam to be placed above the high beams.

The proposal incorporates what NHTSA describes as "a process to remove unnecessary barriers to technological innovation while ensuring motor vehicle safety is not compromised." It seems the primary "barriers to technological innovation" in autonomous vehicles are the current requirement for all cars to have a turn signal operating unit, as well as the requirement for all vehicles to be equipped with a headlamp beam switching device.

Proposed modifications to the regulation would specify that these changes would only apply to vehicles that are not equipped with manually operated driving controls, so there would be no changes in regulations for vehicles with a steering wheel and pedals.

Finally, the proposal changes the requirements for telltales, which are the sounds and little lights on your dashboard that illuminate when your turn signal or hazard lights are activated, or when your low-beam headlights or high-beam headlights are activated. NHTSA now wants to specify that these telltales are only required for cars with a driver, which it defines as "an occupant of a motor vehicle seated immediately behind the steering control system." By this definition, an autonomous vehicle without a steering wheel cannot have a driver, so it shouldn't be required to have these telltales.

As autonomous vehicle technology comes closer to reality, it's likely that many other NHTSA regulations will have to change, so we will keep you updated as those changes roll out. For now, NHTSA wants your input on this crop of changes; there are several ways to do it, outlined in the Federal Register.

#5418. From Barrel to Battery: What Will Europe’s Clean Car Debate Decide

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The new energy reality: 1 kWh of battery is cheaper than a barrel of oil

The EU is fiercely debating the future of the automotive industry and how far the transition to battery electric cars will be slowed down. Last time we had this debate back in 2022, Europe was likewise in the middle of an energy crisis. But today, much has changed and the debate is completely out of touch with reality.

In 2022, the European Green Deal set the direction and demonstrated true political leadership: the future is electric. New cars sold after 2035 would no longer rely on polluting, expensive, imported fossil fuels.

At the time, Europe was suffering from the aftermath of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. Oil prices soared to $120 per barrel, while lithium ion battery prices stood at $170 per kWh.

Today, however, the EU is on the verge of dismantling the Green Deal and specifically its poster child, the clean car targets. Meanwhile, another major geopolitical event has sent oil prices surging back towards 2022 levels. The conflict in the Middle East and the resulting spike in energy prices have once again laid bare the consequences of fossil fuel dependency. European drivers are paying the price, just as they did in previous crises. But unlike in the past, the solution is now readily available.

Since 2022, average battery prices have dropped dramatically to $105 per kWh in 2026 globally.

While oil prices repeatedly breached $105 per barrel between March and May, over the past three weeks they have experienced a sharp surge, continuously climbing well past the $105 mark.

This is a symbolic moment. A kWh of battery capacity costs less than a barrel of oil.

Batteries benefit from compounding learning curves and economies of scale that will continue to drive prices down. Oil, by contrast, remains an inherently volatile commodity bound to geopolitical instability and supply shocks.

Over its lifetime a car will consume more than 100 barrels of petrol which requires more than 200 barrels of crude oil. By contrast, the electric car needs around 50 kWh of battery to travel the same distance. Unlike burned fuel, that battery remains a reusable, recyclable asset long into the future.

This has profound practical implications for European drivers. This year, high road fuel prices led to an oil crisis premium of €55 billion for cars, or €220 euros per car every year. Electric vehicle drivers on the other hand have been largely unaffected by the higher energy prices and currently pay on average 2–3 times less for fuel than petrol and diesel car drivers.

This simple comparison underscores a bigger story, one of political leaders failing in their responsibility to protect European households and the wider economy.

While the EU weathers what the IEA has called the “mother of all energy crises,” the European Parliament and Member States are formalising a deal that includes a backwards-looking vision for the future of the automotive industry. One that allows biofuels cars to replace electric cars, despite all the evidence showing it’s an economic, industrial and technical dead end.

Many political leaders are trying a last desperate tactic to extend the life of the combustion engine by any means possible. Effectively, the proposals discussed would gut the car CO2 regulations by allowing carmakers to comply without selling a single electric car, shielding fossil fuel and biofuel interests while undermining the business case for Europe’s homegrown EV and battery industries.

Circumstances have changed dramatically since December 2025, when the European Commission first proposed easing the 2035 combustion-car phase-out. Politicians must act decisively to protect citizens and make this fossil fuel crisis Europe’s last one. We need an immediate reset of the electric car debate to protect European industry and consumers, as demanded in June by a broad coalition of industry groups and civil society.

The electric car market is surging, with the two biggest markets, France and Germany, seeing the sales of electric cars double in the past few months, compared to the previous year. Now is not the time to choke off that momentum and tell drivers and industry leaders that they just have to live with our debilitating oil dependency.

This autumn, the European Parliament and Council must send a signal to industry: it should focus on the technology of the future, or Europeans will pay the price of remaining in the fossil fuel past.

US military says the restrictions on the electric vehicles at Kadena airbase also apply to delivery drivers and taxis

One of America’s biggest overseas military bases has banned vehicles made by the Chinese electric vehicle maker BYD over security concerns, the US military in Japan said on Tuesday.

The ban, which went into effect this week at Kadena airbase on the southern Japanese island of Okinawa, has fueled speculation that similar measures could be taken at other US military facilities as part of Washington’s crackdown on Chinese technology it suspects of being used to collect sensitive information.

“These vehicles present an unacceptable and unmitigable risk to our personnel and our mission,” Lieut Col Alli Stormer, a spokesperson for the US air force 18th Wing at Kadena, said in a statement.

The restrictions apply to all BYD vehicles, including privately owned cars, taxis and delivery vehicles, reports said, adding that security personnel stationed at the base’s gates had been instructed to block BYD vehicles and instruct drivers to turn around.

Kadena, the biggest US air force base east Asia, would be directly involved in responding to a crisis involving China, Russia or North Korea.

Its inventory includes the powerful Typhon missile system, which is said to be capable of firing Tomahawk missiles as far as 1,600km, putting parts of China and Russia within striking distance. Kadena also hosts fighter planes and aircraft used for mid-air refuelling, surveillance and reconnaissance, according to its website.

The move comes amid concerns of potential information leaks via vehicle-mounted technologies such as cameras, sensors and GPS devices, South Korea’s Yonhap news agency said.

American military officials have not identified a specific threat, but the ban is part of wider measures taken by the US and other western countries to restrict Chinese smart technology in vehicles, mobile phones and other devices they fear could be used to “spy” on their owners.

In June the Pentagon added BYD to a list of 188 Chinese firms it believes have ties to the country’s military, drawing a protest from Beijing. The blacklist also includes the automaker Nio, e-commerce firm Alibaba and internet company Baidu.

The US defence department banned the sale and use of smartphones from Chinese companies Huawei and ZTE at military bases worldwide in 2018, again citing security concerns.

#3533. Anthropic trained Claude on illegally obtained Stanford professors' books

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI giant Anthropic illegally scraped and pirated the work of over 10 Stanford professors to train its Claude models, a federal judge ruled in a May 2026 lawsuit.

Anthropic previously trained Claude on illegally obtained texts from professors Tobias Wolff, Abraham Verghese, Chang-Rae Lee, Daniel Mason, Hayagreeva Rao and Myra Strober. In Bartz v. Anthropic, which the Authors Alliance calls the largest copyright settlement involving an AI company, Judge William Alsup ruled that Anthropic could no longer pirate books to create a permanent central library.

While blocking the piracy of work to train AI, Alsup justified the legal acquisition of books to train Claude’s Large Language Models (LLMs) as fair use. He compared AI training to how humans can read, internalize and remember a book’s themes and style. “Like any reader aspiring to be a writer, Anthropic’s LLMs trained upon works not to race ahead and replicate or supplant them — but to turn a hard corner and create something different,” he wrote.

The $1.5 billion settlement went to authors and publishers of nearly 500,000 registered works. Wolff and 27 other authors say the settlement isn’t enough.

“To put me to work that way, without remittance, is a form of slave labor,” said Wolff, professor emeritus of English and winner of the National Medal of Arts. “They’re using my work without paying for it. As time goes on, they’re going to make the lives of writers very difficult if there’s no fruit to their labor.”

10 of Wolff’s publications had been used for AI training without permission.

“[The settlement] still gives Anthropic pretty free rein to roam through our literature and basically take what they think will be useful and profitable for them,” Wolff said.

Wolff, along with English professor Chang-rae Lee, opted out of the Bartz settlement. In May 2026, they filed the separate non-class lawsuit Cruz v. Anthropic, seeking a jury trial for full statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed work, surpassing the approximate $3,000 of damages per work offered in the Bartz settlement.

Similar to the claims in Bartz, the plaintiffs allege that Anthropic illegally downloaded, reproduced and distributed their copyrighted books from “shadow libraries” like LibGen and PiLiMi, and that Anthropic broke the law by downloading and storing the pirated files in a central library.

“Our agents didn’t think the blanket lawsuit covered quite enough of the liability to us and are seeking redress in more particular ways,” Wolff said.

If Wolff and his fellow authors are successful, they would gain a permanent injunction against Claude model training using their works.

Paul Goldstein, a Stanford Law leading scholar in intellectual property and one of the plaintiffs represented in the class action, acknowledged the difficulty of addressing and preventing copyright issues for AI companies.

“The essential problem … is what economists call ‘transaction costs,’” Goldstein wrote in an email to The Daily. “Millions of individual copyrighted works may be copied in the course of any training incident, and it’s simply impracticable for the platform to obtain licenses from the copyright owner of every — or, indeed, any — single work.”

Outside of Bartz and Cruz, many authors, visual artists and news outlets, including The New York Times and Getty Images, have filed over 70 infringement lawsuits against AI companies. While Bartz established the current rule for pirating books, the legal future of mass scraping AI models used to train LLMs remains uncertain.

The Authors Guild is also pursuing other lawsuits, including one against OpenAI and Microsoft, as part of a “multi-pronged approach to ensure that the law is clear that AI companies must license the books they use,” according to The Authors Guild Chief Executive Officer Mary Rasenberger.

“Licensing allows authors and publishers to be compensated and to require the use of filters that ensure their works do not appear in outputs unless separately licensed,” Rasenberger wrote in an email to The Daily.

The Authors Guild is working with Congress to create national legislation that will require licensing.

“The Guild is up for the challenge,” Rasenberger wrote. “This is something we have been doing for 114 years, and the moment we are in today, while challenging, also presents opportunities for us to firmly realize our mission.”

For Wolff, this support is critical for the next generation of writers.

“I’m lucky that I’m able to have a life as a writer where I enjoy the profits of my work,” Wolff said. “I worry much more about the young writers I’ve worked with at Stanford and elsewhere being able to do that in the face of these technological challenges, because they’re very hard to grasp and to combat.”

Some authors are waging these difficult legal battles to achieve a sense of credit for their work, outside of the settlement payment.

“Attribution … is very important to the vast majority of authors, and not just academic authors,” Goldstein wrote. “The great majority of countries have something called the ‘moral right of attribution’ on the books to ensure that authors are credited for their work. The U.S., alone among industrialized nations, has no such moral right, which is a terribly unfortunate situation for authors generally.”

As Stanford authors and AI companies continue to navigate copyright challenges for training, both sides must navigate a “foreign land,” Wolff said, meeting at the intersection of creative rights and technological growth.

#439. Mayo Clinic discovers new ways to fight the “zombie” cells that drive aging

Seven studies from Mayo Clinic are revealing new details about senescent cells, often called "zombie" cells because they stop dividing but remain active in the body. These aging cells release inflammatory substances that can contribute to diseases associated with growing older.

The research, published in Nature journals, was conducted by scientists at Mayo Clinic's Robert and Arlene Kogod Center on Aging. Rather than simply confirming that senescent cells play a role in aging, the studies investigate how to recognize different types of these cells, why they produce harmful inflammation and how their effects might be reduced.

The findings also contribute to a nationwide effort to create detailed maps of senescent cells throughout the human body. Known as the Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet) Consortium, the project brings together researchers across the United States and receives funding from the National Institutes of Health Common Fund. Its goal is to document where senescent cells occur in different tissues and how they change throughout a person's lifetime.

"This body of work is moving the aging field forward to precisely identify, understand and target the specific senescent cells that contribute to age-related dysfunction, which is the necessary groundwork to develop new therapies that support healthy aging," says Marissa Schafer, Ph.D., a Mayo Clinic scientist whose laboratory directed several of the investigations.

Scientists Discover That Zombie Cells Are Not All Alike

Identifying senescent cells reliably has long been a major obstacle for scientists studying aging. The new research shows that these cells are much more varied than previously understood. Their characteristics differ depending on where they are located in the body and which biological processes caused them to become senescent.

To help address this challenge, researchers developed a tool called the SenMayo gene set. It uses patterns of gene activity to identify senescent cells more consistently across tissues and experimental systems.

Scientists tested the approach using bone biopsies from older adults. They also found that the gene signature became less prominent after senescent cells were removed in preclinical experiments. Since its development, SenMayo has become a widely used resource for researchers investigating aging and related diseases.

Mayo Clinic endocrinologist Sundeep Khosla, M.D., served as senior author of the study, "A new gene set identifies senescent cells and predicts senescence-associated pathways across tissues." Other Mayo Clinic researchers who contributed to the paper include Nathan LeBrasseur, Ph.D., Diana Jurk, Ph.D., João Passos, Ph.D., and LaTonya Hickson, M.D..

A Hidden Connection Between Mitochondria and Chronic Inflammation

Other studies explored one of the most damaging features of zombie cells: their tendency to generate ongoing inflammation.

The researchers discovered additional biological processes that explain why senescent cells continue producing inflammatory signals. Several of these processes involve malfunctioning mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for producing much of a cell's energy.

In one pathway, DNA and RNA originating from mitochondria activate immune responses that contribute to inflammation. Another involves changes in mitochondrial metabolism that influence inflammatory gene activity through epigenetic mechanisms. These mechanisms affect how genes are switched on or off without changing the underlying DNA sequence.

The discoveries suggest that researchers may eventually be able to interfere with the processes responsible for chronic inflammation while leaving the senescent cells themselves intact. This would offer another approach to treatment beyond attempting to destroy every harmful aging cell.

Experimental Treatments Show Promise Against Aging Cells

The Mayo Clinic team also investigated possible ways to reduce the number of senescent cells or limit the damage they cause.

The approaches included senolytic medications, which are designed to eliminate senescent cells, a natural compound present in tomatoes, and genetic techniques intended to target particular populations of aging cells.

When tested in older mice, these interventions produced encouraging results. Depending on the approach, researchers observed reductions in inflammatory measures and improvements in several aging-related outcomes, including physical frailty, inflammation in the brain and performance on cognitive tasks.

Another investigation uncovered a possible way to measure aspects of biological aging through blood testing. Researchers identified IL-23R as a potential biomarker that might eventually help scientists evaluate aging-related changes and determine whether future treatments are working.

These results remain preliminary in terms of medical applications. More research will be necessary to establish whether the approaches are safe and effective in people. Nevertheless, the experiments provide valuable clues for developing treatments that more precisely address the cellular processes involved in age-related diseases.

A New Direction for Healthy Aging Research

Together, the seven studies represent an important shift in how researchers approach cellular aging. Scientists are moving beyond the broad observation that senescent cells contribute to age-related decline and toward identifying which populations cause the greatest problems, what makes them harmful and how those specific effects could be controlled.

The findings also challenge the idea that eliminating senescent cells must always be the primary treatment strategy. By understanding the molecular pathways that trigger persistent inflammation, researchers may be able to develop therapies that block damaging signals without removing the cells responsible for producing them.

Mayo Clinic researcher Darren Baker, Ph.D., served as senior author of the paper "Senotypes define the diverse landscape of senescent cells," which examines the varied characteristics of these aging cell populations.

The work also supports Mayo Clinic's broader Precure Research initiative. This program aims to equip physicians with better ways to identify and intervene in harmful biological changes before they develop into disease or advance into conditions that are difficult to treat.

By revealing how zombie cells differ, why they promote inflammation and which processes might be targeted, the research offers a more detailed roadmap for future treatments designed to support healthier aging.

Ripple has received final approval from the U.S. Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) to launch a national trust bank.

Its CEO, Brad Garlinghouse, wrote on X that Ripple National Trust Bank will be a federally supervised platform to support Ripple USD Ripple USD (RLUSD) and offer digital asset custody services to enterprises.

"We've always said crypto and banks work better together — this is what that looks like. Real innovation, real accountability, federal oversight," Garlinghouse said.

The full approval comes less than a year after Ripple obtained conditional approval from the OCC. Ripple filed the initial application for the U.S. banking license in July 2025, and received conditional approval on Dec. 12, 2025.

National trust bank charters do not permit deposit-taking or lending and are distinct from full-service commercial bank licenses. Ripple’s structure places reserve management and custody under federal supervision while leaving issuance with the New York-regulated entity.

According to Ripple's announcement, Ripple National Trust Bank is authorized to manage the segregated reserves supporting RLUSD, act as collateral trustee of those reserves for RLUSD holders, and provide fiduciary digital asset custody services. RLUSD will continue to be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company, Ripple's NYDFS-regulated limited purpose trust company. The national charter also adds federal OCC supervision to the existing state-level oversight of the stablecoin.

RLUSD, which launched in December 2024, has grown to a $2.5 billion market capitalization, the company said. Users utilize the stablecoin across Ripple's payment infrastructure and as collateral within Ripple Prime.

Meanwhile, Circle Circle Internet Group, Inc. (CRCL) received its final OCC approval in July this year, bringing the USDC reserve under federal supervision. BitGo BitGo Holdings, Inc. (BTGO) received its green light in December 2025, while Coinbase, Crypto.com, and World Liberty Financial have gained conditional approvals this year, among others.

This wave of OCC approvals to crypto firms has been criticized by some lawmakers, such as Sen. Elizabeth Warren, who accused these of violating the National Bank Act and posing risks to the financial system.

Crypto industry group The Digital Chamber rejected such accusations, arguing that Warren was mischaracterizing the issue as an improper expansion of banking privileges. It noted that national trust charters are limited to trust and related fiduciary activities, and that the firms voluntarily submitted to federal supervision.
