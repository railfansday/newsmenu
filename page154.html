If you've ever tried explaining Donnie Darko to someone who hasn't seen it, you know how quickly things get complicated. And while the 2026 movie schedule has plenty to offer, Richard Kelly's sci-fi cult classic celebrates its 25th anniversary on Oct. 26. As the anniversary approaches, the cast has been looking back on the movie that inspired countless theories about time travel, alternate realities and a very unsettling rabbit. Now, Noah Wyle, whose character had to explain some of that stuff, has joked that he still isn't entirely sure what happened.

That's pretty funny coming from an actor who's spent so much of his career making complicated medical jargon sound natural on ER and, more recently, The Pitt. In fact, Wyle has previously joked about just how much medical knowledge he's picked up playing doctors. In an interview with People, Wyle reflected on Darko's devoted following and the unlikely amount of recognition he still gets for his small role. He's grateful for the attention, though he'd probably prefer someone else handle the plot summary:

Its lasting impact on cinephiles of all ages has been extremely gratifying. Just don’t ask me what the movie is about…

I love that answer, especially considering Wyle played Dr. Kenneth Monnitoff, the high school science teacher who fields Donnie's questions about time travel and parallel universes. If anyone in the movie should have a handle on what's happening, you'd think it's him. Apparently, 25 years hasn't made the explanation much easier.

Wyle also talks about getting involved in the 2001 film, which stars Jake Gyllenhaal as a troubled teenager who begins seeing a mysterious figure wearing a frightening rabbit costume. It turns out Drew Barrymore, who helped produce Donnie Darko through her Flower Films company and played English teacher Karen Pomeroy, helped bring the Enough actor aboard. As Wyle explained:

We shared a publicist and wanted to work together. I thought the script was cool — confusing, but super cool.

That sounds about right for Donnie Darko. Kelly's screenplay jumps between teenage angst, questions about fate and increasingly strange science-fiction territory. Even after several viewings, there's plenty to debate, and fans have spent decades doing exactly that. The confusion apparently started before the cameras even rolled.

As for Wyle's actual experience making the movie, he remembers it being pretty painless. He wasn't on the production for long, although his character's conversations with Donnie became some of the film's more memorable moments:

I think my part was shot out in about three days. I have nothing but fond memories of working on it.

Darko's journey after filming was considerably bumpier. In fact, Christopher Nolan helped save the flick from going straight to television before it eventually opened in limited release on Oct. 26, 2001, just weeks after the Sept. 11 attacks. A film featuring an airplane engine crashing into a teenager's bedroom was understandably a difficult sell at the time. It made roughly $517,000 during its original domestic theatrical run before finding a much larger audience through home video and subsequent releases.

The Falling Skies alum told the outlet that the movie still gives him considerable "street cred," possibly more than his other work. That's saying something for an actor who spent years playing Dr. John Carter on ER and is now leading one of TV's most talked-about medical dramas.

And with Kelly planning to expand the Donnie Darko story through a sequel novel and potential films, fans may eventually have even more material to puzzle over. As for Wyle, I think he's earned the right to enjoy the movie's legacy without having to explain every last detail of its timeline. Frank the rabbit can deal with that.

Peacock’s Crystal Lake is an unexpected treat in a lot of ways, taking one of horror’s most iconic figures—Jason Voorhees of the Friday the 13th franchise—and digging into the universe’s background by focusing on his mother, Pamela. Famously the actual serial killer in the original film, Pamela’s development into a psychotic killer who inspired her son to become the psychotic killer of all time is the engine that drives Crystal Lake, finding the damaged humanity behind the terror. If you’ve burned through all eight episodes of Crystal Lake’s first season, and you’ve worked through the streamalike series with similar DNA, it’s time to check out some movies—starting with the perfect companion film, 2022’s Pearl.

The second film in Ti West’s X trilogy (starting with X and concluding with 2024’s MaXXXine) is the ideal film to watch after Crystal Lake. Like the series, it explores a female serial killer’s backstory and recontextualizes your understanding of their motives and psychology. It also requires that you be familiar with the original story—while you can certainly watch Crystal Lake or Pearl without having seen the Friday the 13th films or X, you’ll be missing out on a lot if you do.

In X, a group of young people descends on a dilapidated old farm to film an adult movie in Texas in 1979, and by the end of the film most of them are dead, killed by the farm’s elderly couple Howard (Stephen Ure) and Pearl (Mia Goth), with Pearl presented as the most deranged and dangerous of the two. It’s a riveting update to the classic splatter movie template, and Goth nails her dual role as the Final Girl Maxine and the psychotic, murderous Pearl. The prequel, Pearl, jumps back to 1918, when Pearl (Goth again) was a young woman living with her strict mother and paralyzed father. Pearl has big dreams of being an entertainer, but her mother tries to trap her to ensure she’s there to care for her parents and work the farm. Pearl’s lonely unhappiness and frustration (both mental and, er, physical) inspire a slow boil of tension as her grip on sanity frays, and her anger toward her parents curdles into something frightening.

Like Crystal Lake, Pearl takes a killer who seems fairly one-dimensional in their original presentation and gives them a deeper back story and psychological framing. Pamela Voorhees wasn’t just crazy—she was driven to madness by the horrible things that happened to her and her son, and the awful way she’s treated by her fellow humans. Similarly, Pearl is a hopeful and innocent young woman who is slowly molded into a killer by the events of her life. Both the series and the film serve as reminders that monsters are rarely born—they’re made. And it’s usually the casual cruelty of the people around them that does the making. If you loved Crystal Lake’s fresh take on Pamela, Pearl is the perfect choice for a movie night. Stream Pearl on Hulu or rent it on Prime Video.

Need more serial killer movies that hit the Crystal Lake vibe? Never fear—here are some other excellent choices to pair with the series.

If you’ve never actually seen the original Friday the 13th (or if it’s been a while), this is a must-watch for fans of Crystal Lake. Although the series takes some liberties to update the timeline, it’s still intended as a glimpse into the events that shaped both Pamela and Jason Voorhees into inhuman (and somewhat supernatural) monsters. Initially given savagely bad reviews, the film was a surprise blockbuster and is now regarded as a classic of a genre it helped to create in the first place; rewatching it today not only helps contextualize Crystal Lake, it puts the entire slasher genre into perspective. Stream Friday the 13th on Netflix or rent it on Prime Video.

The success of Friday the 13th led to a rush of copycat films, of course. Most of those weren’t particularly good or even interesting, but Sleepaway Camp is an exception (although it was similarly savaged by critics at the time). Now a cult classic, Sleepaway Camp has two things that recommend it to fans of Crystal Lake and Friday the 13th: a weird sensibility that gives it an otherworldly atmosphere that enhances the sense of dread, and what is probably the most unexpected twist ending in the history of slasher films (and it’s probably a Top 10 twist in general). Stream Sleepaway Camp on The Roku Channel, Kanopy, or Tubi, or rent it on Prime Video.

One reason Crystal Lake exists is the enduring power and influence of slasher films in general and Friday the 13th specifically. Few recent films have tapped into that dreamy nightmare feel like Jane Schoenbrun’s Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma. As with most of Schoenbrun’s work, the film delves into themes of identity, subverting queer tropes, and the malleable nature of reality itself. If you’re looking for a slasher story that’s less straightforward than Crystal Lake while dealing with some similar themes of transformation and destiny, this is it. Stream Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma on Mubi.

If you’ve seen all the Friday the 13th movies and want more slasher horror with a bit more edge, check out The Burning. Is it a great film? Not really. Combining the straightforward slasher thrills of Friday with some of the villain motivations of fellow classic franchise A Nightmare on Elm Street, The Burning dials the blood and guts up to 11 (and beyond) and remains notoriously gory even by today’s standards. But if your fave part of Crystal Lake is the frequently over-the-top gore and violence, this is an ideal movie choice. Stream The Burning on The Roku Channel or Kanopy.

Want a slasher film that explores the psyche of a female killer the way Crystal Lake deconstructs Pamela Voorhees? Check out May. A fragile young woman, May struggles to find connection with the people around her, and slowly descends into a fantasy world that inspires her to literally make her own ideal friend. What makes May so interesting is the depth of the exploration of May’s personality that’s similar to how Pamela is handled in Crystal Lake, tracing each step of her stumble into a bloody hell. Stream May on Tubi or rent it on Prime Video.

If you are part of the Windows 11 Insider program, you may have finally received the option to test your internet speed directly from Quick Settings in the latest experimental build. That means no more going to a browser to see if your new broadband package is as speedy as advertised.

#4680. Doctors Just Discovered a New Type of Depression

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

NEARLY ONE IN four cases of depression may fall into this category. A study published in the British Journal of Psychiatry identified “circadian depression,” and people with it have an internal 24-hour clock that’s out of sync with their external environment.

Like really out of sync. We’re not talking about jet lag. This is when your body’s internal timing network just can’t regulate your body’s systems.

It's important to get this out of the way upfront: Circadian depression isn't in the DSM-5—the “psychiatry bible” mental health professionals use to make diagnoses. In other words, it's not an official condition yet. But it’s important to know about. We had mental health experts break it down.

CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION IS a subtype of depression caused by circadian disruption, which is when your body’s circadian rhythm doesn't align with your external environment. People with circadian depression have disrupted sleep-wake cycles, lower levels of daytime activity, low energy, and weight gain during depression episodes, according to the study.

People who were classified in the study as having circadian depression were more likely to have severe symptoms of depression, including:

People with circadian depression also seem to be more likely than those with other forms of depression to have other conditions like ADHD, anxiety, bipolar disorder, obesity, or insulin resistance.

THE STUDY’S RESEARCHERS analyzed clinical and genetic data from more than 10,000 Australians with depression. To determine whether people had the proposed subtype, circadian depression, rather than general depression, they looked at factors like a person's social jetlag (the mismatch between their internal clock and external schedule), whether they were night owls, how tired they felt in the morning, and whether they experienced excessive daytime sleepiness. They also looked at whether people had poor responses to antidepressant treatment, and people with severe symptoms that were a mix of bipolar disorder, depression, and somatic disorders.

About 23 percent of participants fell into the category of circadian depression. This is more than a label. It’s important to recognize because, as the researchers pointed out, people with circadian depression tend to be less responsive to traditional antidepressant medications. That means treatment tailored to their subtype is especially important.

A FEW THINGS are happening here. First is people’s sleep schedules getting thrown off, possibly from chronic inflammation and a messed-up metabolism. There is prior evidence of a link between circadian rhythm and these two factors (though researchers are still on the fence on what causes what).

“The body’s internal clock helps coordinate changes in the brain throughout the day," says Hillary Ammon, PsyD, clinical psychologist at the Center for Anxiety and Women’s Emotional Wellness. That includes a feeling of alertness and motivation during conventional waking hours. When the timing is off, these coordinated changes are also affected.

“Increased tiredness, low motivation, or decreased focus can all be factors that lead to depression and also be traits of depression,” says Ammon. Having an internal clock that's off schedule also impacts your metabolism and stress hormone cortisol.

Increased cortisol release at night can lead to an increase in unease and difficulty sleeping. Changes in metabolism rates can cause weight gain. Weight gain and decreased activity are classic symptoms of depression.

The concept of circadian depression is not an entirely new idea, says Aaron P. Brinen, PsyD, assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. “We've known that there is a link to sleep in a number of different psychiatric disorders, including depression,” he says. “When people are depressed, their sleep gets disrupted and vice-versa—when sleep is disrupted, you're more likely to get depressed.”

SINCE CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION was just discovered, there is no set treatment for it yet. But doctors who treat depression say the new discovery is a huge reminder that depression requires tailored treatments.

In the study, for example, the researchers found that people with circadian depression didn’t respond as well to standard antidepressants like SSRIs or SNRIs. In fact, people who meet the criteria for circadian depression might experience more side effects on these medications. Their suggestions? Treatments like light therapy and melatonin may reset the body's internal clock and ease symptoms.

Therapists spend a bit of time with the client anyway trying to pinpoint the drivers of their depression, says Brinen. “If it's being driven by sleep or sleep disruption, you're going to target that.”

The basics around mental health still stand. If you're having symptoms of depression, talk with a mental health professional about an evaluation and appropriate treatment. Plus, don’t be an armchair therapist and try to diagnose yourself. “I would be more mindful of the symptoms versus the diagnosis,” says Ammon.

Your provider can give you a full evaluation and recommend appropriate next steps tailored to your situation. “It may be that you need to be more thoughtful about sleep, travel, and work schedules,” Brinen says.
