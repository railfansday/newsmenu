This week Garmin's screenless Cirqa smart band received a firmware update that fixes one of its most obvious gaps: the lack of alerts from your phone. With Version 4.20, you can now set up your smart band to buzz your wrist for texts, calls, and other smart notifications from your phone. The addition is listed on Garmin's forums as "Support vibration for smart notifications/incoming calls." Reaction has been upbeat, too—users on the Garmin subreddit have been glad to see the feature arrive.

Amusingly, Garmin's own FAQ still states that the Cirqa does not vibrate for text messages, phone calls, or other smartphone notifications. Clearly that page is now out of date, so Garmin will want to correct it. Since Garmin doesn't yet have step-by-step instructions, I tested the text-message vibrations myself. Here's how you can set up your Cirqa smart band to vibrate for calls and texts, in addition to a number of other promising updates to check out.

To receive these alerts, your band must be running firmware 4.20 or later. Here is how to get everything configured.

Update the firmware. Open your Garmin Connect app and sync your Cirqa, so the app can check for and install version 4.20 or newer.

Grant the necessary phone permissions. In your phone's settings, make sure Garmin Connect has permission to access SMS, Contacts, and Phone.

Configure notifications in Garmin Connect. Back in Garmin Connect, tap the Menu (or More) tab. Select Garmin Devices, then choose your Cirqa Smart Band. Go to Notifications & Alerts (or Smart Notifications) and turn on text and call alerts.

Troubleshooting tip: If alerts aren't coming through, check that Do Not Disturb, Sleep Mode, and any aggressive Battery Saver settings are turned off on both your phone and the band.

Duration alerts: Duration time alerts for speaking out now accumulate.

Stress alerts: Stress alerts are now blocked while Auto Activity Detection is recording.

Bedtime reminders: Fixed an issue where the bedtime approaching notification sometimes failed to trigger.

Live Activity: Heart rate now appears on the Live Activity screen before recording begins.

Pinned activity bug: Fixed an issue where the pinned activity reverted to Timed Activity after changing the phone's language.

Workout editing: Fixed a problem that prevented editing reps during a workout.

Stability: General system stability improvements.

#5499. Bill calling for nationwide plug-in solar adoption introduced in House

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A national bill proposing the legalization of plug-in solar was introduced in the House of Representatives on Oct. 19.

Since late 2025, legislative bodies have been passing bills permitting the consumer-level use of these portable solar generation devices within particular states. HR 10851, also known as the “Socket Full of Sunshine Act,” is the first bill to be drafted supporting nationwide use of plug-in solar products.

Unlike interconnected solar projects, which require permits for construction, plug-in solar setups use one or more solar modules connected to a microinverter that literally plugs into an AC outlet. Rep. Rob Menendez, a Democrat congressman from New Jersey, authored the bill.

“Families are getting squeezed by electric bills that keep going up,” Menendez said in a press release. “Plug-in solar is a simple, affordable way to cut those bills, but outdated rules and unnecessary fees are standing in the way.”

Socket Full of Sunshine proposes removing any interconnection barriers for portable solar systems; and system owners would not be required to obtain utility approval to use them or incur any insurance or operating fees. If enacted, this would apply to plug-in solar systems with a power rating of 1.2 kW or lower.

This legislation would amend a section of the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 (PURPA), a historic bill that made utilties reponsible for purchasing energy from alternative sources, like wind and solar. Through it, Socket Full of Sunshine would require state utility regulators to initiate preparations for plug-in solar within one year of passage, and establish a standard for it within two years.

The bill would also create a Dept. of Energy grant program for states to issue rebates to low-to-middle-income residents for purchasing plug-in solar systems. Those rebates would not exceed $1,000 and would only be eligible if 55% of the components purchased originate from the United States.

Menendez specifically cites protecting renters’ rights to using this technology, a segment of the population that is often excluded from traditional solar projects. A landlord could not prevent a tenant from purchasing and using a plug-in solar system if this legislation passed, with some exceptions in regards to spacing limitations.

Socket Full of Sunshine through the Environmental Protection Agency would also create a standard for estimating how much a plug-in solar system would save consumers and would establish consumer safety standards for these products.

“As America works to rebuild its domestic solar manufacturing industry, we need to make sure growing demand for solar energy also supports the workers and companies making these products here at home,” said Mike Carr, executive director of the Solar Energy Manufacturers for America Coalition, in a press release. “The Socket Full of Sunshine Act recognizes that expanding access to affordable solar energy and supporting American manufacturing should go hand in hand. By prioritizing American-made products and tying federal rebates to domestic manufacturing and content requirements, this legislation can help create new demand for U.S.-made solar products and strengthen our domestic supply chains.

#3868. Playwright Jocelyn Bioh Stars in ‘School Girls; Or, the African Mean Girls Play’ on Broadway

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jocelyn Bioh is center stage on the Samuel J. Friedman Theatre in a cream ensemble—hair perfectly coiffed, ready to do business. She’s playing Eloise Amponsah, a scouter for the Miss Ghana pageant 1986, in the Broadway production of School Girls; Or, the African Mean Girls Play. In this role, Eloise has one goal: Find the girl talented and poised enough to not only win Miss Ghana but also go on to represent Ghana in the Miss Global Universe pageant. As one by one the girls try to impress Bioh’s beautifully imposing pageant scout, her character has finally had enough. The Miss Ghana pageant isn’t really about the teenage girls competing, it’s about the hunt for a worthy successor to the crown. In a Ghanaian accent with notably British affectations, Eloise lays bare what she wants, what all the girls attending the Aburi Girls’ Boarding School want. “I want for once in my life to finally be seen,” she exclaims.

It had been years since Jocelyn Bioh acted onstage—nine, to be exact. And yet, Bioh felt completely at peace when she finally treaded the boards again. “It was so strange, because when I was getting ready to go on in front of an audience on Friday night, I felt no nerves.” It may have helped that the critically acclaimed play Bioh was acting in was her very own.

For one whirlwind weekend in October, Bioh joined the glitzy cast she’d helped select, including Tony nominee Jasmine Amy Rogers, The Gilded Age star Denée Benton, and Tony winner Patina Miller.

Set entirely in Ghana in the cafeteria of the Aburi Girls’ Boarding School in 1986, School Girls follows a group of teenage girls led by their fearsome queen bee, Paulina Sarpong (Benton), as they prepare to audition for the Miss Ghana pageant. When a biracial student from Ohio, Ericka Boafo (Rogers), transfers to the school and threatens to upend Paulina’s very curated social order and chance to compete in the Miss Ghana pageant, hilarious chaos ensues.

Despite her acting bona fides, Bioh had no intention of performing in the play, but life—and Patina Miller’s schedule, which includes starring in Power Book III: Raising Kanan—had other plans. “We knew we wanted Patina,” says Bioh. But, alas, alongside her acting projects, Miller had committed to running the New York City Marathon in November. And she had to miss another weekend of performances, a mere week and a half after School Girls’ opening night. Bioh knew that losing a top-billed star for a number of performances during a pivotal time for a new Broadway play to establish itself, without an exciting replacement, could cripple the momentum of the show.

Enter Tony-nominated School Girls director, Whitney White. “Whitney was like, “Well, I have an idea…” Bioh says with a laugh. A fellow multihyphenate, White has collaborated with Bioh on a number of projects in the past, including Jaja’s African Hair Braiding, Bioh’s play set in a Harlem hair salon, which earned White a Tony nomination for best director. “She’s been trying to get me back onstage for quite a long time,” says Bioh.

In the past, Bioh always politely declined. “It just didn’t feel it was right,” she says. But something about School Girls felt different. “I don’t know why I said yes this time,” she admits. “But this play, what this play means to me, being able to do that on a Broadway stage. That just felt like that’s an offer you can’t pass up.”

School Girls is in part inspired by an actual controversy that surrounded the Miss Ghana pageant in 2011 that saw an American-born woman compete in the pageant. “It was like a scandal in the Ghanaian news media because they were like, ‘Who is this light-skinned girl who is American and doesn’t know the culture?’ It was a scandal there,” Bioh says. In Bioh’s hands, School Girls manages to blend those events with elements inspired by Tina Fey’s classic 2004 film Mean Girls about the cutthroat nature of teenage girlhood, presenting a rip-roaring play that packs in an exploration of beauty standards, colorism, colonialism, opportunity, class, friendship, the African diaspora, and beauty pageant culture in a taught 80 minutes, with humor and pathos to spare.

Bioh and Denée Benton in rehearsal for School Girls.

A graduate of Ohio State and Columbia’s MFA playwriting program, Bioh appeared in the Broadway production of The Curious Incident of the Case of the Dog in the Night-Time in 2014, as well as multiple Off Broadway productions of works written by Pulitzer Prize–and Tony Award–winning playwright Branden Jacobs Jenkins. In 2017, she was nominated for a Lucille Lortel Award for outstanding featured actress in a play for Jenkins’s Everybody. A true multihyphenate, Bioh was still writing and producing plays throughout this time, including 2015’s Nollywood Dreams and School Girls, which was originally produced Off Broadway at MCC in 2017.

“It was never my intention to step away [from acting],” says Bioh. “I just went where the water was flowing. In this case, that original production of School Girls just did so well. It did so much better than I ever imagined. My life completely changed after opening night of that play. I can very clearly mark my career from the night before and the day after opening night.”

Many theater people both act and write—see Harvey Fierstein’s Torch Song Trilogy, Heidi Schreck’s What the Constitution Means to Me, and, of course, Lin-Manuel Miranda’s Hamilton. While it wasn’t necessarily her plan, with her stint in School Girls, Bioh proves she can throw herself into that realm if she so chooses. Her acting—grounded, natural, and honest—seems to be a natural extension of her writing, which often possesses a keen yet empathetic eye for sectors of the world that are often overlooked.

Bioh, Rogers, and Heather Alicia Simms in rehearsal for School Girls.

It’s no wonder her writing career took off after School Girls, taking her to Hollywood to write for television shows like Natasha Lyonne’s Russian Doll (2019) and Kathryn Hahn’s Tiny Beautiful Things (2023). Still, her heart belonged to the theater—and she made space for it. Bioh made her Broadway debut as a playwright in 2023 with Jaja’s African Hair Braiding, which would earn a Tony nomination for best play, a win for best costume design, and a Special Tony Award for hair and wig design.

Given Bioh’s rising star, it’s no surprise that Manhattan Theatre Club would want to revive Bioh’s breakthrough play, School Girls, for a larger Broadway audience. And although she wasn’t nervous, stepping back onto the stage for the first time in nearly a decade, Bioh was confronted with a well of emotions, specifically visions of her past and all the work that led up to that moment. “Me being a server at Carmine’s, checking coats at every restaurant in New York, handing out flyers for Shrek the Musical in Times Square—fighting with the girls who hand out flyers to Chicago, who always would do high kicks,” she says. “It was just like, every sort of crazy starving-artist struggle moment all just flashed before me. I just became so overwhelmed with emotion.”

Still, reacclimating to stage performance wasn’t easy. “We were so focused on the actual production of the play, I really put the whole stepping into it out of my mind until after opening night. We opened and I had a couple of down days, and that’s when I was like, Well, let me crack open the script and make sure I know all the lines.” Bioh had just one dialect session with the cast’s vocal coach and a single rehearsal before she stepped into the show on Friday, October 9.

At that rehearsal, which I attended, Bioh was confident and at ease, but not necessarily word-perfect. It helped that at least one actor on the stage, Heather Alicia Simms, who plays Aburi’s principal, had acted with Bioh years before. “You all know, Jocelyn used to be on these stages,” Simms said after a particularly delicious line reading from Bioh. Her Eloise is arguably as ruthless as the resident Regina George of the school, Paulina. She was Miss Ghana 1966, after all, and will not let anyone forget it.

“I just love really complicated women characters,” says Bioh. “I’m just fascinated with delving in deep and humanizing what I think some would categorize as a villain. I think that’s true of Paulina. I think that’s true of Eloise. I think that’s true of all the girls in the show. They all are multifaceted and have experiences that are weirdly and strangely universal.” While Bioh set out to tell the stories of a number of girls, in a way, she was telling a part of her own story. “My mother went to the boarding school that the play is set in. Both my parents are from Ghana, and she went to Aburi,” says Bioh. “I went to Milton Hershey School in Hershey, Pennsylvania, which is a scholarship-based boarding school. I always knew I wanted to write about my boarding school experience because it was a very specific experience. Most of us who went to that school had academic promise, but didn’t necessarily have the financial means to go to a good program—the school was 70 to 80% Black and brown kids. So I had a very unique boarding school experience, where I went to school with other people who looked like me.”

Benton, Erin Morton, Bioh, Lucia Aremu, Rogers, and Nia Otchere-Sarfo in rehearsal for School Girls.

The cast is composed entirely of Black women, and each actor in the cast gets a throughline and an individual moment to shine, including supporting characters like the confident Ama (Lucia Aremu), shy Nana (Erin Morton), bookish Mercy (Jordan Rice), and the scene-stealing class clown Gifty (Nia Otchere-Sarfo). A rousing musical rendition of Whitney Houston’s “Greatest Love of All” during their audition for Miss Eloise brings the house down, showcasing each girl’s unique comedic chops as well as their vocal prowess. Paulina and Ericka, the most talented singers of the group, go head-to-head with their solos, riffing and wailing attempting to best each other. They couldn’t be more different—Paulina, the Ghanaian mean girl trying to raise her station in life via the pageant, and Ericka, the new girl in school desperately trying to find a place where she fits in. And yet, in that moment, they both want the same thing: to be chosen, and to be seen. “Every Black person has not lived the same life,” Bioh adds. “That’s why it’s so ridiculous how we’re always pushed into these weird monolithic tropes.”

Bioh credits the strength of the cast with getting her through the sometimes terrifying ordeal of acting in her own work. “It’s the best company on Broadway, and they had my back one billion percent,” she says. “They were backstage cheering for me when I got off for my first scene. Anything I needed, they were there, and they were just so supportive.” It’s something she hasn’t necessarily gotten to experience in her dual role as writer and actor. “It’s actually quite special as a playwright. You never get the vantage point of being in the play with the actors you’ve created it with.”

Bioh made it through the weekend without a hitch—almost. “What I forgot and what I was reminded of this weekend is you always got to be prepared for a set piece or a costume piece to go awry. The first night, one of my earrings slipped off. Last night, my necklace slipped off,” she says. “I was like, I’m a pro. I’ve got this. I know how to seamlessly just pick up the earring and keep going. And that’s what I did.”

If you want to see School Girls, you have until November 15. But even those not able to get to New York are in luck—the play is livestreaming its final week of performances, from November 10 to November 15, for all the world to see. Although Bioh won’t be in those performances, now that she’s successfully returned to the stage for the first time in almost a decade, there’s only one question. Is the itch to perform back? “I don’t know about acting in my own stuff. I don’t know about that,” she says. “But never say never."

In mid-September, internet sleuths scoured the website of an obscure Russian organization, the International Club of National Unity. Populated by various Russian politicians, state media propagandists, and security services operatives, the club declares that it was created in “accordance with Decrees of the President of the Russian Federation.” Although the club claims to support “humanitarian missions to the peoples of Ukraine … and other countries facing dire straits” on the basis of “respect, peace, harmony, and mutual assistance,” many of the supernumeraries associated with it are vocal advocates of nuclear strikes against Western capitals and Ukrainian cities.

What makes this instrument of Russian propaganda unusual, however, is that it contains the names of several Americans as members of its leadership team. The most prominent is Columbia University economist Jeffrey Sachs, who has promoted Kremlin talking points that delegitimize the Ukrainian state, including the canard that the 2014 Maidan Revolution was a coup backed by the United States. The recent evolution of Sachs from a widely respected academic to a willing propagandist for a dictatorial regime perpetrating an increasingly bloody and genocidal war cannot be explained without examining the phenomenon of the fellow traveler.

The term “fellow traveler” has a long pedigree. It appeared in the early days of the Soviet Union, when the nascent communist state sought external support and hoped to foment revolutions in the capitalist world. Today, the term has come to describe a range of Western public figures who sympathize with Russia, its leadership, and its war.

Following Russia’s invasion of Crimea and the eastern Donbas in 2014, many of these allies were European. Nowadays, however, they are predominantly American and span the political spectrum. To be sure, one can still find pro-Kremlin stalwarts in Europe, such as former German Chancellor Gerhard Schröder and former Austrian Foreign Minister Karin Kneissl, who now lives in St. Petersburg. Both used to work for Russian energy companies. However, many of Russian President Vladimir Putin’s former European admirers, such as French far-right leader Marine Le Pen, have become critics of Russia since the full-scale invasion in 2022. Ironically, as parts of the European right have tempered their support for the Kremlin, Americans are now supplanting them.

Podcaster and right-wing influencer Candace Owens attends the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum in St. Petersburg, Russia, on June 4.Olga Maltseva/AFP via Getty Images

At the annual St. Petersburg Economic Forum in June, Putin’s American fellow travelers were highly visible, with right-wing podcaster Candace Owens the star of the show. Sachs—who is now a frequent guest on the TV show hosted by Vladimir Solovyov, the most virulent Russian cheerleader for annihilating Ukrainians and bombing Western cities—participated by video from New York. After attending the forum, Andrew Tate and his brother Tristan, who are wanted in Britain on charges of rape and human trafficking, visited a Russian military training ground. They broadcast their positive views of Russia and its military to their predominantly young male global following. Since then, University of Chicago professor John Mearsheimer, who blames the West for Putin’s desire to crush Ukraine, also visited Moscow. Unsurprisingly, the Russian government has used Mearsheimer’s statements to justify its war on Ukraine.

These are not the only prominent Americans in thrall to the Kremlin and its narratives. Others include former Fox News host Tucker Carlson, film director Oliver Stone, Republican Rep. Anna Paulina Luna, right-wing influencer Steve Bannon, and former U.S. National Security Advisor Lt. Gen. Michael Flynn. During her trip to Russia, Owens was interviewed by another fellow traveler, former CNN anchor Rick Sanchez. He is now based in Moscow, where he hosts the Kremlin’s flagship English-language news program on RT, a propaganda channel banned in the United States and Europe since 2022.

Like Carlson before her, Owens praised Moscow’s supposed cleanliness and safety while insisting that any distrust of Russia is an unnecessary “Cold War hangover.” Both Carlson and Owens unwittingly echo the words of fellow traveler Lion Feuchtwanger, an exiled German novelist and playwright who visited the Soviet Union in the 1930s. In the immediate aftermath of Stalin’s mass terror and genocidal famine, Feuchtwanger mused that “everywhere in that great city of Moscow there was an atmosphere of harmony and contentment, even of happiness.”

A view of Red Square in Moscow on Jan. 1, 1935.AFP via Getty Images

To be sure, today’s Russia supporters are a pale shadow of the Western luminaries who sympathized with Moscow in the past, including artists such as Picasso and Fernand Leger, philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre, and writers such as André Gide, Theodore Dreiser, John Dos Passos, Ernest Hemingway, George Bernard Shaw, and even (briefly) W. H. Auden. Today’s Russia-aligned voices may lack artistic and literary greatness, but they make up for it with their vast social media audiences and wide-ranging influence on the MAGA movement.

How, then, should we understand what motivates these contemporary allies of a militaristic, repressive society? One place to start is by examining the Kremlin’s original Western allies. To that end, I recently dove into David Caute’s The Fellow-Travellers, a comprehensive 1973 history of the phenomenon during the Soviet era.

Caute’s book explains that the main impulse of the original fellow travelers stemmed from a belief in progress and the idea that society could be engineered from the top down. Caute also shows that their admiration for the Soviet project stemmed from their deep disillusionment with their home countries and the West in general. Many of these Western intellectuals were concerned about the rise of fascist movements in their countries and believed that communism could serve as a countervailing force.

Consequently, the fellow travelers often overlooked or even justified the regime’s heinous crimes, including the famine-induced genocide that the Stalin regime perpetrated in Ukraine in the early 1930s. At the height of the murderous wave of cultural and political repression known as the Great Terror, many Western intellectuals proudly stood with Stalin’s Soviet Union. In 1937, for instance, a conference on “Peace and Friendship with the Soviet Union” in London was addressed by Dean of Canterbury Hewlett Johnson, book publisher Victor Gollancz, members of the British aristocracy, and Labour Party parliamentarians. Influential Western journalists played an especially pernicious role; chief among them was New York Times correspondent Walter Duranty, who became an enthusiastic supporter of Stalin and whitewashed Soviet crimes.

Just as it did then, today’s Kremlin propaganda seeks to win Western supporters by exploiting their discontents and anxieties. To the Western cultural right, the Kremlin emphasizes Russia’s supposed support for traditional values. To global altruists seeking to avert conflict with a nuclear power, the Kremlin offers Ukraine’s capitulation as the price of peace.

Pro-Russian activists march to commemorate the Soviet victory over Germany in World War II in Washington, D.C., on May 3, 2025.Amid Farah/AFP via Getty Images

As Caute documents, the Soviet Union’s outreach was carried out by the Society of Cultural Relations with Foreign Countries, which collaborated with Western communist parties, as well as by various Soviet friendship societies that targeted non-communists. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Moscow’s foreign influence mechanisms disintegrated, only to be rebuilt under Russian President Vladimir Putin with fresh resources. When Russia first invaded Ukraine in 2014, the Kremlin used these influence networks to spread its narrative that the invasion was actually an intra-Ukrainian civil war, cultivate critics of Ukraine, and denigrate its Western supporters. RT, as the Kremlin’s global, multi-language TV network, played a significant propaganda role.

Following the shutdown of RT in the West, Russia shifted its influence operations to social media. The Kremlin boosted influential voices opposed to the “woke agenda” of LBGTQ rights, secularism, and immigration, also targeting those hoping to avert a larger conflict with Russia and thus also susceptible to Moscow’s narratives. To support these aims, the Kremlin devoted extensive resources to a wide range of initiatives, including the Club of National Unity.

As part of the effort to win over the cultural right, Putin issued Decree 809 in November 2022. The document, titled “On the Approval of the Fundamentals of State Policy for the Preservation and Strengthening of Traditional Russian Spiritual and Moral Values,” is a broad attack on Western decadence and liberalism, counterposing Russia as a traditional, faith-based patriarchal society. The decree closely aligns with the Russian Orthodox Church, whose purged and infiltrated remnants became a major propagandist for the Soviet war effort under Stalin during World War II—and which has been controlled by the Kremlin ever since.

Empires inevitably decline, and Putin’s project will be no exception.

Enhanced propaganda efforts were also facilitated by the “Foundation for the Support and Protection of the Rights of Compatriots Living Abroad,” which secretly disbursed millions of dollars to organizations and individuals, primarily in Europe.

The Kremlin’s efforts to influence the U.S. right are well resourced. It has spent millions of dollars on U.S. influencers and podcasters, not to mention the social media visibility boost that Russia-friendly voices get from a vast network of Russian internet bots. In turn, American allies provide a media platform Russian propagandists such as Alexander Dugin, a far-right nationalist who has long been tasked by the Russian intelligence services with wooing right-wing ideologues in the West. American influencers who have platformed him include Tucker Carlson and Alex Jones, helping them to propagate the view of Russia as a safe haven for Westerners who reject what they regard as the destructive liberal ideological agenda.

The phenomenon of otherwise reputable Western opinion leaders aligning with and fawning over murderous tyrants has a long pedigree on both the left and right. In the 1930s, the former British king and then Duke of Windsor; the industrialist Henry Ford; the aviator Charles Lindbergh, IBM CEO Thomas Watson, and French writer Louis-Ferdinand Céline praised Hitler and the Nazi regime. Similar to Moscow, Berlin built an international network called Auslandsorganisation that sought to win Western public support through various propaganda activities. Fans of Mussolini’s fascism included Columbia University President Nicholas Butler Murray and the poet Ezra Pound. In the 1960s and early 1970s, the North Vietnamese dictatorship could count Mary McCarthy, Bertrand Russell, Sartre, and Simone de Beauvoir among its sympathizers. Noam Chomsky and Jane Fonda visited North Vietnam and expressed support for what they characterized as its anti-imperial agenda. Chomsky would later question the crimes of one of the most vicious 20th century murderers, Cambodia’s Pol Pot.

Actor Jane Fonda meets with North Vietnamese Deputy Prime Minister Nguyen Duy Trinh in Hanoi on July 21, 1972.TF/AFP Files/AFP via Getty Images

The through line that connects many of these fellow travelers is that they seem to be consumed by their domestic political or intellectual hatreds—including contempt for political opponents and various aspects of the societies they live in—to the point that they will embrace the most brutal, genocidal foreign regime: The enemy of my domestic enemy is my friend.

As Caute’s book explains, some of the original fellow travelers became disillusioned with Moscow after the 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, which allied Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union during the first two years of World War II, facilitating their joint invasion of Poland. Others only acknowledged their disaffection in the war’s aftermath as they witnessed Central Europe fall to communist tyranny. In 1949, several former sympathizers—including Gide, Stephen Spender, and Arthur Koestler—contributed to an anthology, The God that Failed, in which they reckoned with the roots of their support for Soviet rule, described their gradual disaffection, and acknowledged their moral failings. More fellow travelers fell away after Stalin’s death and the revelations of his crimes by his successor, Nikita Khrushchev; still others after Soviet tanks crushed the 1956 Hungarian Revolution and 1968 Prague Spring.

Will today’s generation of fellow travelers face a similar reckoning someday? There is little reason to hope for a change of heart. Past generations were aided in their reckoning by the fact that most of Stalin’s mass crimes occurred in darkness. Later revelations led many to reject him. Today’s Putin cheerleaders, by contrast, live at a time when virtually everything is known about the nature of Putin’s regime. There is abundant digital and eyewitness evidence of Russia’s systematic war crimes in Ukraine, including unspeakable horrors inflicted in the regions it occupies. Modern-day fellow travelers ignore these copiously documented facts or dismiss them as irrelevant.

Tucker Carlson interviews Russian President Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin in Moscow on Feb. 6, 2024.Gavriil Grigorov/Pool/AFP via Getty Image

Equally disturbing is their indifference to Putin’s system of censorship and political repression, which stifles free discussion and criminalizes any disapproval of his “special military operation.” U.S. podcasters and pundits who endlessly rail against the alleged suppression of free speech in the United States and present themselves as rigorous truth-seekers have no interest in how this issue is treated by Putin in Russia.

Stalin’s crimes were an order of magnitude greater than what Putin has managed so far. But the current crop of fellow travelers is more reprehensible and dangerous than those of the last century. While support for Stalin by prominent Westerners mattered morally, it had little practical effect—there was no credible way to stop him or avert his crimes. In contrast, the West today has the means to effectively counter Putin’s aggression. Today’s apologists for Putin, by fanning controversy and skewing the public debate, have the power to delay, slow down, or cut off needed aid to Ukraine. In doing so, they are actively contributing to Putin’s war. History will likely judge them far more harshly than their misguided predecessors.

Yet no one will be judged more harshly than the newest member of Putin’s circle of fellow travelers: U.S. President Donald Trump. For years, Trump has glorified Putin and expressed admiration for him while frequently showering Ukraine’s democratically elected president, Volodymyr Zelensky, with contempt. In recent days, however, Trump has gone far beyond expressing personal sympathy for Putin and crossed a geopolitical Rubicon. After initially shifting the United States from an outright ally of Ukraine to a neutral intermediary, Trump has both weakened Ukraine’s ability to defend itself and, by lifting energy sanctions, provided the Kremlin with financial resources that will be deployed against Ukraine. It is disgraceful that he has chosen to do so while Russia is trying to render Ukraine’s major cities uninhabitable with escalating waves of attacks.

Near-universal condemnation of Trump’s alliance with Putin by other Western leaders, as well as substantial support for Ukraine in the Republican Party and its voter ranks, may yet force him to change course. But the cynical amorality of Trump’s latest actions will leave a permanent mark on his already controversial, disruptive, and disgraceful presidency.

#4698. How a London Town House Solved A Contest Between Husband and Wife—With a Drop of Pink in the White Ceilings

How did a Thurloe Square town house in London—one bathed in greenery and sun-diffused light from private communal gardens—become a house divided, one torn between the visions of the men in the house and that of the wife and mother? Despite being outnumbered four to one by her husband and three sons, the wife had a secret weapon in her arsenal: London-based AD100 interior designer and childhood friend Leonora Hamill, who worked with the family on both the cosmetic makeover of their South Kensington home and on the art of compromise.

There’s room for everyone within these 5,000 square feet. Take the drawing room: There are patterned Pierre Frey curtains and a rose-velvet-upholstered Luigi Caccia Dominioni sofa playing with a pair of modernist Pierre Jeanneret chairs with bespoke tweed cushions and a sculptural stark white coffee table by François Bauchet.

Hamill bought the pair of Pierre Jeanneret chairs at auction and added a de Le Cuona tweed cushion. She also found the pair of 1950s Swedish reading lights by Paavo Tynell at auction.
