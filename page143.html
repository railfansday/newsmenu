#1410. Michael Carrick left in limbo as vilified chiefs Omar Berrada and Jason Wilcox meet for crisis summit at Carrington: Inside Man United's day of reckoning after humiliating Bournemouth defeat

Published: 21:16 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 21:32 GMT, 26 October 2026

Michael Carrick is in limbo as Manchester United head coach following a day of crisis talks between the club’s owners and senior executives over his future at Old Trafford.

Carrick remains under huge pressure after Sunday’s home defeat to Bournemouth left United one point above the Premier League relegation zone.

Chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox were at the club’s Carrington training ground on Monday and are understood to have held conference calls with minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe and co-chairmen Joel and Avram Glazer.

It is unclear if United have reached a final decision over whether to sack Carrick or if he will take training on Tuesday morning when the players report back to Carrington to prepare for their next game at Chelsea on Saturday lunchtime.

The squad had Monday off after losing 3-0 to Bournemouth, but Carrick turned up at the training ground to watch a behind-closed-doors game against Hull City while Berrada and Wilcox discussed his future in another part of the complex. It isn’t known if the 45-year-old spoke to them during the three hours he spent there.

Carrick arrived at 1.50pm Monday, 10 minutes before the game involving a mixture of fringe players and youth-teamers kicked off. He watched it with his assistant Steve Holland and Under-18s boss Darren Fletcher before leaving the training ground shortly after Wilcox.

Michael Carrick arrived at Carrington to watch the under-21s - with his bosses deciding his fate

Carrick's future at Old Trafford is in jeopardy after Sunday's awful 3-0 defeat by Bournemouth

To add to the intrigue, Bruno Fernandes was also at Carrington for treatment along with Benjamin Sesko, Patrick Dorgu and Manuel Ugarte, 24 hours after Carrick had dropped his captain for Sunday’s game and left him on the bench.

Although Carrick insisted that his decision was purely for football reasons, it felt like he was punishing Fernandes for criticising United’s tactics in a television interview after the Champions League defeat to Como in Italy last week.

The Portugal star seemed to respond with a veiled dig at Carrick on Sunday night when he liked a cryptic Instagram post that read: ‘Never push a loyal person to the point where they no longer care.’

Carrick’s job is hanging by a thread after losing half of his 12 games this season. United have made their worst start to a league campaign in 40 years, and have failed to win in eight games for the first time since 2015.

Carrick was visibly emotional after the defeat to Bournemouth when the fans gave him a rousing show of support while directing their fury at the Glazers, Ratcliffe, Wilcox and the players.

The former United midfielder appeared to say farewell to supporters afterwards, although he later insisted that was not the case.

It followed a heartfelt five-minute monologue to reporters at the start of his pre-match press conference on Friday, two days after he approached the travelling fans in Como to apologise for a 4-1 defeat.

After playing away in Spain and Italy over the last fortnight, United have a free week because they were knocked out of the Carabao Cup by Brighton and aren’t in action again until Saturday’s trip to Stamford Bridge.

Jason Wilcox and Omar Berrada met for crunch talks as Carrick spent time with the youth team

Carrick has 18 months left on the two-year contract he signed in May after guiding the club back into the Champions League.

If United decide to change head coach again, Eddie Howe and Julian Nagelsmann – both currently out of work and able to take over immediately – are the leading candidates after bookmakers stopped taking bets on Carrick being sacked.

Although Howe is available after leaving Newcastle in the summer, it’s understood that United would still owe his old club around £3million in compensation as well as having to pay off Carrick and his coaching staff.

#4946. Unitree Stock Jumps as Investors Bet Chatbot AI Will Finally Give Robots a Brain

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Unitree Stock Jumps as Investors Bet Chatbot AI Will Finally Give Robots a Brain

Shares of Chinese robot maker Unitree Robotics have jumped more than 60% since Oct. 15, lifting its market value to 271.8 billion yuan (roughly $40.8 billion). Investors are betting that smarter AI will give its robots the brains they lack.

The wager is that the technology behind chatbots can soon operate machines in the physical world, a field called physical AI. If it can, a robot that tidies a room or folds laundry on command stops being a lab demo, and the companies that build the bodies, motors, and joints profit.

Unitree founder Wang Xingxing fueled the rally on Oct. 22 at the World Physical AI Conference in Hangzhou, the company's first partner conference in a decade of operation. He said large models, the chatbot-style AI systems trained on vast amounts of data, could break through in physical AI within a few years.

“If everything goes well, it will take one to two years, otherwise it will take two to three years,” Wang said.

Unitree shares hit the 20% daily limit on Friday, the most a stock may rise in one session, and closed at 624 yuan ($93.55). They rose another 7.69% on Monday to 672 yuan ($100.75).

JPMorgan upgraded Unitree to overweight from underweight on Monday, which is analyst shorthand for owning more of a stock than the market does, and raised its price target to 995 yuan ($149.17). The bank had argued the company leaned too heavily on hardware and was weak in software. It changed its view after hearing from Wang and other executives and reviewing the humanoid lineup in Hangzhou.

Unitree went public on Aug. 19 on the Shanghai Stock Exchange's STAR Market, a tech-focused board. Its G1 humanoid costs roughly $16,000, cheaper than rival humanoids. Researchers have flagged security flaws, including audio, video and spatial data streaming to outside servers without users being notified but the premise has proven interesting enough to push its valuation up.

Unitree already sells robots, while Tesla has only just started producing Optimus robots at its Fremont, California, factory and expects outside deliveries around the end of 2027. Figure AI partnered with OpenAI on its humanoids back in 2024, using OpenAI’s models to power its robots.

With one notable exception, Quentin Tarantino has avoided writing and directing sequels in his career. Now, Once Upon A Time in Hollywood is getting the sequel treatment, at least spiritually. Counting Kill Bill as one project, as Tarantino does, this will be the first time the legend will be part of a continuing story. The movie, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, is written by Tarantino and directed by David Fincher. Surprisingly, it was a comment in a review that convinced the Pulp Fiction director to bring back Brad Pitt’s character from Once Upon a Time.

Over the weekend, Tarantino made an appearance at the AFI Fest in LA for a screening of OUATIH and a 17-minute preview of the new Cliff Booth movie. He talked about the upcoming sequel, which will hit the 2026 movie schedule on November 25 before being able to be streamed with a Netflix subscription on December 23, and why he decided to write it. It all came from a positive review of the first movie. Tarantino commented (via THR):

I tend to not remember good reviews of mine in quotes; I kind of remember the bad reviews in quotes, but I don’t really remember good reviews of mine in quotes. But one quote that I do remember somebody writing about Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, it was like, ‘This seems like the first world Quentin Tarantino has ever created that he would like to live in,’ and there is that aspect.

I wasn’t just audiences who really connected with Booth and Pitt as Booth. Tarantino went on to explain that he loved the character too, saying:

I loved the Cliff Booth character, I loved the way that Brad played him, and there was this aspect of him as sort of like a paperback hero. I could see like a series of paperbacks with Cliff Booth getting involved with this adventure or that adventure or something like that.

That led to The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, which Tarantino was originally reported to be directing in addition to writing, before he decided to step back from directing and bring in Fincher. Tarantino is also writing a novelization of the movie, turning Booth into a literal paperback hero. I have to wonder if there is more ahead from the character, assuming this movie ends up a hit. Tarantino wonders too, adding:

As time went on, I just thought about coming up with a different adventure, getting him in now a misadventure. I wanted to see what would happen with that. I’ve got nothing to do but just invest in this mythology. I invested in it before I did the movie, and then I invested in it in the years since I’ve done the movie, just so I know the mythology. The audience doesn’t need to know everything, but it’s kind of nice that I know everything, and you can trust me that I know it.

Given that Tarantino has expressed that he plans to direct just one more movie in his career, could there be a future where he writes a series of paperbacks about Cliff Booth? I have to admit, I’m intrigued by the idea.

#5267. Meta to sell up to $95B in stock to finance AI infrastructure buildout

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meta Platforms Inc. will sell up to $95 billion worth of stock to finance its data center projects.

The fundraising initiative, which the company announced today, will kick off with a series of transactions cumulatively worth $35 billion. About two-thirds of that sum will come from the sale of common stock. Meta said Nvidia Corp., Advanced Micro Devices Inc. and Broadcom Corp. had expressed interest in buying up to $5 billion of the shares.

The company will sell the common stock alongside $12.5 billion worth of securities it calls Series A depositary shares. Each such instrument corresponds to 1/20th of a so-called Series A mandatory convertible preferred share. The latter securities will turn into Meta common stock on Aug. 15, 2029. Until then, they will trade under a separate ticker symbol on the Nasdaq.

Meta’s funding haul could increase if the banks underwriting the transactions decide to buy more stock. The company will give them the option to buy 37.5 million additional depositary shares and 3.375 billion additional shares of common stock.

Meta intends to follow up the deals with a stock sale that is expected to raise as much as $60 billion. It will be structured as an at-the-market offering, which means that the company will sell shares incrementally over a relatively long time period. It will begin issuing the shares early next year.

The deals will help Meta finance its expensive data center construction program. The initiative is headlined by four so-called AI superclusters that are each expected to provide more than 1 gigawatt of computing capacity. The largest development, a Louisiana campus dubbed Hyperion, is set to provide 5 gigawatts. Meta will commission 10 new power plants to meet Hyperion’s energy requirements.

The company is not relying on stock sales alone to finance its data center investments. Hyperion is a joint venture between Meta and investment firm Blue Owl Capital, which has committed $7 billion to the project. Meta plans to build its Texas supercluster through a similar partnership with BlackRock Inc., the world’s largest asset manager.

The company’s data center network powers its Muse family of large language models. The newest addition to the series, Muse Wonder Full, is one of the most advanced LLMs on the market. It outperformed GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 on multiple popular benchmarks including the DeepSWE collection of coding challenges.

Meta stated today that its AI engineering roadmap places an emphasis on making its models more cost efficient. The company plans to achieve that by lowering its LLMs’ hardware footprint and enhancing the underlying inference infrastructure. Meta also intends to make better use of its off-peak capacity, computing power and energy that is underutilized at times when user traffic is low.

As furious youth protests sweep across Europe, Gen Zers in Serbia have proved that youth can achieve transformative change through nonviolent political engagement. The extraordinary discipline, diligence, and organization exhibited by Serbian students over two pressure-filled years are worthy of a Nobel Peace Prize.

The improbable bid to dislodge the corrupt regime of former President Aleksandar Vucic, which held all instruments of power, also offers a seminal opportunity for the United States and European Union, in and beyond the region. Serbia’s neighbors have often voiced skepticism of the student-led movement, which includes hard-line Serbian nationalists along with pragmatists who grasped the need to blunt Vucic’s charge that the students and their supporters are “anti-Serb.” But Serbian students have created an inspiring, viable model for channeling mass discontent within the political process—provided the new government succeeds.

Fully capitalizing on the dramatic student-led triumph in Serbia means first understanding its ingredients. The sharp contrast between Serbia’s constructive students—operating in a hostile environment—and their attention-hogging counterparts in liberal France is teeming with lessons.

First among them is the commitment to nonviolence. While student unrest featured from the outset of the French school protests, it was the opposite—violence inflicted on peaceful student protesters in Belgrade—that sparked the nationwide student movement in Serbia. A key factor in attracting nationwide support, as opposed to fleeting press attention and opportunistic rallies, was Gandhian student restraint, even as the regime escalated provocations and attacks, notably after a third mass protest in late June 2025.

Unlike the protests that brought down former President Slobodan Milosevic, demonstrators occupied no government buildings, only university faculties. In Novi Sad, the site of the train station tragedy that cost 16 people their lives and ignited nationwide outrage, university students proudly were at pains to show the care they exercised over the facilities. Determination to achieve change—not vitriol—propelled the collaboration and collective decision-making that were central to the success of the movement.

Second, Serbian youth have focused on the essential needs of society, over parochial demands on the cost and conditions for education. Sacrifice—demonstrated in blistering, long-distance marches, marathon bike rides, and tedious meetings—instead of instant gratification is the hallmark of this successful movement. While French youth storm the Bastille, Serbian students have fanned out across the country, creating citizen groups in large towns and small villages, rekindling the belief of ordinary people in participatory democracy.

Third, the movement did not dogmatically reject engagement in the electoral process, as many student activists do across the globe. Anti-corruption protesters in Bosnia and Herzegovina a decade ago, for example, fell for this mistake.

Initially, the Serbian students refrained from any political stance, demanding only that the government discharge its legal and moral duties to the victims of the Novi Sad tragedy. After the resignation of Prime Minister Milos Vucevic in January 2025, the students called for a transitional government to prepare for new elections. It was government intransigence in the wake of the massive rally that March—the largest gathering in Belgrade since the end of World War II—that convinced the students to change course. A clear majority of students understood and accepted their unique role as the only untainted political actor with the standing to oust the regime at the polls.

Unlike many of their youth counterparts in the United States, Serbian students recognized their lack of life experience as a handicap in running for office or running a government office. Instead, they turned to the public, allocating a proportional number of candidate positions to each university faculty across the country. Applicants endured strict vetting, thorough interviews, and several rounds of selection. Only those enjoying a solid reputation in their respective fields cleared the bar.

One absolute criterion dominated the selection process: Nominees could have no political affiliation or background whatsoever. Astutely, the students grasped that the so-called professional Serbian opposition, benefiting from state funding, operating through patronage, and widely seen as ineffectual, would have no credibility with voters. Indeed, the few opposition parties that contested the elections—including the longtime leading opposition party, the Party of Freedom and Justice, headed by former Belgrade Mayor Dragan Djilas—have, as of this writing, failed to clear the threshold. Marking the new political era, even the governing party of former Prime Minister Ivica Dacic—a fixture in Serbian politics and the leader of the Socialist Party of Serbia, founded by Milosevic—also will likely fail to enter parliament.

Now is the moment for the United States and EU to advance political, economic, and people-to-people contact between Serbia and its former Yugoslavia neighbors, restoring trust lost to years of Serbian revisionism under Vucic. Indulged by a pliant EU and both the Biden and Trump administrations, Vucic’s Serbia became the most potent platform for Russian and Chinese influence in all of Europe and the primary source of regional discord. As much as it detests Europe, the Trump administration now needs to actively support the Brussels-led dialogue between Serbia and Kosovo.

And with the Serbian presidential election looming in December, Brussels must move with rare alacrity to support and sustain a successful transition from the entrenched, would-be autocracy of Vucic. Strong economic performance under Vucic led his Serbian Progressive Party to retain more than 100 seats in the Serbian parliament, a far greater seat percentage (some 41 percent) than former Prime Minister Viktor Orban’s Fidesz party now holds in neighboring Hungary (albeit with a similar share of the electorate). Unknown is how many loyalists Vucic will still control in government, particularly in the security services. This makes rationalizing—under clear, accountable authority—the disparate internal security agencies that served the regime an urgent priority.

For its part, the Trump administration should quickly pin the long-delayed ouster of the Russian owners of Serbia’s national oil company on the caretaker government led by Vucic’s party, before it leaves office. When it returns, the Senate should swiftly confirm Michael Young, a rare qualified administration nominee, as ambassador to Serbia, with the admonition that rule of law—not crony capitalism—is the foundation for increased, expedited U.S. investment.

They can take inspiration from Serbia’s students. Although they were widely characterized as “leaderless,” the hundreds (not thousands) of core student activists who engineered this historic election victory actually embodied the essential qualities of leadership: sacrifice; persistence; devotion to the cause, not to personal interest; courage; collaboration; effective communication; sophistication; and, above all, integrity.

#172. Watch out, YouTube — Instagram goes from short-form to 90-minute videos

If you could have 1 million followers on any social media platform, which would you choose? There is a correct answer, and it’s YouTube, assuming you’d like to get paid for your work. But Instagram’s latest update could upend YouTube’s long-held position as the most lucrative platform for creators, if only Instagram plays its cards right.

Instagram announced on Monday that it’s testing a new type of video upload that will allow creators to share “Features,” videos up to 90 minutes long. After years in which every social platform (even LinkedIn!) aggressively pursued short-form video, this is a notable shift in strategy for Instagram.

Short-form, vertical video content has defined the internet of the 2020s, but the creators behind these clips don’t actually make much money directly from platforms like Instagram — the Meta-owned platform has some monetization tools like subscriptions, but it doesn’t typically pay creators on a per-view basis.

“You can get paid by the platform directly, you can get paid by a fan, and you can get paid by a brand,” Instagram head Adam Mosseri explained on the Colin and Samir Show, an interview show about the creator economy. “And right now, the way that it works on Instagram primarily is you primarily get paid by brands. Some people get paid by fans. We have still a small performance-based program, but … we spend more money than we make back by a long shot, and so it doesn’t really make sense to scale up because we’re just burning capital.”

YouTube’s payment model is more straightforward: There are ads on videos, and creators enrolled in YouTube’s Partner Program get 55% of the ad revenue YouTube earns from their videos. That adds up. The platform said last year that it paid more than $100 billion to creators, artists, and media companies in the previous four years.

Instagram and TikTok haven’t been able to replicate YouTube’s model with short-form content, which is inherently trickier to monetize than longer videos because there’s less room to place ads. Many creators see those accounts more like promotional arms for their YouTube channels than meaningful drivers of revenue.

Instagram is encouraging creators to upload longer videos, as it already did eight years ago with the now-defunct IGTV. It’s possible that its parent company, Meta, which makes its billions on advertising, will put ads in those long-form videos, too.

If Meta does add ads, could Instagram meaningfully compete with the YouTube Partner Program for the first time?

“It’s too early to share a long-term monetization model, but we’re exploring ways for creators to earn from brands, fans, and Instagram, including performance-based incentives,” a Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch. “Over time, we think there will be compounding benefits for creators to tell new stories, reach new audiences, and find new partners, which should lead to many monetization opportunities.”

It’s not a particularly promising answer, but it’s also not a no.

Mosseri explained in a video announcement why the platform is suddenly embracing long-form content after scrapping its old video product, IGTV.

“For a long time, I have been against doing long-form video on Instagram,” Mosseri said, noting that shorter content like Reels drives more shares and DMs among users. Instagram VP of Product Tessa Lyons, who has been advocating for this change, finally convinced Mosseri to give long form another shot.

“Reels is still going to be a core part of Instagram. But when you want to go deeper with the creators you love and the content that they’re creating, you should be able to do that on Instagram,” Lyons said in the video.

In other words, Instagram would rather keep fans on its own platform to enjoy both short- and long-form content from a creator than lose them to YouTube. Instagram is testing a feature called “Clips as Reels,” which lets creators make Reels out of their long-form content. The Reels link viewers to the longer video. Viewers can also save the longer video to send to the Instagram app on their TV.

Instagram is currently testing “Features” and “Clips as Reels” in Brazil and will now expand to the U.S. in a small test. The platform is also working on improved discovery features for Live video and a hub on a creator’s profile for episodic Reels, meaning a series of connected Reels similar to TikTok’s playlist feature.

“The thing that the team has been working on that we’re excited to share with you is trying to make it so that the short-form and the long-form video can be symbiotic,” Mosseri said. “We’re going to try to support these different types of formats, both in the mobile app, but also on the TV app, which I think is … going to take some time to get right. But I’m actually very excited about it, and we’re very committed to it long term.”

#5235. Buying Land for a Prefab Home? The Most Important Things About Property for Modular and Manufactured Units

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With so many people being priced out of the housing market these days, more and more homebuyers are considering prefabricated homes—also known as modular and manufactured homes.

And the powers that be are getting wise to this. President Donald Trump signed an executive order to create a task force that accelerates manufactured housing development in October, while Congress codified changes to the sector in its 21st Century Road to Housing Act earlier in the year.

The desire to ramp up a road to these kinds of home is understandable: Prefab houses are way less expensive than your typical home—and often just as beautiful. Indeed, many of today's models can be easily mistaken for a traditionally built home.

Buying land to put a prefab home on is part of the adventure. Do you want to live on 40 lush acres in Stockton, MO? A desert oasis in Joshua Tree, CA? Or how about an off-grid lot in Grants Pass, OR? The possibilities are endless.

If you're dreaming of buying a shiny, new modular or manufactured home, here's what you need to know before you purchase land to put it on.

Prefabricated homes are constructed via an assembly line production in sections inside a factory and then assembled on-site.

Modular homes are constructed to the same standards as conventionally built homes and must meet regional building codes. Homebuyers can choose from one- or two-story homes in a wide variety of floor plans and architectural styles, such as a Cape Cod, Craftsman bungalow, Colonial, A-frame, or farmhouse.

Manufactured homes (also known as single-wide and double-wide) don't need to comply with local building codes but must adhere to the strict federal building codes set by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. Manufactured homes aren't as customizable as modular homes, but they offer several different floor plans and add-ons.

A great number of tiny homes on the market are prefabricated houses that are built in factories and then assembled on site.

Many accessory dwelling units, which are gaining popularity, are also prefabricated homes—otherwise known as modular ADUs.

When it comes to the costs of these types of homes, there are so many variables to consider.

First, there's the cost of the prefab home itself.

The average sales price of a manufactured home is $124,300, according the most recent HUD and Census Bureau data.

Meanwhile, a 600-square-foot, two-story tiny home that comes complete with a roof deck, glass sunroom, and up to four bedrooms is currently for sale on Amazon for $49,999.

But that's not "all-in pricing" by any means.

The cost of the land you put your prefab home on can vary widely—as can the expenses you'll likely incur next.

You've got to pay for site preparation—including clearing the land, laying the foundation, and possibly testing the soil.

Transporting the home to the site, as well as securing permits and inspections, can be quite expensive.

If your home is unassembled, you'll need to hire a contractor to put it together, unless you have the know-how to do it yourself.

And you'll need to cover the costs of setting up utilities like water, electricity, and sewage, and hooking them up as well.

All of these expenses are outlined in greater detail below.

You can't just plop down your new modular or manufactured home anywhere you like.

Many neighborhoods don't allow manufactured homes or tiny homes, so it's important to do your due diligence before signing on the dotted line.

Zoning ordinances, setbacks, and other requirements must be met before your home can be delivered.

Luckily, you don't always have to figure that out yourself, depending on what type of prefab home you buy.

"Our home consultants help future homeowners navigate the land-buying process to accommodate the home they are interested in purchasing," says Danny Warrick, Clayton Homes retail president.

Here are some essential things to look for when selecting the ideal property for your new prefab home:

Zoning ordinances designate certain areas for residential purposes. And your new home will need to meet the size, shape, height, and other requirements of your local zoning board.

As recent Realtor.com research reveals, as of 2026, only about 17% of zoned land across the country expressly allows for manufactured home parks, and manufactured housing is prevented in many jurisdictions by zoning restrictions on minimum floor sizes, architectural design, and by-right installation.

If you buy a manufactured home, your home salesperson can help determine if your home checks all the boxes for a piece of land.

A property's setbacks will also be vital to determining if a home can be placed on a property. Setbacks determine things like underground facilities and breathing room from your neighbors. They also dictate where your house is placed from the front, side, and rear property lines. You probably won't have much to worry about if you have your eye on a large parcel of land, but the setbacks could limit your options in town.

And a warning if you have your sights on a coveted corner lot: "The building department typically considers a corner lot to have two front setbacks, which are typically the largest footage from each street," says Leo Poggione, chairman of the Manufactured Housing Institute and managing partner of Craftsman Homes.

So, you could wind up with two large front yards and a much smaller side yard and backyard.

You'll probably be able to set up electricity and internet services relatively easily if you buy a lot in town. But you could hit a few snags if your property is in a rural setting.

Don't just assume you will be able to get electricity because you see a pole with wires or a cellphone tower nearby. Check with your local individual provider first.

The same goes for internet service. You don't want to end up with sketchy internet or none at all. Instead of the typical installation fee, utility companies charge by the linear foot when running cable to a rural property. And that could really add up, depending on how far the house will sit from the main line.

When the land you want is in town, you might be able to tap into the city water and sewer lines.

"If not, the property will need to pass a percolation test for a septic system and a well," says Warrick.

If the soil perks too fast or too slow, this can add a large expense. Basically, you will have to have an "engineered system" designed to accommodate the septic's leach fields.

You might not think this isn't your problem. After all, you're not the one driving the truck.

Yet, it's critical the truck transporting your new home can safely get to the property you have in mind.

Urban settings can be trickier than rural ones, Poggione notes.

Crowded towns can have low-hanging utility wires, overgrown trees, and department-of-transportation-specific routes for transporting wide loads.

"Always consult with your dealer before purchasing the land or the home, and have them do a site inspection," advises Poggione.

You'll also need to prepare the land for your home once you buy it.

"Trees may need to be cleared, grade works completed, and the home needs a home pad or concrete footings," says Warrick.

Make sure to account for all these expenses when planning the budget for your prefab home, as they can quickly accumulate.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, who turns 81 on Tuesday, was reelected to his fourth term as president of Brazil on October 25. He is the seventh in a family of eight children from the state of Pernambuco, in northeastern Brazil. His political career began in the 1970s, at the Indústrias Villares factory, where he started working as a lathe operator at age 14. He led the metalworkers union in São Paulo in one of the strongest protests against the military regime of the time. He has gone from prison to the halls of power, with a beard reminiscent of that of Che Guevara and Fidel Castro. His missing finger, the pinky on his left hand, is a testament to his journey, having been severed by an industrial press during his time as a metalworker.

He took the victory stage alongside his third wife, Rosângela “Janja” Lula da Silva, whom he has been married to since 2022. He celebrated victory over Flávio Bolsonaro in a runoff election after Bolsonaro failed to win an outright majority in the first round.

Lula, who has become the leader of the left movement in Brazil, surpassed his far-right challenger by about 4 million votes: 51.79% to 48.21%. Bolsonaro has still refused to concede. He contests the Supreme Court’s decision to extend polling hours by two hours—a measure that was previously requested by both candidates. The president of the Senate, Davi Alcolumbre, and the the president of the Chamber of Deputies, Hugo Motta—both from center-right parties—congratulated Lula and declared the election valid.

“This election was a defeat for denialism, a defeat for lies,” Lula said in his victory speech, thanking “the Brazilian people for this generosity they have shown me for so long…the youth who took to the streets to defend democracy, and the elders who went to vote.” Lula looked to the youth during the campaign while denouncing foreign interference—whether from digital platforms or directly from Donald Trump: “This country will not accept being anyone’s backyard.” He assured the nation that he will collaborate with parliament, where the right wing has a strong presence. He has already done so over the past four years, making compromises on his progressive policies.

Last spring, few would have bet on yet another victory for Lula. He began a course of superficial radiation therapy on his scalp following the removal of a cancerous skin tumor, after previously being diagnosed with throat cancer in 2011. Throughout the election campaign, he repeatedly emphasized that he was in good health, assuaging doubts as his age and diagnoses aroused concern.

Lula previously governed Brazil from 2003 to 2010, after being elected in two of three campaigns. His promise at the time was to provide everyone with a roof over their head and three meals a day while honoring agreements with foreign investors. His third term came after he spent nearly a year and a half in prison, which he was sentenced to for corruption. The conviction was overturned due to procedural flaws and apparent bias. He won in 2023, defeating Jair Bolsonaro by less than two percentage points, after which Bolsonaro attempted to maintain his power by force. His policy places government investment at the center of the economy in a country with heavy public debt.
