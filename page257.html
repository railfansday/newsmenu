#3983. I Tried Strava's New Strength Coach Feature, and I'm Optimistic

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Strava has built its reputation on runs, rides, and swims. Strength training, however, has always felt like an afterthought in the app. Strava is trying to close that gap with its new guided strength workouts, and I spent some time with the beginner session to see whether it delivers.

The update brings guided workouts directly in the app. Instead of just logging that you lifted, you're walked through each exercise with animations, and you track sets and reps live as you go. Tap into any exercise, and you'll find form guidance, tips, and the specific muscles it targets.

When you finish, everything (every exercise, set, and rep) is saved automatically to your activity through the Workout Log. Your muscle map then updates to show where the effort went, which is a neat way to see at a glance whether you've been neglecting certain areas.

I started with the beginner workout, and I want to be clear about who it's for: true beginners. There's no equipment required, so you don't need dumbbells, bands, or a gym membership. If you've been lifting for years, this isn't going to challenge you—but that's clearly not the point. For this first workout, it's designed to lower the barrier to entry.

The interface is clean and easy to use. I especially liked the graphics, which make it simple to follow along without having to pause and decipher what a movement is supposed to look like. Everything worked smoothly throughout, with no glitches, lag, or lost progress.

Exercise instructions sometimes cut off. A few of the written descriptions were truncated with no way to see the full text, so I couldn't always read the full guidance on form.

Switching sides isn't always clear. For movements that work one side at a time, it wasn't obvious when I was supposed to switch. A simple cue or prompt would go a long way, especially for beginners who are already juggling a lot of new information.

Strava says more workout series will arrive before the end of the year, which should give the feature more range beyond that first beginner session. The company is also planning additional ways to log strength work:

Screenshot uploads: Snap a screenshot of text-based sets and reps from a training plan or notes app and import it instantly.

Build-your-own workouts: Create custom sessions instead of relying only on Strava's.

More partner integrations: So strength data can flow in automatically from wherever you already train.

These additions are what could turn this from a nice beginner tool into something I'd actually use. The real test will be whether Strava delivers the variety, progression, and range that lifters need to stick with it over time. For now, if you're new to strength work or curious, the free sample workout is an easy way to try it out. I'll be watching to see where Strava takes it from here.

#5852. Arizona’s first NEVI fast chargers are finally open – here’s where

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Arizona’s first three NEVI-funded DC fast-charging stations are finally online, giving EV drivers more places to plug in along I-10, I-19, and I-8.

Getting here took years of planning and procurement, with a Trump administration funding freeze disrupting the state’s broader rollout along the way.

The new Arizona NEVI fast chargers are in Tucson, Green Valley, and Wellton:

They’re the first operating sites in Arizona’s plan to use some of the $76.5 million in federal funding to build as many as 79 stations statewide.

The National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) program was created by the Biden administration’s Bipartisan Infrastructure Law in 2021. But Arizona didn’t begin taking bids for its first round of DC fast-charging stations until January 2024, after developing its deployment plan and working through federal requirements. The first awards followed in August 2024.

Arizona Department of Transportation (ADOT) initially said construction would take about a year after final contracts were signed, with some locations expected to open by late 2025. Construction ultimately began in early 2026.

Then came the sabotage. In February 2025, the Trump administration suspended states’ approved NEVI plans and blocked new funding commitments while it rewrote the program’s guidance. Reimbursements under existing project agreements were allowed to continue.

That had a direct consequence in Arizona: Court records show the state canceled a solicitation for 35 prospective charging locations after losing access to roughly $48.1 million in funding that hadn’t yet been committed to projects.

Arizona joined the states challenging the freeze. A federal judge granted preliminary relief in June 2025 and ruled in January 2026 that the funding had been unlawfully withheld. So the timeline includes both Arizona’s lengthy initial procurement and a federal interruption that set back its next round of stations. The records don’t establish how much the freeze delayed each of the three sites now open.

Next up are Seligman and Holbrook along I-40, where two more stations are expected to open before the end of 2026.

ADOT now says most stations will offer both NACS and CCS plugs, with at least four 150-kilowatt DC fast chargers at most locations, as required by NEVI. Its opening announcement doesn’t provide a connector-by-connector breakdown for the three operating sites.

Honest question. How do those two Tesla Supercharger locations qualify for NEVI funds? The NEVI program requires every federally funded DC fast-charging port to feature a permanently attached CCS1 connector as a primary baseline requirement. Federal guidance also permits or accommodates additional NACS connectors alongside CCS1, as long as the primary requirement is met.

ADOT is also seeking bids for 49 locations, backed by an estimated $49 million in federal funding. That round includes 31 new locations and 18 previously unawarded sites. Proposals are due January 15, 2027; construction is expected to start in 2028 and take about a year.

Juventus had chances to respond: Randal Kolo Muani had an effort ruled out for offside, while Nick Woltemade and Nico Gonzalez both went close. The match statistics showed 58 per cent possession for the hosts and nine shots apiece, but Lazio registered three efforts on target to Juventus’ two.

Digital Sun - the indie studio behind moreish roguelike Moonlighter and moody RTS Cataclismo - has delayed a planned Steam playtest for its next game, ReVamp, citing concerns over "games being reverse-engineered, cloned and published before the original is even out."

The developer was planning to let folks into its vampiric "Tower Defensevania" ReVamp near the end of this spooky month, but a new trend of gen-AI copycats cloning both old and unreleased games left the studio worried.

"So many of you signed up for ReVamp's first playtest here on Steam (thank you!), but we've decided to start with something more contained on our Discord, with the public playtest coming a bit later," the developer wrote in a recent Steam blog post.

From here, Digital Sun will grant access to ReVamp's first playtest in waves starting from the 29th of October, via Discord. ReVamp launches in full on 25th March, 2027. Here's hoping the move can successfully shoo away any would-be clones.

Digital Sun isn't the only developer having to work around the gen-AI boom. Xbox recently announced an entire department that sounds like it's focused on gen-AI game-making, as just one example.

Meanwhile, Alliance Arts outright cancelled a Steam Next Fest demo for its anime-inspired Russian Roulette game, Drunken Goddess Reflux, because of similar issues. #4001. How to Prepare Now so the End of Daylight Saving Time Is Less Jarring

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sadly, we did not manage to abolish Daylight Saving time changes during that brief, hopeful moment a few years back, so in just a few weeks we will all be going ahead with our usual ritual: Wake up early, forgetting that the time changed during the night, then get confused when the time on our phone doesn’t match the one on the microwave. Here’s how to be a little more prepared.

Daylight saving time is what we call the time in summer, and yes, it’s daylight “saving” time, singular. This time actually gets its own time zone abbreviation: EDT (Eastern Daylight Time) instead of EST (Eastern Standard Time) on the east coast, with corresponding CDT, MDT, and PDT across the country. So in November we change from daylight time to standard time.

Specifically, daylight saving time ends at 2 a.m. on Sunday, Nov. 1, 2026. That’s when the clocks “fall back.” (In the spring, they "spring forward"—get it?) If you’re changing your clocks manually, 2 a.m. becomes 1 a.m. This means that if you’re used to waking up at 7 a.m., you’ll open your eyes after sleeping the usual amount of time to find that it is now 6 a.m.

The fall time change is a great moment for me, a night owl. When I was younger I would be thrilled to stay up an extra hour and still not feel excessively tired in the morning. These days, I’m more likely to use the time change as an assist to my morning routine. I’ll be up at 6:30 (for example) instead of 7:30, and I can get in an early workout or do some journaling.

If you’re more of a morning person, this may be a bit less pleasant. You’re going to fall asleep watching TV in the evening—earlier than you usually do, anyway. Maybe you should try to stay up a bit, to get a head start on shifting your routine an hour later.

For the creatures in your house who are blissfully ignorant of the concept of standardized time, routines are about to get a little weird. Most importantly: They’ll get hungry (and everything else) an hour earlier than usual.

If you think ahead, you can start moving breakfast time later, before the time change arrives. Instead of feeding your dog at 7, start feeding him at 7:15 now, and you’ll be less likely to get a cold nose in the face at 6 a.m. (Since we have about two weeks from this writing, you can shift the time to 7:30 after a few days, and then 7:45. You get the idea.)

Same goes for kids: Expect them to wake up an hour before the clock says they should, and to get tired (and overtired) an hour earlier than they normally would. A gradual shifting of routines will help here as well, and you may want to explain the situation ahead of time to kids who are old enough to understand. And if you expect your own tummy to be grumbling on cue? Consider a gradual change in mealtimes for yourself, as well, or at least have a snack on hand in case you need it.

When daylight saving time ends, we stop “saving” that extra hour of sunlight in the evening. Your evening workout or your evening commute will be darker and colder than before. On the bright side, we’ll see light earlier in the morning, which can be a good excuse to take a morning walk you might not otherwise take. (Morning light and morning exercise both help to set your body clock and may put you in a better mood for the day to come.)

If you head outside for exercise in the evening, check out our guide to how to layer for an outdoor run, depending on the temperature. You may also want to bring a flashlight or wear a headlamp. Here's the one I use.

It may be easier to wake up after the time change, but the flip side is that it may be harder to go to bed. This is a good time to take a look at your bedtime routine, or even to create a bedtime routine if you haven’t really had one.

I have a guide to good sleep hygiene here, but one of the most important tips is to give yourself a wind-down time in the evening—30 minutes minimum—where the lights are dim and you aren’t staring at a screen. It’s a good time to take a bath, read a book, or do something else you find relaxing. Soon you’ll be locked in to your new schedule—at least until our next time change in the spring.

#4010. How I'm Using the Time Change to Kickstart My Morning Exercise Routine

I look forward to the fall time change every year, because I have plans for that extra morning hour. Turning back the clock is a perfect opportunity to kick-start a morning routine, since you can get up an hour early without it feeling like you're getting up an hour early.

#5877. Priority’s fan-favorite Sauce bike just got a big electric upgrade

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Priority Bicycles has spent the last decade proving that bicycles don’t need to be complicated to be good. Now the New York-based bicycle company is taking one of its more playful pedal bikes and giving it the electric treatment with the new Priority E-Sauce.

The E-Sauce is based on Priority’s existing Sauce, a chunky-tired, belt-driven, single-speed bicycle that was originally expected to appeal to a fairly narrow group of riders. (Fun fact: it also comes in an 8-speed internally geared hub variant, known as the Hot Sauce.)

Instead of living out its product life in obscurity as a bike between niches, Priority says the bike found a much wider audience than they originally expected thanks to its relaxed riding style, distinctive looks, and ability to handle everything from city streets to trails.

And so, naturally, now they stuck a motor on it.

Unlike many of the e-bike companies we cover, Priority didn’t spring up during the e-bike boom with a catalog of imported electric bicycles. The Manhattan-based company was founded by legit cyclists and spent years building traditional bicycles before increasingly embracing electrification. Its calling card has long been bike-shop-quality construction paired with unusually low-maintenance components, particularly Gates carbon belt drives, which the company features on every model it offers, right down to their cute little kids’ bikes.

That same formula carries over to the E-Sauce, which uses a single-speed Gates belt drive rather than a derailleur and chain. Power comes from a 750W rear hub motor, and there’s a throttle for riders who don’t feel like pedaling. The bike ships configured as Class 2 with a 20 mph (32 km/h) speed limit, though pedal assist can be unlocked for speeds of up to 28 mph (45 km/h).

Be sure to check your local regulations to see how that impacts where you can ride. In most states though, the bike will be a 100% street-legal Class 2 e-bike.

Interestingly, Priority is using both torque and cadence sensing – a move we’re starting to see more of.

The torque sensor provides more natural-feeling pedal assist up to 20 mph, while the bike can switch over to cadence-based assistance above 20 mph, meaning they don’t have to pedal like crazy to still get good motor output at higher speeds.

That gives riders the more bicycle-like response of a torque sensor at typical riding speeds without making it difficult to keep the motor working as the bike approaches its 28 mph maximum.

Remember, of course, that the bike only ships in 20 mph mode, and so this will only affect riders who do choose to open it up for more speed to reach the Class 3 limit of 28 mph.

The rest of the bike leans into the Sauce’s do-anything personality. There are wide 27.5 x 2.4-inch WTB Intersector tires and 100mm of front suspension travel, giving it considerably more capability on rough roads and light trails than your typical commuter.

Priority also seems intent on making the E-Sauce surprisingly useful as a utility bike. Standard rear rack mounts work with conventional racks, including Priority’s MIK-compatible rack for carrying panniers, cargo, or even a child seat. Up front, the company’s new frame-mounted Headway Mount will support front racks and other accessories. The bike includes built-in LED lighting, but offers clever innovations like seat-tube integration for the tail light so you don’t have to worry about a light getting whacked off at a crowded bike rack.

Priced at $2,199, the E-Sauce obviously isn’t going to win a watt-per-dollar battle against the endless sea of $1,000-ish 750W e-bikes. But that’s also missing the point. The E-Sauce is a different type of product. You’re getting a belt-driven bike designed around nearly zero maintenance, built by an actual bicycle company run by real bike people, yet still sold with the convenience and pricing advantages of a direct-to-consumer model.

Priority says that simplicity is central to the bike: a smooth and quiet Gates belt drive, punchy hydraulic disc brakes, quality components, minimal upkeep, and enough versatility to serve as anything from a trail toy to a commuter or light-duty cargo bike.

The original Sauce apparently taught Priority that a fun, simple bicycle can have a much broader audience than expected. With a 750W motor and throttle now thrown into the mix, I have a feeling the E-Sauce might cast an even wider net.

#4553. "Too big a risk": Moonlighter devs delay new game's demo for fear of genAI cloning, calling on platforms to "keep strengthening protections for original work"

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Digital Sun "concerned" by recent news about genAI rip-offs

Moonlighter and Cataclismo developers Digital Sun Games have postponed a public Steam playtest for their new game ReVamp, for fear of people using generative AI to clone their work before release.

"So many of you signed up for ReVamp's first playtest on Steam (thank you!), but we've decided to start with something more contained on our Discord, with the public playtest coming a bit later," the devs write on MechaHitler.com. "Why? We've been concerned by recent news of games being reverse-engineered, cloned and published before the original is even out. With launch still months away, a large public build of ReVamp felt like too big a risk."

They're now hoping to run a public playtest in January, with the closed Discord showing starting on October 29th. "Showing games early is how indies get seen and find the fans who keep us going, so we hope platforms keep strengthening protections for original work," the developers conclude, without going into details about the kind of protection they'd like to see.

There's been a flurry of stories recently about clones of in-progress or just-released games. It's gotten to the point that people cloning needle-in-haystack games are accusing other people who clone needle-in-haystack games of cloning their clones.

In response, some developers have announced that they're pulling playtests or demos, to lower the risk of their WIP game being fed to a chatbot. Last week, the creators of Drunken Goddess Reflux cancelled a planned demo for fear that it "could be used to quickly imitate or replicate the game's presentation and gameplay with AI or other tools, and that such works could be released before our launch."

Digital Moon cited our Drunken Goddess Reflux story when they mailed me about their decision to delay the demo, earlier today. If I were being ungenerous, I might say that they saw a chance to get some publicity for ReVamp - they're still running some kind of playtest this year, rather than battening down the marketing hatches till the game's release - but I've read a few comparable reactions from other devs over the weekend. The worry seems pretty genuine.

People have been ripping each other's work off since the dawn of capitalism, of course, and I'm wary of making a case for the kind of punitive copyright culture that sees GTA 5 modders getting their wrists slapped for recreating bits of GTA 4.

You could also argue that the flurry of genAI rip-offs is less an indictment of genAI than of a marketplace that thrives on easily appropriated viral gimmicks – the kind of game streamers love to pounce on. I don't want to categorise all games made with streamers in mind as shallow and easily replicated - streaming has facilitated specific kinds of videogame, including devious and slapstick co-op affairs such as Lethal Company and Peak. And I wouldn't class Digital Sun's ReVamp as opportunistic Tuber-bait, in any case - it's a rearrangeable modular tower defender in which you play Dracula. It seems like a substantial piece of work, and the dev's past action-RPGs and strategy games speak for themselves.
