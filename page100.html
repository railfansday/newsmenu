Mitsubishi Motors is back at the SEMA Show for the first time since 2012, but it’s not bringing a wild custom build to Las Vegas as you might expect but a production model.

This year’s SEMA Show (Nov. 3-6) marks the world debut of the 2028 Outlander Diamond Trail Edition, the most rugged variant yet of the brand’s bread-and-butter SUV. The Japanese automaker obviously wants a bigger slice of the popular off-road SUV market, and a cheap way to do that is to give the existing Outlander some off-road chops.

The off-road model gets an array of exterior, interior and hardware upgrades that make it “the most capable and adventure-focused Outlander ever offered in North America”—Mitsubishi’s words, not ours.

For starters, the Diamond Trail Edition adds a new Rock mode to the Outlander’s standard Super-All Wheel Control System (S-AWC) for more capability off the beaten track. The setting is said to use enhanced brake control to quickly suppress wheel-slip and help secure traction. Furthermore, Rock mode is said to activate faster than other S-AWC modes.

Additionally, when Rock mode is selected, the Hill Descent Control (HDC) system is automatically activated as well to help the driver control speed in rocky or mogul terrain. HDC can also be adjusted for the speed at which it holds the vehicle.

Another new addition is a screen that can be displayed in the instrument cluster showing front-wheel steering angle, individual wheel-slip and traction-control intervention. The Multi-View Camera System can also be activated and remains on up to 18.6 mph, a 6.2 mph increase from the standard model.

Seen from the outside, the 2028 Outlander Diamond Trail Edition looks significantly more rugged thanks to redesigned bumpers that improve approach and departure angles—from 18.3 to 20.5 degrees (approach) and from 22.2 to 23.1 degrees (departure). The bumpers match the aggressive over-fenders and the unique 18×7.5″ black alloy wheels shod with dedicated Nexen Roadian ATX all-terrain tires, contributing to a rugged, trail-ready look.

The Outlander Diamond Trail Edition also gets a new matte black grille with black Dynamic Shield, as well as a black finish for the headlights. Keeping with the off-road-capable theme, the SUV also gets two round projectors attached to the front bumper and raised roof rails that allow easy installation of roof racks. Finally, the Outlander Diamond Trail Edition debuts a new Trail Green Metallic color that can be offset with a white roof.

Moving to the cabin, there’s an all-new seating material finished in dark grey with khaki stitching. Unique to the Diamond Trail Edition, this material repels water and is easier to clean, which should come in handy for an adventure vehicle.

The interior also features unified matte and dark tones, as well as textured matte black, dark chrome and dark satin. The Diamond Trail Edition logo, which represents skilled athletes on a downhill course, is found on the seat backs, floor mats and the door trim.

While Mitsubishi has yet to confirm whether the all-new Montero/Pajero body-on-frame SUV is coming stateside, its Momentum 2030 long-term business plan notes that the 2028 Outlander Diamond Trail Edition will head a new generation of off-road-capable vehicles for North America.

Future models will focus on adventure and vehicle capability while building on the brand’s history of quality, dependability and reliability.

This vehicle represents the next step in our Momentum 2030 vision, combining Mitsubishi Motors' off-road heritage with the capability, technology and versatility today's customer demands. From the production model to the customized concepts on display, we're showing enthusiasts how far their adventures can go, while reinforcing our goal to helping customers truly ‘Drive their Ambition.’

Mitsubishi will showcase three Outlander Diamond Trail models at SEMA. One vehicle will be stock except some Mitsubishi Genuine Accessories and a color-matched Thule roof box, while the other two will be heavily modified.

#3511. 4 drawbacks you should know before buying an Xbox Series X

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Xbox Series X can still provide solid value, but you should understand what you're getting six years in.

This has been a pretty grim console generation for Microsoft, between hard-to-swallow Game Pass price hikes, upsetting studio closures and all those Halo Infinite "Craig the Brute" memes. You can expect an Xbox Series X to last years if you look after it properly, but should you even buy one in the first place?

Considering it's never been more expensive to pick up a Series X, the console is easily overpowered by Sony's PS5 Pro, and you can play pretty much every Xbox game of the current generation on PC, it doesn't look like a great time to buy an Xbox Series X. Let's get into the negative factors you need to consider before taking the plunge on Microsoft's console.

That really is an Xbox Series X dressed in the official Starfield console wrap Microsoft released back in 2023. And yes, I was basically using it as a glorified Christmas prop by this point (please don't block your console's vents with decorations). Suffice it to say, my Xbox Series X was no longer worth it to me, so I promptly sold it.

The market forces that have seen Xbox Series X prices skyrocket lately make it much less financially appealing than the box I bought back in 2020. So much of the reasoning behind console costs is down to global firms' desire to quench the insatiable memory and storage thirst of AI data centers.

When the Xbox Series X first shipped for $499 in November 2020, it looked like a relatively good value. Here was a small, 4K/60 fps capable box that could do ray tracing. Compared to the disastrous launch of its DRM-obsessed Xbox One predecessor that cost $500 back in 2013, the Series X looked destined to become a sales success.

Skip forward to 2026, and average Xbox Series X prices range anywhere from $725-$900 depending on storage configurations. The 1TB disc-based model generally sells for around $799, while the digital-only "Robot White" is  $750.

For those with plenty of disposable cash, the investment could still be worth it. The Xbox Series X is the second most powerful console on the market, and a terrific gaming machine. Yet in the current financial climate, a 60 percent price increase makes it a much harder sell, especially for aging hardware.

There was a time when Xbox Game Pass represented tremendous value. When it launched in June 2017, it cost $9.99 per month and granted access to a library of over 100 Xbox One games, plus a selection of backward-compatible Xbox 360 titles. The higher-tier Xbox Game Pass Ultimate launched in June 2019, costing $14.99/month. From here, the service started to get less appealing.

After the subscription gradually crept up to a monthly fee of $19.99 by July 2024, Microsoft made the decision to set the price of Game Pass Ultimate to $29.99 starting in October 2025. Following six months at this wild cost, widespread fan backlash forced Microsoft to reduce the monthly Ultimate sub to $22.99, which is its current price.

Other Game Pass tiers don't feel as value-friendly as the original service from almost 10 years ago. The $9.99/month Game Pass Essential is a stripped-down service that offers around 50 games. There's also $14.99 Game Pass Premium, which gives you access to over 200 titles playable across Xbox Series X|S, PCs and cloud devices. However, neither of these include day-one releases; you need Ultimate to play titles like the upcoming Fable reboot on launch day.

If you play enough, you can still get your money's worth from Game Pass. I'm not going to pay $70 for Forza Horizon 6 when I can experience it with a subscription and jump to something else when I'm not feeling it anymore.

For hardcore gamers, there have always been compelling reasons to own a capable Windows gaming PC over an Xbox Series X. That's because a powerful rig is currently the best way to experience Xbox Series X games.

Forget overpaying for a six-year-old console. Providing you own a decent gaming laptop or PC, you can play over 300 titles with PC Game Pass for just $13.99 per month. This includes day-one Microsoft exclusives like Gears of War: E-Day. The PC and Xbox Game Pass libraries don't match exactly, but you'll almost certainly find something you're interested in playing. Very few games are truly console-exclusive — and for anything not on Xbox, Steam sales are common.

The PS5 Pro is significantly more powerful than the Xbox Series X on the GPU front. Microsoft's elite box has 12.15 TFLOPS of GPU power compared to PS5 Pro's 16.7 TFLOPS, while they both support ray tracing thanks to their custom AMD RDNA APUs. As for processors, both use AMD Zen 2 chips with 8 cores and 16 threads, with the Pro boasting a slighter higher 3.85 GHz clock speed compared to Series X's 3.8GHz.

So while the PS5 Pro has a clear graphical rendering advantage, allowing the machine to run many games at higher resolutions than the Xbox Series X, there's not much difference between their processors. That means in open-world games that are often CPU-intensive, you don't typically see big FPS bumps from the Pro console.

Speaking of which, GTA VI is causing a PS5 Pro shortage. Folks are paying exorbitant sums on the secondhand market to get the best experience in Rockstar's highly-anticipated title. Used PS5 Pro systems are listed for nearly $1,400 at the time of writing (with the current MSRP being $899).

Will GTA VI look better on PS5 Pro compared to Xbox Series X? Almost certainly, thanks to Sony's PSSR 2.0 (PlayStation Spectral Super Resolution), which looks so good in Resident Evil Village. But when it comes to performance, it looks like both consoles will be stuck at 30 fps.

#5048. OCC Lets Crypto Firms Keep Paying Stablecoin Rewards

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The OCC’s new GENIUS Act rules let exchanges like Coinbase and Robinhood keep paying interest-like stablecoin rewards, despite warnings from banks about deposit flight.

Crypto companies can keep paying interest-like rewards to customers, according to federal bank regulators. The ruling arrives despite warnings from banks that such programs could pull trillions of dollars out of the banking system.

The decision is part of the rollout of the GENIUS Act, which was signed into law last year to set rules for stablecoin issuers. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) published the new rules on Wednesday.

Under the OCC’s framework, stablecoin issuers like Circle remain barred from paying interest to people who hold their coins. Exchanges that trade and hold those stablecoins, including Coinbase and Robinhood, can pass earnings from the coins on to customers as interest-like rewards. Regulators said exchanges can do so on a case-by-case basis. Banks had wanted the practice banned outright.

Stablecoins are crypto tokens designed to hold a steady value, typically $1 per coin. They are backed by bank deposits and U.S. Treasuries, which earn interest, and that interest sits at the center of the fight between banks and crypto firms. The industry wants to turn the income from those reserves into a yield for customers.

Banking groups have pushed for tighter limits because crypto firms often offer higher yields than traditional savings accounts. Coinbase, for example, offers up to 3.5% on USDC balances. The FDIC’s latest figures put the average savings account yield at 0.38%.

If stablecoins and exchanges outcompete banks on yield, customers could shift their savings into crypto, a trend known as deposit flight. The banking industry argues this would reduce banks’ ability to lend and, in a worst-case scenario, cause some to fail. The same issue sparked intense debate when lawmakers drafted the CLARITY Act.

The American Bankers Association said the decision threatens the funds banks use to make loans that drive the U.S. economy. It argued the rule preserves the status quo for crypto platforms despite Congress’s clear intent to protect local lending.

Consumer advocates have joined banks on this issue, which is unusual. They worry that customers will see crypto rewards as equivalent to bank account interest, even though the crypto world has no equivalent of FDIC deposit insurance. Phillip Basil of Better Markets said the OCC has handed stablecoin issuers a free pass to do the work of banks without the protections that keep the banking system safe.

#2284. Swiss manager identified as strong candidate in Burnley’s new boss search

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Burnley are on the lookout for a new boss after parting ways with Nicky Hayen on Saturday. The Clarets’ interest in Rob Edwards and Carlos Corberan has been rebuffed, and they are turning their attention elsewhere.

According to The Athletic, Swiss coach Fabio Celestini, who was last in charge of CSKA Moscow, has been identified as a strong contender. It is said that the 50-year-old and his staff have already travelled to England, although the terms of his potential appointment are yet to be agreed.

Celestini was last in management in May, when he departed CSKA. He is an experienced manager, having previously worked in his native Switzerland at FC Basel, FC Luzern and more.

Burnley face Portsmouth this Saturday, with fixtures against QPR and Cardiff City following. The club spent a long time on their appointment of Hayen in the summer and will be keen to avoid repeating that in their search for a new head coach.

Celestini’s CV is a mixed one. He won the Russian Super Cup with CSKA Moscow, as well as the Swiss Super League and Super Cup with FC Basel. He took the latter over when they were bottom of the league, later leading them to the domestic double in 2024/25.

He had tougher spells at FC Sion (six games, 0.33 PPG) and FC Lugano (46 games, 1.15 PPG).

Celestini often operates with a 4-2-2-1 formation, sometimes switching to a 3-4-3. He likes his team to get the ball in attacking areas as quickly as possible.

In the 2024/25 campaign with FC Basel, Celestini’s side averaged 158.67 forward passes per game. They also topped the goalscoring charts, which is indicative of Celestini’s direct approach.

At the time of writing, Celestini has managed 377 matches in his career. He has accumulated a 39% win rate alongside a handful of domestic trophies in Switzerland and Russia.

Celestini has the potential to be an exciting appointment for Burnley. His fast, attacking approach may allow the likes of Marcus Edwards and Largie Ramazani to flourish.

However, it is the defence where the Clarets have found most of their struggles this term, with errors and lapses in concentration leading to their 25 goals conceded in 12 league matches. Such an attacking approach may not address these glaring problems.

Furthermore, Celestini’s lack of English experience may concern some fans given Hayen’s struggles.

With other names in the running, Burnley will need to make a decision quickly to bounce back and find form. It remains to be seen how the interest develops, but Celestini would be a gamble by the Clarets at a crucial time.

#5930. American Airlines Keeps Selling Upgrades Until Boarding, So Why Keep Chasing Elite Status?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Major U.S. carriers are continuing to sell discounted premium cabin upgrades until shortly before boarding while their elite members wait for those same seats. Today, I want to take a closer look at the practice, with particular focus on American Airlines. I understand why AA or any other carrier wants the money, but this is a perfect example of why I now buy the flight I want rather than organizing my travel around earning airline elite status and foolishly hoping for the illusory upgrade benefits that accompany it.

View From The Wing highlights an example of American continuing to offer paid upgrades close to boarding, even with high-ranking elite members waiting for those seats. With just 28 minutes before boarding, AA was still selling upgrades from Philadelphia to Los Angeles for $242, a pretty good deal for six-hour flight.

That sparked a discussion on reddit with many other stories. One Executive Platinum member bought an upgrade on a New York LaGuardia–Dallas flight despite being third on the upgrade list, moving ahead of the two passengers above him. Another traveler flying New York–Delhi paid $640 to move from economy to business class shortly before boarding. He had been waiting for a systemwide upgrade for two months and was third on the list with one seat remaining.

Each example illustrate the same practical problem: a passenger can spend months waiting for an upgrade earned through loyalty, only to see the seat sold shortly before departure.

These are individual passenger accounts, not an announcement of a new American policy. But American’s published upgrade rules do not promise anything and as the carrier seeks to return to profitability, it is hawking upgrades aggressively and in my experience at much better rates than Alaska Airlines, United Airlines, or Delta Air Lines.

I have no fundamental objection to American selling an otherwise empty first class seat. A passenger waiting for a complimentary upgrade has only a conditional benefit. Status does not confer ownership of an unsold seat, and American has never promised that every Executive Platinum member will sit up front.

But that does not settle whether pursuing the status is worthwhile.

Airlines encourage us to concentrate our flying and credit card spending with them. Choose our flight, even if another airline has a better schedule. Use our card. Reach the next threshold. Earn the certificates. Enjoy the recognition. I call it a hamster wheel.

Then, when it comes time to deliver one of the primary benefits associated with status, the airline still tries to find someone willing to pay cash for the seat and not just until the upgrade windows open, but until boarding begins!

That is a perfectly understandable commercial decision. At the same time, it also gives customers a perfectly understandable reason to reconsider their own spending.

If an occasional traveler can buy an attractive upgrade without spending the year chasing status, perhaps that traveler has figured out the better approach.

I would certainly consider a reasonably priced upgrade myself. In fact, I often do that instead of buying first outright because the coach + instant paid upgrade cost saves me money. I’m not so idealistic that I would decline paying for an upgrade out of solidarity with the people above me on the list. My concern here is with the calculation that persuaded those passengers to spend so much pursuing their place on that list in the first place.

Recently I was chatting with a friend who flew from Des Moines to Denver to Dallas on United rather than fly nonstop from Des Moines to Dallas on United. He’s on the hamster wheel and I knows it, but I realize the chase of status can be a powerful aphrodisiac itself. I’m here, though, to tell you that it’s not worthwhile.

For the last two years, I have been a free agent. I still enjoy flying United and it receives plenty of my business. But I have flown other airlines much more this year because directing all my travel toward another year of Premier 1K simply did not make sense.

If I want first class, I buy first class. Sometimes that means purchasing it outright. Often an upgrade offer makes sense, my most frequent route to the forward cabin. Ironically, I’ve received more complimentary upgrades as a lowly Advantage Gold (lowest tier) on American than I do on United as Premier Gold. In an any case, I can compare a concrete price with a concrete benefit rather than pay more throughout the year and hope the benefit eventually appears.

Of course, status still has value. Extra-legroom seating, baggage allowances, priority assistance, and help during irregular operations can be useful, even as an AA Gold. Alliance benefits can be valuable when traveling internationally. For someone whose normal travel earns status without changing purchasing decisions, there may be little downside to chasing it.

But someone taking an inconvenient connection, paying a higher fare, or diverting substantial credit card spending needs to account for those costs. The possibility of an upgrade should be valued according to how often it actually happens, not how on a theoretical basis.

Systemwide upgrades require the same scrutiny. A certificate can be extremely valuable when it clears. Spending more to earn one that remains unused or leaves you buying the upgrade anyway is a much less compelling proposition.

I would rather keep the money and choose the best flight. Trust me, it’s liberating.
