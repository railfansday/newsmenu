#5957. InKind Dining App: How It Works, Why I Love It, And My Experience

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

InKind is a dining rewards program that lets you pay participating restaurant bills through the app and earn a minimum of 20% back in the form of InKind Cash. You can also purchase InKind Cash with promotional bonuses and discounts, making it possible to save considerably more than 20%.

InKind currently has thousands of participating restaurants, bars, and cafes, across the United States, and I find them to largely be very high quality. If you’re new to InKind, you can even get $25 off your first meal of $50+ by using a referral link.

In this post, I’d like to take an in-depth look at InKind, and all of the ways that you can save with the app. For some background, I started using InKind over two years ago, and since then, I’ve consistently used this at least once or twice per week for dining out, and have had nothing but great experiences. I can’t believe it took me so long to download this app, so I’d like to share my experience, for anyone who may not be familiar.

I find this to be significantly more rewarding than your typical airline or hotel dining program. It’s also super straightforward to use, and has virtually no restrictions in terms of when you can dine, what you can order, etc. I love the simplicity of it.

On the most basic level, InKind is a free app that offers a 20% discount (or more) at over 8,000 restaurants around the United States. The participating restaurants are primarily in major cities, from Chicago, to Los Angeles, to Miami, to New York, to San Francisco. So if you live in a smaller market, unfortunately this app probably isn’t for you, unless you’re traveling.

What impresses me about InKind is that the restaurants on the list are actually largely high quality and popular, unlike with many other dining rewards programs, which basically try to incentivize you to eat at crappy restaurants.

For example, I live in Miami, and several of the restaurants participating on InKind are ones I’d frequent anyway, from Chug’s Diner, to Ghee, to KYU, to Michael’s Genuine, to Otto & Pepe, to Phuc Yea, just to name some. Even some of the more popular “clubby” restaurants are on the list, like Sparrow Italia.

I also find it interesting that InKind’s business model is different than that of most other dining rewards programs. With most other dining rewards programs, the dining network simply takes a percentage of the bill in exchange for offering guests rewards for using a particular credit card.

InKind, meanwhile, seems to work more closely with restaurants, and has a different business relationship, whereby it provides financing to restaurants. The idea is that ordinarily when private equity firms invest in restaurants, they take a huge stake. Instead, InKind provides capital to restaurants, in exchange for dining credits.

It’s a very interesting business model. Obviously I can’t speak to it from the perspective of a restaurant owner, but I appreciate the concept that InKind is going for. The company isn’t simply skimming a percentage off each transaction under the guise of sending new diners to restaurants, but it’s a closer partnership than that (which is also why the restaurant network is smaller).

There are so many ways that the InKind app can save you money for dining at a participating restaurant. At a minimum, you should look at it as receiving 20% off your bill, though potentially you can get a much better return than that. Let me go through each of the ways you can save.

InKind has a refer a friend program. When you’re referred by an existing user, you’ll get $25 off a bill of $50 or more, to use at a participating restaurant. Similarly, the friend who refers you can get a credit as well, which is typically $25-50.

You can find my refer a friend link here, and others are welcome to leave their link in the comments section as well.

If you’re going to pay with your card linked to your InKind account, then you can always receive 20% off your meal, with no maximum savings. That 20% back comes in the form of credits you can use toward future dining at InKind restaurants.

Note that you don’t receive the 20% back on tips, fees, or portions of the bill covered by discounts or paid with InKind Cash. This credit expires two months after the calendar month in which it’s earned. So if you earn credit on October 15, it would be valid through December 31. The oldest accrued credit is always used first for redemptions.

The InKind dining app also lets you purchase InKind Cash at a discount. The exact offers vary over time, but in my account I’ve seen the opportunity to buy a $312 InKind dining credit for $250, or an $8,700 dining credit for $6,000. As you can tell, this offers anywhere from a 25% to 45% bonus.

If you go this route, then you won’t earn the 20% back, but instead you potentially get a larger discount. Of course you should only use this if you frequent restaurants that are part of the InKind network, so I don’t recommend taking advantage of this if you’re just trying out the app.

For example, I decided to buy the $312 credit for $250, since I know I’ll spend that at participating restaurants.

Often there are additional promotions where you can buy credits with an even higher percentage bonus or discount, so keep an eye out for that. The base credit purchased never expires, while the bonus credit purchased this way expires after three years.

To take advantage of this, you just have to add your eligible card to your InKind profile, and then pay for a meal with the card, through the InKind app. You’ll then automatically be issued the credit. This is a fantastic perk on a card that’s already arguably a very good value, especially for those who value American Admirals Club access.

Beyond the above, InKind often has other promotions you can take advantage of, so it’s worth keeping an eye out for those. For example, sometimes there are promotions with Amex Offers and SimplyMiles, which can be stacked with the above.

Furthermore, InKind often emails members with targeted and other limited time promotions. For example, I’ve had offers for an extra 10% back if I make two InKind transactions at the same restaurant within a month, or targeted promotions for larger savings at specific restaurants.

If you go directly to the website of a participating InKind restaurant, you may also see a pop-up offering savings of a certain amount for dining at that restaurant. The catch is that this can’t be combined with the refer a friend offer, so while it can at times be lucrative, it doesn’t offer the same flexibility.

All of that sounds great, but what’s it like using the InKind dining app at when you’re actually at the restaurant? Is there confusion from the servers? Do they roll their eyes? Well, that’s what delights me the most about the experience.

When using InKind to pay, you don’t need to make a reservation in any particular way, there are no restrictions on what days or times you can dine, and you don’t even need to let the server know when you’re seated that you’ll be paying with InKind.

There’s not even any confusion, since you actually pay the bill on your end, without even interacting with the server. That’s because all InKind restaurants use the Toast platform for payments, so it’s all connected pretty nicely on the backend.

For example, one of my most recent InKind meals was at Ghee Indian Kitchen. At the conclusion of the meal, I was presented with a check, so I pulled up Ghee Indian Kitchen on the InKind app…

The app asked to enter the check number, which was clearly visible on the check.

I was then asked how much I wanted to tip. Note that the gratuity will always be charged to your card on file, and will not be taken from any balance you may have.

I had two options for how to pay. I could either use my existing InKind balance (which I acquired with a 35% bonus), or I could pay with my linked credit card, and receive 20% back, in the form of InKind credit.

I completed that all on my phone, and a minute later our server showed up, and said we were all set, without me even having to mention that I paid through InKind. It honestly couldn’t have been more seamless, and that matches every experience I’ve had with InKind.

Since I had purchased the credit for 35% off, I ended up saving around $43 on the $123.58 total. Alternatively, if I had gone for the 20% back, I would’ve received around $25 in credit to use toward a future dining experience.

#4649. The Portaluppi Bathroom is the New “It” Loo

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The dominance of the gray-and-white “spa bathroom” may be waning. You know the ones—spare, mostly white, flooded with light, and sheathed in acres of Calacatta Viola marble. The new look involves dramatically veined stone in dark green or dusky red, sculptural Art Deco–style fixtures, and rich woodwork. Think: the bathrooms of AD cover stars like interior designer Athena Calderone and supermodel Paloma Elsesser. The reference point is 1930s Milan, where bold material combinations and precise geometry turned these utilitarian spaces into showpieces.

Few designers are as identified with this look as Piero Portaluppi (1888–1967), the Rationalist architect behind the iconic Villa Necchi Campiglio and Casa Corbellini-Wassermann. While his dramatic entry halls and sweeping stairs have inspired designers for decades, it’s the rooms with plumbing that are currently having a moment.

The Inspiration: Piero Portaluppi's iconic bathroom at Villa Necchi Campiglio, built between 1932 and 1935

At Villa Necchi, mirrored walls multiply the swirling patterns of Arabescato Orobico marble, while at nearby Casa Corbellini-Wassermann, Portaluppi set vibrant green Verde Challant marble against graphic black stonework. “He frequently used contrasting materials to frame hallways, thresholds, and architectural elements, allowing these moments of juxtaposition to draw the eye and bring a sense of ceremony to the space,” explains Calderone, who visited Villa Necchi with architect Danielle Siggerud while reimagining her own Tribeca apartment.

Gregory Rockwell’s design for model Paloma Elsesser’s bathroom

AD100 designer Gregory Rockwell says he is drawn to Portaluppi's unconventional palette. “What I love about Portaluppi is how controlled everything is architecturally, but then he’ll do something extravagant with the marble or an unexpected color,” the designer says. For the primary bathroom of Elsesser’s Brooklyn town house, Rockwell and colleague Hester Hodde layered burgundy Rosso Francia marble, walnut-and-mirror paneling, and pistachio-green walls. “It’s a slightly strange combination when you think about it, but I love the colors together,” he says.

Sherle Wagner Pagoda Stone Lever Basin set

For Calderone, the magic is in the stonework. “There was such ingenuity in the way Portaluppi clad entire bathrooms in stone, often using smaller, individually composed pieces,” she says. For her primary bathroom, she edged sweeping expanses of pale stone with intricate inlaid tile borders and added Art Deco–inflected fixtures and geometric mirrors. Looking back at Portaluppi’s examples, she observes: “It feels almost at odds with how we approach stone today, where expansive, perfectly bookmatched slabs have become the prevailing ideal. He embraced the irregularity and variation in the veining.” That appreciation for the individuality of natural stone is also on display in her own powder room, which features a pedestal sink in deeply veined Verde Alpi marble.

Athena Calderone’s powder room includes a pedestal sink in deeply veined green marble designed by Calderone in collaboration with ABC Stone, sconces by Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, and a 1940s Italian mirror.

This powder room designed by Giampiero Tagliaferri is enveloped in rosewood and features a sink of Verde Alpi marble, Pia Guidetti Crippa sconce and a Maurizio Donzelli painting.

Another sink in the same deep-green stone can be found in the powder room of a Milan pied-à-terre Giampiero Tagliaferri recently designed for a young art collector. “The custom pedestal sink takes its bold, geometric form from Rationalist architecture, but we kept the lines clean and the layout modern,” Tagliaferri says. As the designer explains, revisiting 1930s Milan isn’t about exactly replicating a Portaluppi bathroom. “Reinterpretation is about extracting a period’s core DNA, not literal replication.”

Another view of the malachite-wrapped San Francisco bathroom designed by Summer Thornton,

Crate & Barrel Verde Green Marble Tissue Holder

That thinking extends beyond bespoke interiors. New bath collections are borrowing from the Portaluppi bathroom vocabulary, offering ways to incorporate patterned stone, geometric forms, and saturated color without a full-scale renovation. Artistic Tile’s Calacatta Gold marble mosaic brings the intricacy of Calderone’s inlaid borders to walls and floors, while Kelly Wearstler’s oxblood-hued Treillage tile for Ann Sacks puts a dimensional spin on the burgundy tones of Elsesser’s bathroom. For Clé Tile, Reath Design created marbleized patterns that evoke the movement of natural stone. The influence extends to smaller details: Charles Edwards’s alabaster dish light has an Art Deco–inspired silhouette, while Crate & Barrel’s Verde Green marble soap pump and tissue holder bring the distinctive color and veining of those Verde Alpi sinks down to countertop scale.

Charles Edwards Bridgford Alabaster light

Whether it’s an entire room wrapped in marble or a single well-chosen detail, the impulse is much the same. “I’ve never really understood why bathrooms should be any less interesting than the rest of the house,” Rockwell says. “They might as well be beautiful and exciting.”

It’s also a place for dust to seep in and break the phone, as with Samsung’s original Galaxy Fold in 2019, forcing the company to pause sales and fix the issue. Some devices handle it better than others, and as the product category has grown, those issues have largely diminished. But fewer problems don’t mean easier fixes.

On Sunday, repair company iFixit released its teardown of the iPhone Duo, including a video detailing the specific parts inside and what to look out for if people need to fix their $1,999 phone one day—maybe sooner than they think.

The culprit behind the screen breaking hasn’t been officially verified yet, but iFixit has a theory. Along the hinge, a display cable passes across the center of the hinge. The tricky part, according to iFixit, is the inner display. A thin matte nano-texture finish sits on top of the OLED screen, and if you’re not careful, you could easily peel the layer off. (Apple strictly tells users not to remove this finish on a support page.)

The iPhone Duo may not be perfectly repairable—folding phones might never get there. But when a device starts having problems like cracked screens, it’ll be nice to know how to fix the thing.
