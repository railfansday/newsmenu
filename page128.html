At a star-studded ceremony at the London Palladium, Kane beat competition from Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe after a scintillating season for club and country.

The Bayern Munich and England forward scored 73 goals in all competitions last season and has started the current campaign in blistering form with 16 goals and nine assists.

#3632. The Mythical Man-Month in the Age of AI

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last weekend I watched four people desperately trying to get a laptop to connect to a screen. I was happily observing their efforts, with zero intent to join in.

But someone nudged me: can't you have a look at it?

My reply: "Haha sorry no! My experience is that adding more people will not solve this problem any quicker".

I was of course -- indirectly -- referring to Brooks's Law:

"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."

It was formulated by computer scientist Frederick P. Brooks Jr. in his 1975 book The Mythical Man-Month, based on his experience managing the IBM OS/360 project.

However I got to thinking. Does this still hold up in the age of AI agents and LLMs?

At first glance, you’d think no. The traditional bottlenecks -- onboarding time, endless stand-ups, and office politics -- disappear overnight. An AI agent is up to speed in milliseconds and doesn't demand a coffee break.

But when you look closer, and the law hasn't vanished. It just mutated.

The famous "Baby Problem" remains: nine agents still can’t deliver a baby in one month. Sequential tasks are still sequential.

Overhead hasn't disappeared; it shifted: instead of managing people, you are now managing agents. Someone still needs to define "done", review the code, catch hallucinations, and stitch the pieces together.

Throw nine autonomous agents at a single codebase without crystal-clear orchestration, and they will happily rewrite each other's work in a high-speed loop.

Maybe the 2026 update to Brooks’s Law should be:

"Adding agents to a project with unclear requirements gets you the wrong answer faster."

#225. 'I would do it again' - Kaepernick reflects on price of protest, 10 years on

'I would do it again' - Kaepernick reflects on price of protest, 10 years on

"I knew the protest could potentially cost me my career...I would absolutely do it again."

Ten years on from making the gesture that catapulted him to iconic status, but which he believes led to his exile from the sport he loved, Colin Kaepernick insists he has no regrets.

It was September 2016 when NFL star Kaepernick knelt during the US national anthem before a match as a peaceful protest against racial injustice and police brutality.

It proved both inspirational and divisive.

In his first interview with BBC Sport, Kaepernick reflects on his journey; his pride at seeing England footballers take the knee, athlete activism, the relationship between sport and politics, and the price he paid for being a figurehead for the civil rights movement.

The former quarterback says he decided to protest despite suspecting it could risk his career.

"History shows usâ¦that is a reality for athletes", he says.

"We also see this in broader movements, going well beyond costing someone their career, and costing people their lives. We see Malcolm X assassinated, we see MLK [Martin Luther King] assassinated, so the consequences are real."

Having initially sat to raise awareness of the plight of black people in the US, Kaepernick first knelt during the Star-Spangled Banner before his San Francisco 49ers team played a pre-season game. It was an act he would repeat before 11 regular-season contests that year.

At the time, he said he could not stand up for - and show pride in - a flag "for a country that oppresses black people and people of colour", adding: "This is bigger than football and it would be selfish on my part to look the other way."

His stance against racial inequality was seen as heroic by many, inspiring other NFL players to follow suit. But there was also condemnation from those who said it was disrespectful to the military.

Those dissenting voices included Donald Trump - then a presidential candidate.

Trump described the act as "a terrible thing" and unpatriotic. He even suggested Kaepernick should find another country to live in, adding players who refused to stand for the national anthem should be fired.

The NFL said it would fine teams whose players did not stand.

Kaepernick opted out of his contract with the 49ers in March 2017, though the club's management later said he would have been released regardless.

Despite leading the 49ers to the 2013 Super Bowl, and throwing 16 touchdowns in his final season, one of the most talented quarterbacks in the sport never found a new team.

Speaking from New York, Kaepernick says he anticipated the reaction to taking the knee. "What I couldn't have predicted was the people who would show up for me in significant ways, and also the people who worked against me and against the movement," he adds.

Kaepernick eventually filed a grievance against NFL team owners, who he believed had conspired not to hire him because of his protests, reaching a financial settlement in 2019.

That year, the NFL arranged a private workout for Kaepernick, but he chose not to attend, instead holding his own session elsewhere to allow media to be present. Hopes of a comeback never materialised.

The NFL later admitted it was "wrong for not listening to players earlier", and encouraged "all to speak out and peacefully protest".

Following the murder of George Floyd while in police custody in Minneapolis in 2020, taking the knee became a feature of Black Lives Matter protests, and widespread across sport.

England's men's and women's football teams were among those to follow suit.

"That was a surprising moment for me," says Kaepernick.

"I did think about the protest going beyond the US and how that may show up in other countries. To be able to see the national teams going out and protesting, it was humbling. I was happy that they were doing it.

"I also know there's still a lot of work to be done within racism in the UK and more broadly. And this is a global challenge that we are collectively fighting against.

"I was very excited to be able to see players actively calling that out, and willing to begin taking action towards addressing it."

Despite keeping a relatively low profile for most of the past decade, Kaepernick remains a standard-bearer for the civil rights movement.

But while surveys suggest the vast majority of professional athletes intend to use their social media channels to raise awareness about racial injustice, external, he accepts the momentum behind athlete activism seems to have slowed in recent years.

It was notable that several players across five clubs chose to remain standing when Premier League players took the knee as part of October's No Room for Racism campaign.

Anti-discrimination campaign group Kick It Out told BBC Sport the gesture "still has value in raising awareness of the structural inequalities that exist in football and society", but added it could "understand the reasons players might become frustrated with the pace of change".

Reports of discrimination received by the charity more than doubled in the past five years, reaching record levels last season.

"I think we're in a moment that demands protests more than ever," says Kaepernick.

"Why I think protests are not as loud right now [is] because of the concerted efforts that have been taken by organisations like the NFL, like the Trump administration, and corporate entities following along and capitulating in that process.

"While those things are happening, I think the reality of the state of the US, and more broadly the world, demands that we show up in a more significant way."

Kaepernick is all too familiar with how polarising it can be to take a political stand in sport.

Opponents of taking the knee have claimed it is a political and divisive statement, and that rather than demonstrating backing for the Black Lives Matter movement, it has shown support for the organisation of the same name.

In 2018, critics encouraged people to destroy Nike goods after the sportswear giant used Kaepernick as the face of an advertising campaign featuring the caption 'Believe in something. Even if it means sacrificing everything.'

So what does he say to those who argue that sport and politics should be kept apart?

"They ring hollow and feel very hypocritical, because politics is inherently part of sports," he says.

"We see within [American] football, we see taxpayers funding NFL stadiums. We see our NFL owners going out and supporting political candidates... so to try to position this as if sports aren't political, in many ways feels just laughable to me, and disconnected.

"Now, where the question comes in for people is: 'We don't want the athletes to be political. Everything around them can be political, but the athletes themselves, we don't want them to have a voice in the politics, because that interrupts our entertainment and our ability to disconnect.' For me, that's where society has a challenge with it."

Having encountered significant opposition from high-profile athletes during his first term, Trump seems to have encountered less challenge from the world of sport during his second, notably becoming the first sitting US president to attend the Super Bowl.

He has also appeared at golf's Ryder Cup, and the finals of football's Club World Cup and World Cup, though he was also booed when attending the NBA Finals in New York in June.

"It ties into the conversation of 'are sports political?' when he is going out and actively trying to use them to gain favour and popularity", says Kaepernick, adding that Fifa's controversial decision to award the US President its inaugural 'Peace Prize' last year was "regrettable".

"The part that I think is great to see is when he's showing up to these events and getting booed by the crowd, which speaks to the political environment that he's created, that the majority of the country does not agree withâ¦when it comes to sports, people need to continue to respond accordingly."

Now 38, Kaepernick has published a memoir titled The Perilous Fight, detailing his childhood in California having been adopted by a white family, encountering racism, and his education and evolution. "People saw the moment. But they didn't see the years that made it possible", he says. "The questions about who I was; the injustices I could no longer ignore; the voices of those who came before me that I carried into that stadium...When I took a knee, it wasn't a sudden act. It was the result of years of becoming."

So how did he find the process of reliving that journey?

"Are there moments that are challenging going back to? Yes. But I hope that insight helps other people navigate situations...that helps them move forward or helps them to go accomplish things."

Kaepernick also supports community organisations via his Know Your Rights education campaign, is a philanthropist, and has written a number of children's books.

Even now, he maintains the fitness regime he had during his time at the 49ers, just in case an unlikely opportunity to resume his NFL career arises.

Kaepernick may not have thrown a ball in anger for almost a decade, but he is widely regarded as one of the most significant athletes in modern history.

The way he used his platform to promote change has been compared to legendary boxer Muhammad Ali's support of the anti-war movement, and US athletes Tommie Smith and John Carlos giving the Black Power Salute at the 1968 Olympics.

"To even be in the same conversation with the likes of [them], is extremely humbling," he says. "But more importantly, I just try to stay focused on the work.

"What can we do to help people? And how do we collectively move forward as a society? And, ultimately, how we build a society that works for everybody. That feels like a very simple principle that has been lost.

"I know in the US specifically, we have a lot of work to do there. I hope we begin taking the steps to get back to a place where we are actually addressing people's needs. And create a society that is equitable for everyone."

Ripple has made a massive regulatory breakthrough in the US market, gaining the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's (OCC) final approval to build its own federally supervised national trust bank.

According to Ripple CEO Brad Garlinghouse's update on X, this development is a major one for the company and its institutional goals, especially leading up to their highly anticipated Swell conference in NYC.

The approved initiative enables Ripple National Trust Bank (RNTB) to handle reserves for its RLUSD stablecoin, act as a collateral trustee for stablecoin holders, and provide digital asset custody services to institutional customers.

"We've always said crypto and banks work better together," said Garlinghouse. "The latest approval shows how to integrate digital assets into the existing financial system.

The revolution isn't outside the system. It's from within the system," the Ripple chief added.

The latest finalized approval comes around 10 months after the conditional approval from the OCC in December 2024.

The national trust bank will be federally supervised, which represents a major boost to Ripple's regulatory and financial infrastructure.

Ripple won't be a traditional commercial bank that accepts regular deposits and makes loans with this charter.

It's important to note that RLUSD will still be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company, which is Ripple's subsidiary regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services.

On the other hand, the federally supervised entity's main focus is managing reserves and providing fiduciary custody services.

To date, Ripple said the market cap of RLUSD has grown to around $2.5 billion since its launch in December 2024.

RLUSD is increasingly used in Ripple's institutional payments and trading environment.

This is an interesting development, especially ahead of Ripple's upcoming Swell 2026 on October 27–29 in New York City.

The event will be attended by industry leaders from traditional finance, the crypto industry, and the entire XRP ecosystem.

Despite the impressive Ripple regulatory breakthrough, XRP did not record a big rally following this latest update.

As of early October 27 trading, XRP's price was hovering between $1.30 and $1.31, with minor movements in the last 24 hours.

As explained, this development is related to Ripple's stablecoin service and institutional custody.

It is not directly related to a sudden new XRP demand.

#204. Donald Trump's biggest economic problem looks strangely familiar

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

US President Donald Trump and his predecessor Joe Biden

The White House is confronting a familiar dilemma. The economic numbers aren't necessarily bad - but the public mood is.

It's a disconnect that haunted Joe Biden in 2024, when solid headline data failed to convince many voters that the economy was working for them.

Now, Donald Trump faces a version of the same challenge.

During the 2024 campaign, Trump promised voters swift relief from rising prices.

"When I win, I will immediately bring prices down," he said.

Delivering on that promise has proved harder than expected.

Economic growth is holding up, unemployment remains low and the stock market is near record highs.

Yet the final inflation report ahead of the midterm elections confirms what voters already know: prices remain high.

The causes are different. Biden was grappling with the aftermath of the Covid pandemic. Trump faces higher energy prices linked to the US-Israel conflict with Iran and the fallout from some of his own trade and immigration policies.

The political reality, however, is much the same. Voters are not judging the economy by GDP figures or record highs on the stock market. They are judging it by what they pay at the supermarket, petrol prices and whether they can keep up with the bills.

US economic growth of 2.2% would be respectable by the standards of many wealthy economies. Germany, for example, has struggled to generate comparable momentum. And a stable US unemployment rate of 4.2% hardly suggests an economy in freefall.

Trump argues that he inherited an economic mess from Biden, and points to strong growth, investment and stock market performance as evidence that his policies are working.

Following the most recent inflation report, White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said the administration's "aggressive, pro-growth economic agenda" had blunted "Biden's inflation crisis".

Larry Kudlow, a Trump ally and economic commentator, has described the current expansion as "the greatest story never told", external, arguing that "nobody is getting fired".

US inflation rises more than expected - here's what is happening with prices

But voters appear to be reading a different story - one in which Trump won the presidency promising to tackle the cost of living, only for prices to keep rising.

Part of the problem is that wage increases have not kept pace with prices. Average hourly earnings have risen 3% over the last year, while consumer prices have increased 3.7%.

That helps explain why polls continue to show voters giving Trump poor marks for his handling of the economy.

For Democrats, the dynamic feels familiar.

Celinda Lake, a veteran Democratic pollster, says the problem begins with the way economists and voters use the same language to mean different things.

"When economists say inflation comes down, they mean the rate of increase has come down," she says.

"When voters say inflation has come down, they mean the price has returned to what it was. Well, of course, it never returns to what it was."

Joe Biden campaigned in 2024 on the economy but voters saw things differently

The Biden administration spent much of 2024 highlighting low unemployment, solid growth and falling inflation. However, many voters remained focused on the higher prices they faced every day.

Lake, who advised Biden's 2020 campaign but was not involved in the 2024 race, says voters judge the economy through personal experience, not economic indicators.

"You can't argue with people about their own lived reality," she says.

That may help explain why Trump's challenge looks so similar to Biden's.

Biden's critics blamed him for inflation. But he could point to forces largely outside his control, including the pandemic and its supply chain aftershocks.

Some economists argue that today's higher prices are at least partly the result of his own policies, including tariffs, as well as the economic fallout from the US-Israel war with Iran.

"The inflation is clearly traceable to Donald Trump," says Peter Morici, a University of Maryland economist who aligns with Republicans.

"It's his tariffs and the war. People know that. They see it."

Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs, announced in April 2025, have added to costs for consumers.

A study by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found the tariffs increased consumer goods prices by about 2.9%.

Donald Trump pledged to lower prices for US households

The impact might have been larger if Trump hadn't later paused some tariffs, struck new trade deals and been blocked by the Supreme Court from using emergency powers to impose many others.

Then came another shock. Conflict with Iran pushed oil prices higher, lifting the cost of petrol and putting fresh pressure on household budgets.

The White House points to tax cuts, job growth and lower prescription drug costs as evidence that the administration is delivering for American families. And it is increasingly embracing policies aimed at easing pressures.

Trump has called for a cap on credit card interest rates and floated the idea of a $5,000 payment to American adults if Republicans retain control of Congress.

The measures reflect a political reality familiar to both parties: when voters are anxious about prices, arguments about solid growth and low unemployment are rarely enough.

Biden never found a way to bridge the gap between economic performance and economic sentiment. The question heading into the midterms is whether Trump can.

If recent political history is any guide, perceptions of the economy may matter more than the economic data itself.

#1954. Parma vs. Udinese - prediction, team news, lineups

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Seye Omidiora | 27 Oct 2026 05:47, Last updated: 27 Oct 2026 06:18

Udinese are in danger of slipping into the Serie A relegation zone as they travel to face second-bottom Parma in Wednesday's gameweek-nine contest at Stadio Ennio Tardini.

Alberto Gilardino has overseen three consecutive defeats since replacing Carlos Cuesta last month and hopes to end that run against Kosta Runjaic, whose position in Udine is also under threat as Le Zebrette seek to halt their six-match losing sequence.

Just as Parma appeared set to go 270 minutes without scoring under Gilardino, Simone Lontani found the net fortuitously against joint leaders Lazio when his attempted cross went in off Christos Mandas's post.

That strike was the Emilians' fifth league goal of the season, comfortably the fewest in the division after eight matches, while the second-bottom hosts have conceded 15 at the other end.

If Gilardino is to take any positives from the 2-1 loss in the capital, it must be his team's strong showing for 45 minutes at the Olimpico, where they held the Biancocelesti at half time before conceding twice within eight minutes before the hour mark to all but seal their fate.

Seeking encouragement from the defeat, the former striker will hope to lift his dejected players for Wednesday as the Crusaders strive to climb out of the relegation zone with the right result.

Victory for the 19th-placed side could lift them out of the bottom three, although that outcome depends on Fiorentina dropping points against Atalanta in Florence.

Facing the beleaguered Runjaic, who may be on borrowed time in Udine, could be timely for the Emilians as they attempt to inflict a seventh consecutive defeat on the Little Zebras.

When Udinese began the season unbeaten in four matches across all competitions, winning twice in the Coppa Italia and collecting four points from six in the top flight, few would have predicted their ongoing malaise.

Unfortunately, Le Zebrette have since suffered six straight defeats and failed to score in three, including the last two, which have brought 3-0 and 2-0 home losses to Lecce and Milan respectively.

Those two setbacks have highlighted the team's decline in front of goal, having scored in each of their opening six matches across all competitions, including a draw with Como and three times in a 5-3 loss to defending champions Inter Milan.

However, Runjaic's men have now fired blanks in three of their last four matches, losing all four during that period to leave them on the verge of slipping into the dreaded bottom three.

Udinese have competed in Serie A for 32 years, but another setback in Emilia-Romagna could force Gino Pozzo to take decisive action regarding the club's managerial position.

Nesta Elphege (illness), Pontus Almqvist (calf) and Hans Nicolussi Caviglia (thigh) are injured and unavailable for Parma on Wednesday; Gilardino will make a late decision on Lautaro Valenti, who has been absent since mid-September but is nearing a return.

Parma are the league's lowest scorers, with only three players finding the net for the Emilians: David Romero and Lontani have struck twice apiece, while the injured Almqvist has scored once.

While Udinese are in a considerably healthier goalscoring position, with six different players finding the net for the Little Zebras in Serie A, only Jurgen Ekkelenkamp has scored more than twice, netting three.

Hassane Kamara enters gameweek nine as the only other player in the squad with more than one goal, a disappointing development after Keinan Davis scored 10 and the trio of Nicolo Zaniolo, Arthur Atta and Ekkelenkamp combined for 15 last season.

However, Davis has yet to add to his Coppa Italia goal in August, Zaniolo has made only four appearances across all competitions and Atta has departed for Fiorentina.

On the injury front, Alessandro Zanoli and Oumar Solet are touch-and-go due to muscle complaints, while David Alaba (calf), Vakoun Bayo (thigh) and Idrissa Guey (ankle) are definitely unavailable.
