#5599. Inside the resilience gap

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 2026 State of Grid Resilience puts data behind the pressures utility leaders face as they strengthen resilience while balancing affordability, performance and investment.

Utilities do not need research to tell them the operating environment has become more demanding. They see it in the systems they manage, the investment decisions they make and the expectations customers and regulators place on them.

New research helps quantify that experience.

Three in four utility and energy industry professionals surveyed for the 2026 State of Grid Resilience say operational risk is higher than it was five years ago. More than half expect load growth on their distribution systems to be very or extremely significant over the next decade. Nearly four in five expect resilience-related investment to increase over the next five years.

At the same time, 61% identify affordability and customer rate pressure as the biggest barrier to accelerating that investment.

Taken together, the findings show a challenge utilities have navigated for years. They are committed to resilience, but how resilience is measured, funded and deployed can affect how quickly they can act.

Utilities already recognize a distinction between reliability and resilience. Nearly half of respondents define reliability as day-to-day operational performance and resilience as performance under disruption.

The measures guiding investment do not always reflect that distinction.

Traditional reliability metrics such as SAIDI, SAIFI and CAIDI provide an important and consistent view of everyday grid performance. But only 48% of respondents agree that current reliability metrics adequately capture performance during major disruptive events.

The difference becomes clearer when utilities are asked what matters during those events. More than half say restoring service as quickly as possible is the most important outcome during a major storm or disruption. When asked what best demonstrates a resilient grid, respondents first point to limiting the number of customers affected and quickly restoring normal operations.

Yet investment decisions remain weighted toward traditional reliability. SAIDI and customer outage frequency are the most-cited metrics influencing investment decisions. Only 17% cite resilience-specific measures such as extreme-event recovery and adaptive capacity.

Reliability measures remain essential. Adding measures of what customers experience during major disruptions, including how many are affected and how quickly service is restored, can provide a more complete picture of performance under stress.

Utilities also confirmed a familiar tension between investing and keeping electricity affordable.

Nearly four in five respondents expect resilience investment to increase over the next five years. Yet 61% identify affordability and customer rate pressure as the biggest barrier to moving faster, more than three times the percentage citing competing capital priorities.

Utilities are already making the case for resilience in terms of customer value. Nearly nine in ten respondents justify resilience investments through service quality and reliability, either alone or combined with cost avoidance. Forty-seven percent use both cost avoidance and reliability improvement.

Better measures of performance during major events could help strengthen that case. If an investment can limit the number of customers affected or restore service faster, utilities have another way to show its value to customers and regulators.

Affordability makes prioritization especially important. Utilities must determine not only how much to invest, but where that investment can make the greatest difference.

The research reinforces the attention utilities are placing on the distribution grid.

Fifty-eight percent of respondents say most customer-impacting outages originate on distribution. This aligns with U.S. Department of Energy research finding that more than 90% of electric power interruptions originate on the distribution system. Fifty-three percent of respondents also identify distribution as part of the grid offering the greatest opportunity to improve customer outage outcomes over the next decade.

Their investment priorities tell a similar story. Fault location, isolation and service restoration (FLISR), protective devices and sectionalizing and distribution automation rank highest for resilience return.

Full deployment remains limited. Automated switches and reclosers are fully deployed at 33% of respondents’ utilities, compared with 18% for FLISR and 12% for self-healing distribution automation.

Respondents identify faster restoration and fewer customers affected as automation’s greatest operational benefits. Those are also among the outcomes they value most during major disruptions.

Investment is beginning to follow. Over the next five years, respondents expect the greatest increases in distribution investment in distribution automation, FLISR, undergrounding and pole and wire hardening.

“The research shows that utilities are being asked to strengthen grid resilience in an increasingly challenging operating environment,” said Anders Sjoelin, president and CEO of S&C Electric Company. “Operational risk is rising, with electricity dependence and demand growing and affordability remains a significant constraint. These findings reinforce the importance of prioritizing investments that deliver the greatest impact for customers and communities, particularly on the distribution grid.”

The research does not suggest utilities are waiting to act. Most expect resilience investment to grow and 79% are confident their current investment trajectory will meaningfully improve resilience within five years.

Instead, the findings support what many utilities are already experiencing. Better measures of performance during major events can help demonstrate the customer value of resilience. A clearer value case can support investment in the distribution technologies that limit customer impact and speed restoration.

The 2026 State of Grid Resilience provides a benchmark for that work and for an industry conversation already underway. As measurement evolves and investment continues, future research can show whether the gaps between resilience goals, affordability and deployment begin to narrow.

#5591. When does a BI value stop being the right number?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For energy companies, the assumptions behind a BI value can change materially during the policy period. Margins and throughput can shift, while repair timelines can extend. External events can also change the economics of an operation quickly. Yet the declared BI value and the policy provisions governing recovery may remain unchanged.

This raises a practical question: what should trigger a reassessment, and how will the policy respond if the exposure has changed materially?

A BI calculation draws on assumptions about revenue, margins, production, throughput, fixed costs and recovery time. Those inputs can change at different rates and for different reasons.

For a downstream operator, a significant movement in crack spreads can change the earnings exposure associated with an interruption. For a midstream operation, a change in throughput or a prolonged constraint on a critical pipeline can alter the financial impact. Across the energy value chain, an extended repair or recovery period can materially increase the duration of a loss.

Organizations should establish materiality thresholds for reassessment, supported by a consistent methodology for revisiting the valuation when conditions change. Where a material change increases exposure, organizations may also need to consider whether to redeclare their values.

Aon’s approach to business interruption valuation provides a methodology for reassessing the valuation when material changes occur.

A stated volatility allowance can overstate the protection available if the exposure has increased materially or operations resume during the recovery period.

The Lloyd’s Market Association introduced an updated Onshore Energy BI Volatility Clause in 2024 after carriers identified the previous version as unsuitable for partial losses. The revised clause was intended to address the policy’s indemnity exposure in those circumstances.

Organizations should model the provision against realistic loss scenarios rather than rely on the stated percentage.

Production may resume at 60% of capacity while repairs continue. The loss has not ended.

Revenue may recover partially while margins remain affected, equipment is still under repair or other constraints limit operations. The financial loss can therefore follow a different trajectory from physical production and the policy response may change with it.

Some onshore property provisions can reduce the applicable volatility cap when partial operations resume, potentially taking the cap below 100% of the reported BI value. The specific outcome depends on the policy wording and the circumstances of the loss. Organizations should discuss whether such a provision is appropriate for their operations and understand how it could restrict recovery.

As Greg Reynolds, Onshore Energy Property Lead at Aon, puts it:

“The critical issue is how the loss develops through disruption and recovery. Partial operations, shifting margins and repair delays can change both the earnings impact and the policy response.”

Effective modeling tests how critical assets, dependencies, mitigation measures and financial impacts evolve across different loss scenarios.

Downstream businesses may be highly sensitive to refining margins, while many midstream businesses rely predominantly on fee-based revenue. LNG exposures may also be less sensitive to margin volatility depending on their commercial structure. A volatility mechanism designed around commodity margins may therefore have limited relevance for some operations, making alternative wording or a carve-out more appropriate.

The starting point should be the economics of the business rather than a standardized treatment across the energy value chain.

Scenario testing should focus on developments capable of changing either the exposure or the policy response:

Test each scenario against the declared BI value, volatility cap, deductible, indemnity period and treatment of partial operations to identify where the program has headroom and where assumptions or terms warrant attention.

This should be an ongoing conversation through the policy year, not only as a discussion before renewal. When conditions change materially, organizations should work with their insurance partners to assess whether the valuation and policy remain fit for purpose. Diagnostics can support that review by helping identify how changing conditions may affect the exposure.

#4275. Scientists discover Arctic sea ice loss could be far worse than predicted

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Arctic could lose roughly one million square kilometers more winter sea ice than scientists previously predicted, according to new research that draws on evidence from a warmer Arctic 6,000 years ago. By using ancient climate records to refine modern computer models, researchers have produced more reliable estimates of how much ice may disappear in the coming decades.

The findings, published in Nature Geoscience, could also have important consequences for winter weather in the United Kingdom and other parts of Europe.

Much of the attention surrounding Arctic ice loss has focused on the dramatic retreat of summer sea ice. Winter ice, however, has received considerably less scrutiny, even though climate models show their greatest disagreements when predicting how much ice will cover the Arctic during the colder months.

Why Arctic Winter Sea Ice Matters for Global Weather

Winter sea ice influences weather far beyond the Arctic by controlling how much heat and moisture escape from the ocean into the atmosphere. Declining ice cover has been associated with colder winters and increased snowfall in the United Kingdom, northern Europe, and North America.

Recent observations have also highlighted the Arctic's changing conditions. In March 2025, winter sea ice reached the lowest annual maximum extent recorded since modern monitoring began. The Arctic matched that record again in 2026.

To improve predictions of future ice loss, scientists at the University of Cambridge and Mexico's Centro de Investigación en Matematicas (CIMAT) turned to evidence preserved beneath the Arctic Ocean.

The team assembled the largest database of its kind containing a chemical marker produced by algae that live within sea ice. The evidence came from sediment cores deposited during the mid-Holocene, approximately 6,000 years ago, when small changes in Earth's orbit helped create a warmer Arctic with less extensive ice cover.

These ancient conditions offered a valuable opportunity to investigate how Arctic sea ice responds to warming and to test whether today's climate models accurately represent that response.

Ancient Ocean Sediments Reveal What Climate Models Missed

The researchers examined concentrations of a biomarker known as IP25, which is produced by algae associated with sea ice. They combined these measurements with other chemical markers left behind by organisms living in open water, allowing them to reconstruct Arctic ice conditions thousands of years in the past.

Next, they compared those reconstructions with simulations generated by 27 climate models for the same ancient period.

Using a statistical method called Bayesian weighting, the researchers assigned greater importance to models that more closely matched the historical evidence. Models that performed less accurately received less influence over the final predictions.

Because the underlying physical processes controlling sea ice remain consistent across different climate conditions, the team could apply these same weights to simulations of both the recent past and the future.

The comparison revealed a significant problem: collectively, the climate models predicted more winter sea ice than the ancient evidence supported.

"When we apply the model weights to historical simulations, we completely eliminated the positive bias," said lead author Crystal Fu, from Cambridge's Scott Polar Research Institute. "The future projections similarly end up being lower than previous estimates, by about a million square kilometers."

More Accurate Arctic Ice Forecasts Reduce Uncertainty by 85%

Scientists have collected substantial evidence about Earth's ancient climates, but translating that information into reliable forecasts remains difficult. Both historical reconstructions and future climate simulations contain uncertainties that complicate direct comparisons.

"The scientific community has amassed lots of paleoclimate records over recent decades, but it's remained a challenge to directly link these records to future projections, since there is uncertainty at both ends," said Fu.

The team's new approach substantially narrowed those uncertainties, reducing the range of possible outcomes in some projections by as much as 85%.

"By using evidence from the past, we can now produce more reliable maps of where Arctic winter sea ice is likely to disappear," said co-author Marco Aquino-López, of CIMAT. "These give us a much clearer picture of what the Arctic might look like in the coming decades, and how much uncertainty remains."

Although the warming that occurred during the mid-Holocene was driven by changes in Earth's orbit rather than the human activities responsible for today's warming, Fu emphasized that the response of sea ice to rising temperatures follows the same basic physical principles.

"It doesn't matter if the warming is natural variation or caused by human activity: if it warms, sea ice is going to melt," she said.

Arctic Ice Loss Could Have Wider Consequences for Europe

The findings may have implications that extend beyond the disappearance of Arctic sea ice itself.

When the researchers applied their improved model weighting to projections of precipitation, they found indications that current climate models may also underestimate future snowfall in southeastern Greenland and rainfall across the Mediterranean region.

Greater snowfall over Greenland could affect the stability of the Greenland ice sheet, while increased Mediterranean rainfall could influence future flooding risks in southern Europe.

These potential effects suggest that the consequences of a warming Arctic may be more extensive than existing predictions indicate. The revised simulations offer researchers a clearer foundation for investigating those risks and understanding how changing Arctic conditions could influence weather elsewhere.

#2215. Post-match comments from Fabian Hurzeler will have Liverpool fans scratching their heads

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Brighton head coach Fabian Hurzeler claimed that only one team ‘tried to win’ in his side’s 2-0 defeat to Liverpool at Anfield on Sunday.

The match was decided inside a five-minute spell just before the midway point of the first half as a Pascal Gross own goal and an Alexander Isak strike ultimately settled the result in the Reds’ favour.

Despite what the final score might suggest, it was far from comfortable for the home side, who needed to be hugely resolute at the back in the second half to emerge with victory and a clean sheet.

Speaking to reporters after the game, Hurzeler was proud of his team’s overall performance but rued the lack of a clinical edge.

The Brighton boss said (via Liverpool Echo): “I think everyone in the stadium felt there was a missed opportunity for us because it’s not only after the goal or the goals they made [that Brighton created chances].

“Before they scored, we had one or two big chances, and today I think it was just the difference that they scored and we didn’t. They kept a clean sheet because they defended well, with their centre-backs, with full-back [Ronald] Araujo, also with [Milos] Kerkez.

“I think they blocked a lot of shots. They really defended the box well. We didn’t have that efficiency. I think there was much more in it and we definitely deserved more.

“When we only look at the performance today, everyone in the stadium saw that there was one team who really tried to create and tried to win this game, and therefore I have to praise my players.”

Hurzeler can rightly be pleased with his team’s overall performance, but it’d be erroneous to suggest that Liverpool parked the bus rather than trying to win the game.

Even aside from the two goals, some of the Reds’ attacking play in the first half in particular was superb to watch, and on another day Cody Gakpo might well have found the net rather than the stands with a couple of his shots.

The Seagulls had substantially more possession, recorded more shots and posted a higher xG (as per Sofascore), but it wasn’t as though there was one-way traffic towards the home side for the full 90 minutes.

Brighton may well have taken something from the game if they were more clinical and if Liverpool’s defending wasn’t so resolute, and Hurzeler’s side will surely be in European contention this season if they maintain this level of performance.

From a Reds perspective, it was an immensely satisfying and hard-fought victory over a strong opponent, and the first 30 minutes in particular set the standards to which they must aspire for the rest of the campaign.

#2481. Genoa vs Juventus - prediction, team news, lineups

By Jonathan O'Shea | 26 Oct 2026 08:44

After relieving some pressure with a weekend win, Juventus aim to build more momentum on Wednesday night, when they visit Genoa at Stadio Ferraris.

Both sides claimed three points on Sunday, but neither will be content with their place in the Serie A standings.

With just one win from their previous four games, Juventus could not afford to slip up when they visited Lecce on Sunday evening, and they duly delivered a 2-0 win.

First-half strikes from Weston McKennie and Douglas Luiz put the Bianconeri in charge at the Via Del Mare, and they ultimately wrapped up maximum points.

Having followed a 3-0 home loss to Lazio with a 2-2 Europa League draw against Rennes, that result eased some pressure on an increasingly agitated Luciano Spalletti, whose position is apparently under threat.

However, Juve still trail behind Serie A’s other top-four contenders, occupying seventh place on 14 points from eight matches.

Sitting below newly promoted Frosinone - and just above city rivals Torino - the Turin giants must try to post back-to-back wins.

Juve’s recent record against Genoa suggests that they can: after a 2-0 home victory in April, they have kept clean sheets in all of the last five league meetings, taking 13 points from a possible 15.

In fact, Genoa’s last league win over Juventus dates back to May 2022, and since then they have only scored once.

The Grifone are currently trying to distance themselves from the drop zone, having flirted with relegation before finishing in 16th spot last term.

Daniele De Rossi’s tenure looked like coming to an end when they started the campaign with three successive defeats, but he was retained and Genoa have since taken seven points from five games.

Having followed a 2-1 defeat of Fiorentina by losing to De Rossi’s beloved Roma, the Ligurian side secured their second home win of the season on Sunday.

With an unstoppable shot from outside the box, Mikael Ellertsson scored the only goal against his former club, as Genoa beat rock-bottom Venezia.

Now back in 16th place, all of their points have been won on home turf at the Ferraris, where Juventus will be sure of a hostile reception.

Facing dismissal just before the first anniversary of his appointment, Spalletti recently switched to a more secure 3-5-2 formation, pairing Nick Woltemade and Randal Kolo Muani up front.

That partnership should continue, but Juventus could make some changes amid a frantic run of fixtures - albeit options are severely limited by absences.

Both injured against Rennes, Zeki Celik and Nico Gonzalez have joined Francisco Conceicao, Kenan Yildiz, Khephren Thuram, Jeff Ekhator, Kamil Grabara and captain Manuel Locatelli in Juve's packed treatment room.

Meanwhile, Genoa will be without Djibril Sow, Elias Havel and Lorenzo Venturino, while Samuel Wiafe suffered an adductor strain at the weekend and is unlikely to play.

Though Lorenzo Colombo has returned from a layoff and made a second-half cameo on Sunday, top scorer Milutin Osmajic is set to lead the hosts’ attack.

Junior Messias, Stephan El Shaarawy, Vitinha and Tommaso Baldanzi are all vying for selection behind the Montenegrin striker.

Real Madrid came away from Camp Nou on Sunday night with three points and a new fitness worry. José Mourinho revealed after the 2-1 win over Barcelona that Fede Valverde had spent a large part of the game carrying an injury, and the timing is far from ideal with three away fixtures to come in the space of seven days.

The coach went out of his way to single out the Uruguayan in his post-match press conference, saying Valverde “played injured for more than 40 minutes”. Mourinho added: “He got injured, wanted to continue, pushed himself to his limits, and gave everything while he was on the field.”

Mourinho did not say what kind of injury Valverde had suffered or at what point in the match it happened. At the time of writing, Real Madrid had not published a medical report on the midfielder, who was named in the starting XI alongside Aurélien Tchouaméni, Bernardo Silva and Jude Bellingham.

The club’s English account still made him one of the faces of the night, sharing a Valverde post shortly after midnight.

The victory was Madrid’s fifth in a row and, as Mourinho noted, leaves them three points behind Barcelona rather than six or nine. Keeping that momentum through three away games will be far easier with Valverde available, so the club’s next medical update on the midfielder will be closely watched before the trip to Santander.

#2347. Uncomfortable truth revealed behind the headlines of West Ham’s £200m summer sales | Exclusive

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With the January transfer window just under ten weeks away, some supporters are hoping for new additions to help push the club to automatic promotion.

However, despite generating huge sums from the sales of stars such as Mateus Fernandes, Crysencio Summerville and El Hadji Malick Diouf, West Ham United’s financial position remains far tighter than many supporters might expect.

While the summer window brought in substantial transfer income, much of that money was never destined for splashy new signings. Instead, it has been used to repair the club’s financial foundations and address several long-standing obligations behind the scenes.

There is likely to be some transfer business in January, with more players expected to leave, as C&H learnt over the weekend from one our sources, including Magassa, Alvarez and Cornet. The club will also not stand in the way of Areola departing given his contract is set to expire at the end of the season.

Fernandes brought in a huge transfer fee from Tottenham

Any money raised from these player sales will be used to fund transfers, yet there is only a small budget, and West Ham simply can’t spend freely.

West Ham entered the summer on the back of a record pre-tax loss, driven by reduced revenues following a disappointing league campaign and the absence of European football. Combined with a historically high wage bill, the club needed significant player sales to support compliance with Profitability and Sustainability Rules (PSR) and ease financial pressure.

The club still had substantial outstanding transfer commitments from previous recruitment drives. As a result, a large share of the money generated this summer was effectively earmarked for instalment payments owed to other clubs rather than fresh investment in the squad.

The eye-catching figures attached to player sales tell only part of the story. Modern transfer deals are typically structured over several years, meaning the total value reported publicly is often far higher than the cash received immediately. That significantly restricts short-term spending power.

Reducing the wage bill was a key priority for the board this summer, resulting in many players having their salaries reduced by half after relegation.

By also moving on or loaning out several high earners and targeting lower-cost arrivals, West Ham have taken meaningful steps towards bringing their wage expenditure back under control after it had risen to unsustainable levels relative to revenue.

Football financial expert Kieran Maguire estimates that dropping into the Championship can cost a club £100m+ through reduced broadcast, commercial and matchday revenues.

The perception that West Ham suddenly became wealthy after raising close to £200 million in transfer fees is misleading. The reality is that much of the income has been absorbed by historic liabilities, financial compliance requirements and cash flow management.

The summer’s business was less about building a transfer war chest and more about restoring stability, and the knock on effect will limit player purchases in January. Nils Koppen, it would seem, has a difficult job ahead of him.

#5463. Amflow launches two powerful new electric bikes, including its first SUV-style e-bike

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amflow, the electric bicycle brand that burst onto the scene with some of the most powerful and lightweight electric mountain bikes on the market, is expanding its lineup with two major releases. The company has announced global availability of its new Amflow TL Carbon, while also bringing its removable-battery Amflow PR Carbon series to the US for the first time.

Both models are powered by the latest Avinox mid-drive motor systems, which have quickly earned a reputation for delivering impressive power in surprisingly compact packages.

The biggest departure from Amflow’s roots is the new TL Carbon, which takes the company’s high-performance mountain biking DNA and applies it to a more versatile SUV-style electric bike. While less common in the US (ironically), SUV-style e-bikes are essentially a hybrid electric bicycle designed to act as an all-in-one, “do-it-all” vehicle combining the rugged build and off-road capability of an electric mountain bike with the practical, everyday utility of a city or trekking bike.

Rather than focusing exclusively on technical trails and aggressive mountain biking, the TL Carbon is designed to handle everything from daily commuting to touring and off-road adventures. And with Avinox’s RS800 battery featuring 800Wh of capacity, it’s got plenty of energy on board for diverse adventures.

The full-suspension bike uses the Avinox M2 motor, delivering up to 125 Nm of torque, which should provide more than enough grunt for steep climbs, heavy loads, and just about anything else riders can throw at it.

Avinox e-bike drive systems are known for many things, but chief among them is the extremely high power and torque they offer, eclipsing most other mid-drive motor systems on the market.

Despite that power and versatility, the TL Carbon weighs as little as 22.6 kg (49.8 lb). It also boasts an impressive maximum total weight capacity of 200 kg (441 lb), including the bike itself, rider, and cargo carried on its front and rear racks.

That’s a compelling combination for riders who want the performance of a premium electric mountain bike but need something practical enough to replace a car for everyday trips.

And perhaps most surprisingly, the price isn’t completely outrageous. The Amflow TL Carbon starts at $3,899 in the US and €3,499 in Germany, positioning it competitively against premium European trekking and SUV-style e-bikes.

Meanwhile, American riders are finally getting access to the Amflow PR Carbon series, which had previously launched in other markets.

Unlike Amflow’s earlier electric mountain bikes, the PR series features a removable battery, addressing one of the biggest practical complaints about high-performance eMTBs with integrated batteries.

The new 800 Wh battery weighs just 4 kg (8.8 lb) and can be removed for convenient indoor charging or swapped for a fresh battery to extend a ride. That’s especially useful for riders who don’t have a convenient charging outlet near their bike storage area.

Amflow claims up to 158 km (98 miles) of range under ideal conditions, though that’s based on relatively gentle riding on flat pavement in Eco mode. Riders tackling technical trails with high assistance levels should expect considerably less. But any way you slice it, 800 Wh is a huge battery for eMTBs and should deliver plenty of range under most conditions.

The standard PR Carbon uses the Avinox M2 motor with up to 125 Nm of torque, while the higher-end PR Carbon Pro upgrades to the more powerful Avinox M2S motor, producing up to 150 Nm of torque.

In the US, the PR Carbon starts at $4,999, while the PR Carbon Pro commands $6,799. European pricing starts at €4,499 and €5,899, respectively, based on German pricing.

All three bikes are available beginning today through ⁠Amflow’s official website and its network of authorized dealers. In the US, both models generally arrive as Class 1 e-bikes (no throttle) with a top speed of 20 mph (32 km/h), though Amflow models can typically be unlocked to Class 3 performance with a top speed of 28 mph (45 km/h).

Amflow has been one of the more interesting brands to watch in the premium e-bike space, largely because of how aggressively it has pushed the boundaries of motor performance and bike weight.

But these latest releases show that the company is thinking beyond simply building increasingly powerful electric mountain bikes.

The TL Carbon is particularly interesting to me because it brings that impressive Avinox performance to a more practical platform. As someone who spends a minority of my riding time in the dirt, Amflow suddenly looks a lot more interesting to me, personally. Not everyone needs a dedicated eMTB, but plenty of riders would appreciate a lightweight, powerful, well-equipped electric bike that can handle both weekday commuting and weekend adventures.

And the PR Carbon’s removable battery is a welcome development, especially as the industry continues to recognize that convenience and serviceability matter just as much as outright performance.

With the Avinox ecosystem continuing to expand across more bike brands and categories, Amflow is also facing increasing competition from other manufacturers using the same impressive motors. That makes it all the more important for the brand to differentiate itself through thoughtful bike design, not just headline-grabbing power figures.
