#5503. If Google Fells 250,000 Trees In Finland, Does It Make A Sound?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What I don’t know about Finland would fill a small library. As a fan of Formula One, I know that many Finnish surnames have multiple k’s — Mika Hakkinen, and Kimi Raikkonen, for example, not to mention World Rally driver Tommi Makkinen. In the world of motorsports, some like to say, “To finish first, you must first be Finnish.”

It’s also a great place to view the northern lights, and it tends to be cold there. Those low temperatures have attracted data center developers, because keeping warehouses full of computers cool is much easier to do when it is near freezing outside instead of broiling hot.

That is one reason why Google is planning to spend $13 billion building two new data centers in Finland. Things are not going well for Google, however. It started out with the slogan “Don’t be evil,” but has transitioned to being one of the largest invaders of personal space, as it helps governments around the world keep track of  citizens who dare to think thoughts not sanctioned by official policy.

According to The Guardian, the company sparked outrage in Finland after it chopped down about 250,000 trees to make way for its data center in the town of Muhos on the border with Sweden. Then it was revealed it felled another 150,000 trees at another site in Otanmäki, which is located in the southern part of the country. Recently, the Finnish Licensing and Supervision Authority (LVV) ordered Google to stop work on both projects because it failed to complete the necessary environmental impact assessments before clearing the land.

Last week, Antti Järvinen, Google’s manager for operations in Finland, apologized for obliterating nearly 400,000 trees without permission, but claimed the company had acted “on the best understanding we had of the legislation at the time.” In other words, he played the Steve Martin card.

The furor over the trees has forced Finland to take a closer look at its permitting laws and procedures. To be fair, it’s economy is not as robust as some of its European neighbors, and the government had been working diligently to cut expenditures wherever possible. It has also been encouraging foreign investment.

When Google announced last month it was prepared to invest $13 billion in the country, prime minister Petteri Orpo said attracting investment was a “top priority” and “the value of the data economy extends far beyond direct investment”. He said the Google deal would “deliver lasting benefits for both parties.”

That’s what political leaders always say, but they seldom talk about the infrastructure costs associated with such economic opportunities. The people putting up the money expect airports, roads, schools, hospitals, and emergency services to be built by the local community, but they never offer to pay for those things, so the costs fall on the shoulders of local residents.

Jussi Kangasharju, head of computer science at the University of Helsinki, said that while data centers brought a limited number of jobs, potentially boosting the local economy, the benefits on a national level were minimal. “Really, for Finland,there are no benefits. They are international companies serving international customers. Very little corporate taxes go to Finland. Data centers ultimately have very little benefit for others except the operator.”

In all, Finland is expected to become the home for more than 145 data centers. Many of its citizens want to know why their country’s renewable energy resources are being used to power the dreams of international businesses. More than 93,000 people have signed a petition demanding more data center regulations and a fee to compensate local communities for environmental damage. Prime Minister Orpo calls what happened in Muhos as a “cold shower”.

Hanna Halmeenpää, chair of Finnish Association for Nature Conservation (SLI) board, said about Google’s bad behavior, “At first I couldn’t believe it. I have never heard that any company has done anything like this in Finland.” She said the system depended a lot on trust, which she believes Google took advantage of. “They bought the vast land area and they just started to cut it, just like that. It’s pure arrogance and it’s amazing.”

Vesa Hyyryläinen, chair of the local SLI chapter, told The Guardian, “All of us nature conservationists are very worried about our Finnish political situation and politics. I wonder why we have to sacrifice so much of our nature. They give everything away too easily. Young people go to Helsinki, and Berlin, and Paris. There are so few people. We have to sacrifice,” he said.

In fact, since the trees were eradicated, the forest animals who lived there have moved to nearby cities, which has made both them and the residents unhappy. There were lots of wolves living in the forest who have now taken up residence in local communities.

We tend to forget that forests and oceans are home to a wide variety of flora and fauna, and that when we degrade them, there are consequences that affect us all. Foreign investments come with a cost, and all that lovely money is cold comfort if your children have to negotiate with wolves on their way to school.

Anni Ranta lives in Otanmäki, a former mining village known for its rare earth elements. She worries about something else. Finland’s neighbor to the east is Russia. Ranta fears a data center in her town could be a target for Russian drone attacks one day. “I don’t want (Google) here. I don’t want them in Finland at all. I don’t like the whole idea of AI, and the world changing, and the way it’s changing. It’s going too fast, too soon.”

The Inter Miami forward shared his thoughts in an interview with EFE in Oviedo, Spain, ahead of the 2026 Princess of Asturias Award for Sport ceremony on October 23. His message looked beyond the numbers and achievements that have shaped his reputation, placing the emphasis on a quality that remained constant throughout his career, even when the results did not go his way.

His return to Spain marked a special occasion for the former Barcelona captain, who spent the most influential years of his career at the Catalan club before moving to Paris Saint-Germain and subsequently joining Inter Miami. The Princess of Asturias Foundation recognized both his extraordinary achievements on the pitch and his ongoing charitable work supporting access to education and healthcare for disadvantaged children.

#5507. What Can Grid Flexibility Actually Do Here?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Electricity-system flexibility has become an increasingly large bucket. Batteries go into it. Demand response goes into it. Virtual power plants, EV charging, interconnectors, hydro, thermal storage and grid-enhancing technologies all get described as flexibility too.

Those resources can all be useful, but a national total of “flexible megawatts” does not tell a grid operator very much about whether a congested transformer can take another connection, whether an evening peak can be covered, or whether the system can get through several difficult days.

I’ve published a new open 99-page TFIE Strategy report, The Flexibility Mesh: A Whole-System Architecture for Reliable, Affordable Electrification, to put those different jobs into one common architecture.

The animation runs the same underlying electricity system through four operating moments. During renewable-surplus midday, useful actions can include charging batteries and EVs, heating water, increasing some industrial loads and exporting electricity where there is somewhere useful for it to go. Move into an evening or winter peak and batteries discharge, some charging can pause, buildings can use heat stored earlier, hydro can increase output and industrial demand may reduce within its operating constraints.

A local network constraint creates a different problem again. An overloaded transformer does not care that there are thousands of megawatts of flexible capacity somewhere else in the country. The resource has to be electrically relevant to that part of the network. Storage, managed charging, operating measures and eventually reinforcement can all have roles, but they have to be compared against the actual constraint.

Prolonged multi-day stress changes the portfolio once more. Response speed remains useful, but duration, replenishment and correlated availability become much more important. A resource that can respond beautifully for two hours does not acquire three days of stored energy simply because it is fast.

This is why the report treats flexibility as a system property rather than a technology category. Different jobs require different resources, locations and durations, while grid expansion and operational flexibility are complementary rather than competing philosophies.

That distinction is easy to lose in technology-led discussions. A battery can be excellent at intraday shifting while being irrelevant to a voltage problem at the wrong node. An interconnector can provide valuable balancing and energy exchange but needs evidence that supply will be available at the other end during the hours for which planners want to rely on it. Industrial loads can be highly controllable while remaining unavailable at particular times because production still has to happen.

The physical resource is also only the beginning of the delivery problem. An asset can be installed, connected and technically controllable without being available to provide the service under discussion. Network permission may be missing. The resource may not be qualified for the relevant product. Customer consent or operating limits may constrain it. Measurement and settlement arrangements have to show what actually happened.

This sounds like administrative detail until the system starts depending on the capacity. Then the difference between theoretical capability and dependable delivery becomes the difference between a useful planning resource and an optimistic spreadsheet assumption.

The commercial side has similar boundaries. Avoiding a grid upgrade may create significant value for electricity consumers, but that avoided cost is not automatically revenue for the company coordinating flexible assets. The beneficiary, buyer, payer, asset owner and service provider can be different parties. A viable arrangement needs to connect the physical job to a legitimate payment route and leave enough value for customers and asset owners to continue participating.

The report makes those relationships visible because they are part of the operating architecture, not something that happens after the engineering is complete. System value, buyer budget, provider revenue, intermediary margin and customer benefit are separate quantities, and the business model has to reconcile them rather than assume they collapse into one pot of money.

Permanent grid infrastructure stays visible throughout. Flexibility can improve utilization of existing networks, help manage temporary constraints and sometimes defer or resize reinforcement. A persistent constraint accompanied by continuing load growth can still need more wire, transformer or substation capacity. Once new capacity is built, the residual flexibility requirement changes again.

That is one reason I called the report The Flexibility Mesh rather than building it around batteries, VPPs or demand response. The physical electricity system runs from interconnected regions through generation, transmission and distribution to customer premises. Storage, thermal flexibility, coordination, operating permission, markets, customer relationships and delivery evidence cross those layers.

The architecture is also available in an interactive version. Rather than trying to read the entire diagram at once, choose an operating moment and see which relationships become important. Individual components can then be explored in more detail, including the economic question: Who benefits, who pays?

The architecture is meant to support better questions, not force every electricity system into one portfolio. Norway, California, an industrial campus, an island grid and a constrained urban distribution network start with different resources and institutions. They can still ask the same thing: what service is required here, which resources can credibly provide it, what allows them to act, and what evidence shows that they actually deliver?

Published: 17:12 GMT, 23 October 2026 | Updated: 17:26 GMT, 23 October 2026

Had things turned out differently a few years ago, Derek McInnes could have been the manager who gave Josh Fowler his first big break in professional football.

Back in the summer of 2023, whilst in charge of Kilmarnock, McInnes took the young striker on a pre-season trip to Northern Ireland.

This was at a time when Fowler had just scored 49 goals in 78 games across two seasons with Beith Juniors.

Killie were not the only top-flight club keeping tabs on him, with Fowler also going on trial with Motherwell during a pre-season trip to the Netherlands.

In the end, neither club decided to take a punt on a young man who was still only 21 years old and who had been working as a joiner.

It could easily have been a sliding doors moment, the kind which sees a young player perhaps start to feel like their chance of making it as a professional has gone.

St Johnstone striker Josh Fowler is joint-top scorer in the Scottish Premiership

But Fowler chose a different route. He was a man in demand. He would eventually leave Beith to embark on spells with Gulf United in Dubai then St Albans Dinamo in Australia before heading back to Dubai City.

He returned home in 2025 for a spell at Queen’s Park which saw him score 19 goals in 25 matches and become top scorer in the Championship, before eventually signing for St Johnstone in January earlier this year.

The goals are now starting to flow in Perth as well. Fowler hit the winner in a 1-0 triumph over Aberdeen a fortnight ago and netted again in a 1-1 draw with Dundee United last week.

He has five goals in his last six matches. All in all, six goals in nine matches so far this season sees him arrive at Ibrox this afternoon to take on McInnes and Rangers as the league’s joint-top scorer.

It is quite a tale. A striker with electric pace plucked from obscurity further down the leagues, before eventually going on to score goals by the bucketload in the top flight, he could well be Scottish football’s answer to Jamie Vardy.

Fowler's nomadic career thus far has taken him to Dubai and Australia before his move to Perth

‘Even now, he still keeps in touch and takes an interest in how Beith are doing. I think there is a lot to be said for that kind of humility.

‘I can vividly remember Josh arriving at Beith as a young boy a few years ago. To be honest, the signing kind of came about by chance as much as anything.

‘Josh was working as an apprentice joiner and his lecturer at Cardonald College was Danny Shearer. Danny was our club president at the time, so Josh just asked him if he could come down for a trial.

‘I think he trained for a couple of nights then his first game was a pre-season friendly against Lugar Boswell Thistle which we won 8-0.

‘Josh played out wide on the left wing in that game and I remember speaking to our manager Bryan Young, who seemed to be a bit unsure whether we should sign him.

‘I basically said to Bryan: “Listen, we’ve got to sign him before someone else does”. I think the fact he then went on to score nearly 50 goals over two seasons probably vindicated that decision.

‘From what I could gather, I think Killie and Motherwell both liked him when they had him on trial. Why they didn’t sign him, I don’t know. You would have to ask them.

On his return from a second spell in Dubai, Fowler immediately caught the eye at Queen's Park

‘I’ve been involved with Beith for a long time now and I can tell you the overall quality and the level is far, far better than most people would give it credit for.

‘Josh is the perfect example of how you can find a hidden gem if you are willing to look down the leagues and take a chance on someone.

‘Listen, you never know, if he can maintain this form and finish the season as top scorer in the country, Sebastien Pocognoli could have a look at him for Scotland. I don’t see why not. What a story it would be.’

Given the way that Fowler has continually thrived every time he’s moved up the ladder to a higher level, it would be foolish to rule out a Scotland call-up.

Could Sebastien Pocognoli be tempted to cap Fowler's rapid rise with a Scotland call-up?

Since taking charge, Pocognoli has spoken of a desire to freshen the squad up and to try and bring through a newer generation of players.

Fowler is still only 24. All of his best years are ahead of him. There will be some, of course, who get sniffy about the idea of a St Johnstone player being called up to the Scotland squad. But Josh McPake would be a prime example of a player who has continued to blossom, having shone for Saints as well.

‘He was quite off-the-cuff with a lot of the things he did. In his first season, he was playing on instinct a lot of the time.

‘Then, in his second season, he went on to another level. In that second season he was easily the best striker in the league by a mile. He was lethal.

Fowler will lead the line for Simo Valakari's St Johnstone against Rangers at Ibrox this weekend

‘I was vice-captain at the time and me and a couple of boys used to car share with him. We knew there was interest in him.

‘Given the start he’s had to the season with St Johnstone, top scorer in the league, I would guarantee you there are clubs looking at him right now.

‘Especially if he goes and scores against Rangers at Ibrox this weekend, an English Championship club will come in for him in January. Clubs down south will always take a chance on someone who can score goals.’

Fowler has emerged as one of the feel-good stories in Scottish football over the past year or so.

A rough diamond plucked from the lower leagues, now thriving in the top flight.

His path has been anything but conventional. But he has proven he can cut it at the top level in Scotland. A young man who continues to take everything in his stride, it will be fascinating to see just how far he can go.

Franklin Templeton, one of the world's largest asset managers with $1.8tn in assets under management, has revealed that it has registered two funds that automatically reinvest their dividends earned from U.S. stocks into Bitcoin-related assets.

The two funds are named the Franklin US Equity Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF (FBEQ) and the Franklin US Innovation Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF (FIBT).

Rather than focusing on tracking the price of the flagship cryptocurrency like most spot Bitcoin ETFs, the new ETFs will offer a combination of U.S. stock market exposure with a strategy that seeks to slowly accumulate Bitcoin.

For starters, the two funds will hold 95% of their portfolio in U.S. equities, with the other 5% in Bitcoin-related assets.

A significant aspect of the two new funds revolves around their dividend reinvestment process, also known as DRIPs.

Traditional DRIPs automatically reinvest stock dividends to buy more shares of the respective companies.

In this case, the two new products from Franklin Templeton will reinvest those dividends into Bitcoin exposure.

Specifically, both regular and special dividends will be reinvested in Bitcoin-related assets on the next trading day after the ex-dividend date.

The two funds aim to slowly grow their Bitcoin exposure without investors having to buy any cryptocurrency assets themselves.

The new development from Franklin Templeton is another step in its journey of expanding crypto investment opportunities.

Currently, the asset management giant offers the Franklin Bitcoin ETF (EZBC), a spot Bitcoin investment product. The spot Bitcoin ETF has seen cumulative net inflows of roughly $335mn.
