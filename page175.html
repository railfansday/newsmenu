#702. McTominay stars in Napoli draw that leaves Allegri ‘not lucid enough’ to talk | Nicky Bandini

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gli Azzurri show positive signs of improvement in seesaw 3-3 draw against a Roma side inspired by Messi-like Matías Soulé

Scott McTominay was leaving nothing to doubt. He appeared to have scored with a close-range header early in the second half of Napoli’s game at home to Roma, but Pierluigi Gollini clawed the ball out from inside the goal. McTominay got his noggin to the rebound, nudging it back across the line. Roma’s keeper pushed away again, so the Napoli midfielder got to his feet and smashed the loose ball into the back of the net.

Just typical. You wait two months for your first goal of the Serie A season, then three show up at once. They only counted for one, of course, but McTominay looked happy enough with that as he ran to thank Matteo Politano for the assist.

What a difference it makes to this Napoli team when their Scottish star is fit and firing. McTominay was out of action for all of September, recovering from an ablation procedure to correct an irregular heartbeat – known medically as an arrhythmia. The condition was characterised by the club as mild and benign, and the medical intervention a complete success.

Napoli had struggled in McTominay’s absence. They continued to struggle right after his return, too. His first two starts back in the side came in defeats to Villarreal and Serie A’s last-placed team, Venezia – leaving the Partenopei with five losses from their first nine games in all competitions. The latter result prompted speculation about whether Massimiliano Allegri’s job as manager could soon be at risk.

A week later, McTominay is starting to change the narrative. His two goals in midweek helped Napoli to their first Champions League win of the season, 4-3 against Bodø/Glimt. On Saturday, he broke his Serie A duck and did everything in his power to deliver another win over undefeated Roma.

McTominay’s three-strikes-in-one had made it 2-0 in the 48th minute, building on Frank Anguissa’s glorious overhead opener. Perhaps McTominay’s willingness to attempt the audacious has been rubbing off on his Napoli teammates too, though the Cameroon international was a title-winner and essential figure before the Scot arrived in 2024.

They could have been further ahead on Saturday. Mathías Olivera had a point-blank effort hacked off the line when the score was 1-0 before half-time. Then again, Bryan Cristante hit the post for Roma moments later.

McTominay’s goal appeared to put Napoli fully in control, until Matías Soulé swapped a one-two with Donyell Malen and picked out the bottom corner brilliantly from outside the box. That was the 69th minute, and Roma were level by the 74th, their centre-back Leonardo Balerdi crashing home from a corner.

And so McTominay answered again, with a wicked finish of his own from almost 30 yards. He matched Soulé’s precision after Roma had failed to clear their lines from a Leonardo Spinazzola attempt, tucking his shot inside the near post.

Against lesser opponents, that might have been the end of it. But Roma are not easily beaten under Gian Piero Gasperini. In the 89th minute, Wesley crossed and Balerdi arrived to head home another equaliser.

It finished 3-3, a marvellously entertaining game, with so many stories to tell. On the Roma side, this felt like a big night for Soulé, the 23-year-old Argentina forward delivering one of the statement performances of his Serie A career to date in a stadium named after Diego Maradona.

Signed from Frosinone in the summer of 2024, Soulé has shown glimpses of immense potential but struggled for consistency, leading to questions about whether he can truly become more than Paulo Dybala’s understudy. But this season, and in the last month especially – which began with a first senior appearance for Argentina, then two assists in a heart-breaking Champions League defeat to Real Madrid – he is stepping ever more confidently from his compatriot’s shadow.

“He’s in his prime, he’s the driving force of this team,” said Gasperini at full-time. “[Donyell] Malen didn’t have a great game, and [Soulé] carried the team. His goal was something crazy; the foot and leg speed reminds you of something [Leo] Messi would do.”

And then there is Balerdi, also Argentinian, on loan from Marseille with an option to buy. Gasperini noted how one of Roma’s strategies in the transfer market had been to target players perceived as leaders for their previous clubs. He named Marten de Roon in this category, too, and it was the Dutchman, wearing the captain’s armband, who put in the cross that led to Balerdi’s first goal.

Unsurprisingly, given how the match unfolded, Gasperini seemed content with the draw. Allegri rather less so. He was sent off after Roma’s third goal and though he claimed not to know why, he was later overheard outside the changing rooms insinuating that the opposition had received favourable treatment from the referee. La Gazzetta dello Sport reported him protesting that: “Last year they handed you [Roma] the Champions League and I paid for it at Milan.”

Without relitigating a year’s worth of decisions, this was a bold line to take. Milan, who spent almost the entire campaign in the top four and much of it as the closest contender to Inter for the scudetto, contrived to miss out on the Champions League by a single point after collecting only seven from their final eight games.

Allegri cut short an interview on Dazn, saying that he was “for the first time in 25 years, not lucid enough to expand on this game”. Another claim that might raise a few eyebrows, from a man who once got halfway to undressing in frustration at the officiating of a final that his own team won.

Perhaps his ire on this occasion would have been better directed at one of his own defenders. During the buildup to Roma’s final goal, Sam Beukema collapsed to the floor after tussling with Balerdi off the ball. A generous reading of the replays would be to say that he “made the most” of some contact, which appeared to be arm-on-arm, though the Napoli player went down clutching his rib cage.

If Beukema had stayed on his feet and continued marking Balerdi, perhaps the equaliser could have been avoided. Instead, he got up too late to do anything about it and then fell back to the floor again after watching the goal go in.

An undignified end to a brilliant game. As frustrated as Napoli might feel at throwing these points away, there were lots of positives here to hang on to. Only a week ago we were asking whether Kevin De Bruyne could be persuaded to occupy the deeper role Allegri sees for him and make space for McTominay further forward. In this game, and the win over Bodø/Glimt, there were positive signs.

Putting three goals past this Roma team – who had only conceded that many in the previous seven games of this Serie A season combined – is no mean feat. Napoli with McTominay are more than capable of improving on their current 10th place in the table and at least competing for a top-four spot.

The challenge is to stay lucid, as McTominay did on his triple goal, sticking at a task until it is completed. And perhaps a little longer to make sure.

The Manchester United manager cycle is the same, but the direction of fury is starkly different, as Henry Winter writes from one of the most extraordinary ends to a match in Old Trafford history

“You can stick your new stadium up your a*se.”

Of the many songs of defiance voiced by the Stretford End yesterday this was another strong railing against the club’s priorities. As one Manchester United season-ticket holder messaged me last night, “We had an email from the club this week asking us for our thoughts on the new stadium and I bet they get some interesting replies. Surely the team is the first priority.” Spot on.

The club will point to differing budgets, stadium and squad, but it is reflective of owners’ priorities. The gleaming new stadium is impressive, will accommodate more fans and generate more money but it is not the priority right now, and the consultation is particularly poorly timed. Why celebrate proposals for the construction of a new Theatre of Dreams, an arena not opening until 2030/31, when the current stage lacks performers?

United’s squad is one of the poorest in the club’s history in strength of match-winning quality and character. Where are the fighters, the Crerands and Robsons, Keanes and Rooneys? The captain Bruno Fernandes criticised Michael Carrick’s tactics. He’s the captain, supposedly the leader of a dressing-room of a club called and celebrated as Manchester United. Together. United.

Failing in loyalty to the shirt, too few of the current group are playing for Carrick, whose days looked numbered in the wake of surrenders to Como and Bournemouth. The priority for the owners should have been ensuring that Carrick had the right players to call upon: right in their positional fit, attitude and ability. The board’s priorities were wrong: they focused on midfield, not defence. They focused on mid-level players like Carlos Baleba, Andrey Santos and Youri Tielemans for reasonable fees, low-hanging fruit, not reaching up for the real talent. In years gone by, when United were well run, their priority would have been in throwing resources at bringing in a commanding midfielder in Elliot Anderson from Nottingham Forest as they once did with Keane.

United’s priority should be to think big again, and invest big. This is Manchester United, the most famous club in the world along with Real Madrid. As the fans’ flag reads, they’re hated, adored, but never ignored. Big club, big news, but big problems.

The saddest part of the shambles is that those who care most about United, Carrick and the fans, are the ones suffering most, the ones most let down by the club and the players. After chanting against co-owners Sir Jim Ratcliffe and the Glazer family, the Stretford End sang in praise of Carrick. The midfielder who won five titles and a Champions League at the club first felt embedded in the hearts as well as minds of United fans on February 23, 2013 at Loftus Road. The away fans sang about Carrick, for almost an hour, during the second half and when filing out. “It’s Carrick, you know; it’s hard to believe it’s not Scholes”. It was the ultimate compliment, comparing Carrick to a beloved, homegrown legend in Paul Scholes. Carrick later described the moment as emotional and unforgettable, hearing that unconditional acceptance.

He heard it again yesterday, and looked similarly emotional at this show of support in his darkest moment. United fans were making a point. Carrick was not to blame. Greater culpability could be laid at the boardroom door. Ratcliffe overseeing such wretched recruitment. The Glazers appearing to focus on what they can take out of United not what they could put. It’s also the players.

In one of the most extraordinary moments in United’s history, the Stretford End serenaded Carrick en route to the tunnel after the final whistle sounded like the death knell on his managerial tenure. Then, when he disappeared from view, they tore into the players.

That felt a hugely significant moment and one that should force some United players, such as Fernandes, to look in the mirror. United fans are famously supportive of players, so their anger must have sprung up from the deepest source, from deep within.

Social media immediately sluiced with observers, many from overseas, lecturing United’s match-going fans on the need to take more direction against the owners, such as boycotting games. Where to start with such naive admonishing? Giving up their tickets – or missing a few games, which allows the club to take the ticket – would simply profit the Glazers even more. More tourists spending inflated sums on tickets, and spending substantial sums on merchandising in the megastore, just means more money for the Glazers.

Giving up their tickets, even scanning in but not taking up the seat, is anathema anyway to fans. Why should they be told to stage a walk-out in protest at the Glazers when all they want to do is watch their team and share moments and memories with friends forged in the furnace of match-day? They work hard to earn the money to support their team. Why on earth should they give that up?

Supporting a team is largely an unconditional relationship. Fans can’t change allegiance. It’s not like a relationship with a car manufacturer or supermarket chain, a connection you can break if you feel they’re letting you down on price, quality, service, level of satisfaction, etc. It’s an emotional connection, and United fans deserve admiration for their loyalty despite the owners and the players. Carrick will probably go, leaving the fans to face yet another grim cycle of life under owners and players whose priorities simply aren’t right for a club of Manchester United’s stature.

Subaru is one of the few major automakers, alongside Porsche, still widely using boxer engines. This type of engine provides smoother operation, as opposing pistons move in opposite directions to help cancel out vibrations, and a lower center of gravity, which can reduce body roll and improve handling.

This year marks the 60th anniversary of Subaru’s first boxer engine, which was introduced in the Subaru 1000 in 1966. To commemorate the milestone, the automaker is offering a special-edition BRZ limited to just 66 examples. The catch, however, is that it will only be available in China, though the special edition still shines a spotlight on the brand’s boxer engine, which is set to disappear from its European lineup by 2028 due to stricter emissions and other regulations.

The BRZ 60th Anniversary Edition isn’t the first Subaru to celebrate the milestone. Subaru China also introduced 60th Anniversary Edition packages for the Crosstrek and Forester earlier this year.

According to China’s Autohome, the special-edition BRZ gets 60th-anniversary graphics, complemented by exclusive gray paint and STI front-fender accents. An STI flexible V-shaped strut brace is also added to improve steering response and chassis behavior.

Safety is likewise improved with a new wide-angle monocular camera for the EyeSight system, which enhances detection of pedestrians and cyclists, particularly at low-speed intersections. The U.S.-bound BRZ also received a new wide-angle mono camera for the 2027 model year, along with added rear parking sensors for the tS model.

The manual version receives a Sport mode, which improves throttle responsiveness. Speaking of which, the engine remains unchanged, with the 60th Anniversary Edition retaining the 2.4-liter naturally aspirated boxer-four. It’s the same engine found in the Toyota GR86 and makes approximately 231 horsepower in China, compared to 228 horsepower in the U.S.

The BRZ 60th Anniversary Edition with the stick shift is priced at 299,800 yuan (about $44,950 at current exchange rates), while the automatic-equipped version costs 309,800 yuan (approximately $46,450). In the U.S., the regular 2027 BRZ starts at $36,140, excluding destination charges.

It is unclear whether Subaru will bring an equivalent 60th Anniversary Edition to the U.S. market. But for those looking for exclusivity, perhaps the 2026 BRZ Series.Yellow is the closest alternative. #420. A 2,700-year-old solar eclipse just revealed something surprising about Earth

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

An international research team has revisited one of the oldest precisely dated records of a total solar eclipse, uncovering new details about how Earth's rotation has changed over thousands of years. By combining historical geography with modern astronomical calculations, the scientists reconstructed an eclipse that occurred in 709 BCE and determined how it would have appeared from Qufu, the ancient capital of China's Lu Duchy.

The researchers also examined an early account that may describe the solar corona, the Sun's faint outer atmosphere that becomes visible to the naked eye during a total solar eclipse. Their analysis of its possible shape suggests that the Sun was returning to a more active phase following an extended period of unusually low activity.

Published in Astrophysical Journal Letters, the findings provide more precise evidence of Earth's rotation speed in ancient times. They also offer potential independent support for previous reconstructions of solar activity based on radiocarbon measurements.

An Ancient Chinese Eclipse Record Holds a Crucial Clue

On July 17, 709 BCE, observers at the court of the Lu Duchy witnessed a total solar eclipse. The event was preserved in the "Spring and Autumn Annals," a historical chronicle compiled approximately 200 to 300 years later. The original account stated that "the Sun was totally eclipsed."

An additional description appears in the "Hanshu" (Book of Han), which preserves a passage written about seven centuries after the eclipse. This later account contains an unusual observation that may refer to the Sun's corona.

"What makes this record special isn't just its age, but also a later addendum in the 'Hanshu' (Book of Han) based on a quote written seven centuries after the eclipse. It describes the eclipsed Sun as 'completely yellow above and below.' This addendum has been traditionally associated with a record of a solar corona. If this is truly the case, it represents one of the earliest surviving written descriptions of the solar corona," explained lead author Hisashi Hayakawa, an assistant professor at Nagoya University's Institute for Space-Earth Environmental Research and Institute for Advanced Research.

Initially, however, the historical account appeared to conflict with modern astronomy. When the team calculated the eclipse's path using existing estimates of Earth's ancient rotation, their results indicated that a total eclipse should not have been visible from the previously assumed location of the Lu court in Qufu.

The researchers traced the discrepancy to a mistake in the city's geographic coordinates. By examining archaeological excavation reports and historical geographic evidence, they determined that earlier calculations had placed ancient Qufu approximately eight kilometers from its actual location.

Correcting this relatively small error made it possible to reconstruct the eclipse more accurately and extract valuable information about Earth's rotation and the Sun's appearance.

"This correction allowed us to accurately measure the Earth's rotation during the total eclipse, calculate the orientation of the Sun's rotation axis, and simulate the corona's appearance," explained Hayakawa, who holds doctorates in solar physics and oriental history.

Why Ancient China Preserved Such Detailed Astronomical Records

The survival of this remarkable observation reflects the importance of astronomy in ancient Chinese society. Successive dynasties employed specialists to monitor celestial events because unusual phenomena in the sky were often interpreted as omens.

This practice, known as omenology, was closely connected to political beliefs. Eclipses, auroras, and other extraordinary astronomical events were sometimes considered warnings that an emperor had acted improperly.

As a result, court astronomers carefully documented changes in the sky. The records maintained across successive dynasties eventually became some of the world's most valuable historical sources for studying ancient eclipses.

Even so, the researchers emphasize an important distinction between the original eclipse observation and the later description of the corona.

The 709 BCE event is regarded as the earliest explicit written record of a total solar eclipse that can be securely dated. It may also be associated with one of the oldest surviving descriptions of the solar corona, but that interpretation remains uncertain because the relevant passage appears in a source written centuries after the event.

Although the corona account requires caution, the established timing of the eclipse provides a reliable basis for investigating Earth's historical rotation. The possible corona description also offers a valuable comparison with other studies of ancient solar activity.

How a 2,700-Year-Old Eclipse Reveals Earth's Faster Rotation

Earth does not rotate at a perfectly constant rate. Over long periods, its spin has gradually slowed, partly because of friction associated with ocean tides generated by the Moon's gravitational pull.

Consequently, Earth rotated slightly faster 2,700 years ago than it does today.

Using the revised position of ancient Qufu, the researchers obtained improved constraints on Earth's rotation during the period spanning the eighth through sixth centuries BCE.

Their calculations placed delta T (ΔT), a measurement used to describe variations in Earth's rotation relative to a uniform time scale, between 20,264 and 21,204 seconds for the eclipse of 709 BCE.

"This new dataset fixes coordinate errors in previous Earth rotation studies. Additionally, it improves the accuracy of dating and reconstructing historical astronomical events," said coauthor Mitsuru Sôma of the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan.

These measurements help scientists better understand changes in the length of Earth's day over thousands of years. They can also improve calculations of when and where other ancient astronomical events would have been visible.

Evidence That the Sun Was Awakening From a Long Quiet Period

Beyond revealing Earth's rotational history, the eclipse may provide evidence of an important transition in the Sun's activity.

Scientists have previously reconstructed ancient solar cycles by studying radiocarbon preserved in tree rings. The possible corona description from 709 BCE offers another way to examine those findings.

"This unique historical addendum for the possible solar coronal structure is critical for providing a spot reference on solar activity reconstructions from tree rings and ice cores, as well as providing independent validation of solar activity models," explained coauthor Mathew Owens, a professor of space physics at the University of Reading.

Trees absorb carbon from the atmosphere during photosynthesis, including small amounts of radioactive carbon known as radiocarbon. Some of this material becomes locked into annual growth rings, creating a natural record that researchers can examine thousands of years later.

Radiocarbon levels are influenced by cosmic rays reaching Earth's atmosphere. When the Sun becomes more active, its magnetic influence generally reduces the number of cosmic rays that reach Earth, affecting radiocarbon production.

By measuring changes in radiocarbon concentrations across successive tree rings, scientists can reconstruct how solar activity varied long before modern instruments existed.

The Sun normally follows an activity cycle lasting approximately 11 years, during which the number of sunspots and the strength of solar magnetic activity rise and fall.

Occasionally, however, the Sun enters much longer periods of reduced activity called "grand minima." During these intervals, sunspots become exceptionally rare, and the usual cycle is much less pronounced.

One such interval, known as the "Neo Assyrian Grand Minimum" or "Homer Grand Minimum," lasted from approximately 808 to 717 BCE.

The eclipse of 709 BCE took place shortly after this prolonged quiet period ended.

According to the researchers, the possible description of the solar corona is consistent with a Sun that had resumed its regular activity cycles. Its apparent structure suggests substantial magnetic activity and indicates that the Sun may have been near the peak of its approximately 11-year cycle at the time.

This interpretation agrees with earlier solar activity reconstructions derived from radiocarbon measurements in tree rings, although the uncertainty surrounding the historical corona description means it cannot be considered definitive proof.

Ancient Astronomers Are Still Helping Scientists Understand the Universe

The findings demonstrate how observations preserved for thousands of years can continue to contribute to modern scientific discoveries.

By bringing together historical documents, archaeological evidence, geographic research, and sophisticated astronomical models, scientists can recover information that would otherwise be impossible to measure directly.

In this case, a correction of just eight kilometers helped transform an ancient eclipse record into a more precise source of information about Earth's rotation and a potential window into the Sun's distant past.

"Some of our ancestors were very skilled observers," noted coauthor Dr. Meng Jin of the Lockheed Martin Solar and Astrophysics Laboratory. "When we combine their careful records with modern computational methods and historical evidence, we can potentially find new information about our planet and our star from thousands of years ago."

The water was rising rapidly. It had already reached Cayni Ali’s waist. Her home, five metres from the flooded River Juba in southern Somalia, never stood a chance. There was nowhere to go. “The airstrikes are everywhere; it is difficult to move. So many people have been killed.”

Their testimony offers a rare glimpse of life inside the stronghold of al-Shabaab, the Islamist militants who control 30% of Somalia. Closed off from the outside world, residents describe a city dealing with significant civilian casualties. Bodies, some said, lay in the streets.

The ongoing conflict is a legally ambiguous, shadowy war coordinated by the United States in east Africa, in which there is little accountability for harm to Somali civilians.

The United States Africa Command (Africom) would not share “operational security” details of a seemingly accelerating campaign that has also raised questions over potential covert US strikes. But there is, perhaps, an even darker dimension to the offensive on al-Shabaab’s de facto capital.

Sources say the military strategy appears to be seeking to drown those living in al-Shabaab territory by “weaponising” a rapidly strengthening El Niño, forecast to intensify Somalia’s rainy season from this month.

“The airstrikes are forcing people to go close to the river. It’s an extremely aggressive offensive, exploiting El Niño as a weapon of war to push the population into flood-prone areas,” said a source monitoring the Juba floodplain in southern Somalia.

Pinning the population – which includes large numbers of women and children – on the river’s floodplain has raised concerns of large-scale deaths in the months ahead.

“The official line is that they are targeting military positions and al-Shabaab, but because al-Shabaab is embedded within the community, it’s difficult to avoid collateral damage,” added the source.

When El Niño last struck Somalia in 2023, it caused the worst floods seen in the country for a century, displacing more than 1.2 million people. Analysts have questioned the ethics of the US military allegedly using mapping data from flood-prone areas – conceived to mitigate civilian casualties – to inflict harm.

Jethro Norman, a senior researcher with the Danish Institute for International Studies, said: “A weaponisation of El Niño is a reversal of process – using humanitarian remote sensing to inflict casualties rather than to mitigate harm. You capture the cumulative impact of a sustained airstrikes campaign in an El Niño flooding context.”

US officials deny exploiting climatic factors. A spokesperson said: “Africom does not target civilians, conduct operations for the purpose of displacing civilian populations or use weather or flooding as a method of warfare. Africom targets lawful military objectives and takes feasible measures to mitigate civilian harm.”

Nevertheless, many wonder if the opportunity to weaken al-Shabaab using the weather was too tempting to ignore, offering a catastrophic drowning event blamed on a supercharged El Niño.

Like many cities, Jilib’s location has been found wanting in an era of extreme weather. On a low-lying plain, the city is chronically vulnerable to flooding. In 2023, vast swathes of farmland were submerged. US defence officials will know the early indicators of El Niño’s impact in al-Shabaab’s heartland. UN agencies say the Juba River basin is of “particular concern”. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), meanwhile, foresees “severe flooding”.

Attacking Jilib using ground forces carries much risk. Airstrikes can yield triumphs such as the death of a senior al-Shabaab commander on 1 October, but are unable to overrun a city of 100,000.
