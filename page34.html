Election officials reported record turnout as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s far-right coalition faced an opposition bloc led by a former military chief of staff, Gadi Eisenkot. A winner might not be immediately clear.

Aaron BoxermanDavid M. HalbfingerLeo Sands and Max Bearak

Polls have closed in an election that has seen Israelis vote in record numbers. In what is widely seen as the most consequential election in the country’s history, voters were deciding whether to keep Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and his far-right coalition in power or chart a new course.

By 8 p.m. local time (2 p.m. Eastern), two hours before polls were scheduled to close, 74.3 percent of eligible voters had cast ballots, the highest rate for that point in the day since 1988.

Turnout appeared to be particularly high among Arab citizens, who make up about a fifth of Israel’s population. By late afternoon, hours before the polls were set to close, turnout in some Arab-majority towns had exceeded the totals from the last election, in 2022.

Responding to the high turnout in those towns, Mr. Netanyahu accused Arab citizens of “trying to steal the elections,” repeatedly using brazen and overtly racial appeals to push his right-wing base to vote. An attempt by his Likud party on Tuesday to have voting hours extended was rejected by the Central Election Committee.

The election is Israel’s first since the Hamas-led attacks on Oct. 7, 2023, which was the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust and ignited three years of war that left Gaza largely in ruins and nearly all its population displaced.

Voters have a choice between two blocs offering starkly different visions. Mr. Netanyahu, the leader of the most far-right government in Israeli history, built a coalition of nationalists and ultra-Orthodox Jews, which faces an ideologically diverse group of Jewish opposition parties loosely led by Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff whose son was killed in the Gaza war. Parties representing Arab citizens of Israel also staunchly oppose Mr. Netanyahu’s government.

Both sides are framing the election as a make-or-break moment for the country. Mr. Netanyahu’s coalition offers the promise of a more religious Israel with an ever-tighter grip over the West Bank and Gaza. His opponents are casting the vote as the last chance to preserve democracy and pull Israel back from the brink of becoming a pariah state under international sanctions.

Voting will close at 10 p.m. (4 p.m. Eastern time), but there is unlikely to be an immediate determination of who will be the next prime minister. Opinion polls suggest that Mr. Netanyahu’s allies and rivals are roughly tied, and assembling a governing coalition in Israel’s parliamentary system could take weeks or months.

Opposition leaders led by Mr. Eisenkot have called for a reckoning over what they call Mr. Netanyahu’s failure to prevent the catastrophic Oct. 7 attacks. They also have pledged to repair Israel’s shattered image abroad, including by cracking down on Israeli extremist violence against Palestinians in the occupied West Bank.

Mr. Netanyahu has resisted the establishment of an independent commission to investigate the Oct. 7 attacks. He has vowed to move ahead with overhauling Israel’s judicial system, a move that critics say could undermine the country’s liberal democracy. And his allies say they hope to permanently entrench Israeli rule in the West Bank and Gaza despite international condemnation.

The 77-year-old leader has dominated Israeli politics for decades. In recent days, he repeatedly suggested that the country could face annihilation without him in charge.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s governing coalition has the distinction of surviving for a full four-year term in office, something no government in Israel has achieved for more than 30 years. Since first taking office in 1996, he has tallied 19 years in power and become Israel’s longest-serving prime minister by far.

On Tuesday, he is seeking a seventh term, in Israel’s first parliamentary election since the deadly Hamas-led attack in 2023 that prompted its devastating war in Gaza.

The longevity of his most recent governing coalition followed three years during which a cycle of collapsed coalitions and five snap elections had left the country without a functional government or national budget.

In 2021, Israelis ousted Mr. Netanyahu from power and then returned him to office 18 months later. He had staged a previous comeback, in 2009, becoming prime minister a decade after his party was voted out of power.

In 2021, Mr. Netanyahu’s government collapsed after failing to pass a budget, setting off legislative elections. At the time, he had been indicted on charges of fraud, bribery and breach of trust, and the coalition he was leading was shaky. According to analysts, heading into an election was a gambit by Mr. Netanyahu to strengthen his grip on power before his criminal trial.

But the electorate that voted that March didn’t give him the seats he needed, ending a period of 12 straight years in office for him. It took months of wrangling for an eclectic coalition of anti-Netanyahu forces to cobble together a single-seat majority in the Parliament. Naftali Bennett, a right-wing former ally of Mr. Netanyahu’s, led the coalition as prime minister, hammering out a deal with Yair Lapid, a centrist and former broadcaster who was appointed foreign minister.

The eight-party coalition proved too unwieldy to endure. After two right-wing lawmakers defected and several on the left rebelled in critical votes, that government collapsed in June 2022.

After the resulting elections, held in November 2022, Mr. Netanyahu returned to power as right-wing voters moved to more extreme parties, diminishing the center left.

Mr. Netanyahu’s Likud party allied with far-right and ultra-Orthodox lawmakers to secure a clear majority in the Parliament, forming the most hard-line and religious conservative government in Israel’s history.

The coalition accounted for 48.4 percent of the vote but had a parliamentary majority because two anti-Netanyahu parties — one leftist, one Arab nationalist — fell just short of the share of votes needed to obtain seats.

It was a reminder of how tiny swings among voters can have a big effect under Israel’s parliamentary system.

Mr. Netanyahu has since overseen an acceleration of the country’s rightward shift, including an effort to overhaul the judiciary that prompted huge street protests. Questions about whether his government could have better pre-empted the Oct. 7 attack have also become hot-button issues.

In April, Mr. Lapid and Mr. Bennett said they would reconstitute their centrist alliance of 2021 for the 2026 election. Mr. Netanyahu will also face Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief of staff who leads the largest opposition party.

When polls close in about half an hour, a frantic, low-tech counting process will begin. At each of roughly 12,000 polling stations, party representatives will begin counting ballot slips by hand, overseen by observers wearing body cameras. The paper tallies will then be transported to regional hubs, where the results will be recorded electronically. Only then, hours after polls close, will the authorities begin releasing results. The process is not expected to be completed before Thursday afternoon.

Days before Tuesday’s pivotal Israeli election, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu shocked his country by offering a bleak economic forecast that critics said could make Israel more like Iran, Russia and North Korea.

Mr. Netanyahu told an Israeli financial journalist on Friday that he was preparing for international sanctions against Israeli banks by drawing up plans for a “bypass economy” based on cryptocurrency. He also told Israelis to prepare for the end of billions of dollars of annual American assistance.

“It’s over,” he said. “It’s only a matter of time.”

The comments were a reflection of Israel’s deepening global isolation, with even once-staunch allies imposing or threatening economic sanctions and embargoes after years of war in Gaza and rampant violence by Jewish settlers against Palestinians in the occupied West Bank.

With settlers advancing into areas that were previously off limits, Britain last month decried what it called “ethnic cleansing” and announced trade sanctions on settlements that it said would also apply to entities that financed their construction. Though Israel’s banking system has not been directly targeted by sanctions, a dozen countries, including Canada, France and Spain, have said they support restrictions on trade with settlements, raising the prospect of further penalties that could affect Israeli financial institutions.

Opponents of Mr. Netanyahu’s government decried his pronouncements, which also drew a rebuke from the generally reserved central bank.

“Crypto currencies cannot serve as a route to bypass sanctions against Israel,” the Bank of Israel said in a statement. The bank added that the government would do better “to focus on preventing sanctions” and preserving Israel’s economic ties with its main trading partners.

Commentators said that Mr. Netanyahu’s outlook sounded apocalyptic, and some said it was part of a scare campaign to improve his election prospects.

Last year, amid growing global condemnation of the Israeli military’s conduct of the war against Hamas in Gaza, where it has been accused of war crimes, and the imposition of some arms embargoes, he warned that Israel would have to become a more self-sufficient “super-Sparta.” That reference to the ancient, insular Greek city-state that practiced militaristic self-reliance was, to critics, a frightening scenario for a small country whose economy is largely driven by a tech sector that is reliant on foreign investment.

Gadi Eisenkot, Mr. Netanyahu’s main rival in Tuesday’s election, said in a social media post that the prime minister’s “Bibi-coin vision,” a nod to cryptocurrency, would “dismantle the Israeli economy for generations.”

“No internet purchases, no high-tech investments, no national security,” he added.

Yair Lapid, the opposition leader who has served as prime minister and finance minister, wrote on social media that a vote for Mr. Netanyahu was a vote for “a ruined economy, sanctions that will lead to insane price hikes, and no military alliance with the United States.”

And a former governor of the Bank of Israel, Jacob Frenkel, told Israel’s Channel 12 on Sunday that moving away from the international financial system would turn Israel into “a leper.”

Mr. Netanyahu later walked back his remarks slightly, saying that he had heard the criticism and that cryptocurrency was only one option to deal with potential financial sanctions.

In a somewhat jarring contrast, Mr. Netanyahu’s campaign has argued that Israel’s economy overall is healthy despite years of war, helped by record export revenues, relatively low inflation and unemployment, and a strong shekel.

But many Israelis are not feeling it. The State Comptroller, a government watchdog, warned last year that the cost of living was “skyrocketing” in part because of structural challenges in Israel’s consumer market and low competition levels.

While foreign affairs and national security have dominated the political conversation ahead of the election, a recent survey by the Israel Democracy Institute, a nonpartisan research group, found that the economy was the top issue for many voters heading into Tuesday.

Both Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc and the coalition of opposition parties are making a final push to urge Israelis to vote. Long waits at crowded polling places have been reported across the country, but the election committee has said anyone who is still in line at 10 p.m. will be able to vote.

The stakes of the Israeli elections are high for Palestinians in the West Bank, but they do not have the right to vote in them.

In interviews, some Palestinians voiced objections to not being allowed to vote, especially as they felt Israel’s occupation of the West Bank was becoming more permanent.

“The Israeli government has enormous influence over our lives,” said Montaser Amro, 32, a supermarket owner in Majd, a village near the southern city of Hebron. “It chooses when roadblocks open and close, it controls the borders, and it decides on how much of our economy functions.”

He added, “Why can’t we vote when settlers living hundreds of meters away can?”

Israel captured the West Bank in 1967 and reached an agreement with the Palestine Liberation Organization to establish the Palestinian Authority in the 1990s. The Palestinian Authority administers parts of the West Bank and has not held parliamentary or presidential elections for roughly 20 years.

Many Palestinians have long hoped the Palestinian Authority would transform into an independent Palestinian state. In recent years, those hopes have dwindled. The Israeli government promotes the expansion of settlements in the West Bank that are widely considered illegal under international law and rejects Palestinian self-determination there and in Gaza. Palestinian officials have also failed to unify their ranks and renew their leadership.

Alaa al-Khabass, 40, a resident of Hebron, said it was unjust that Palestinians could not cast ballots.

“Israel controls just about everything related to us, and I don’t see that changing any time soon,” he said. “We should be able to vote, but we live in painful reality where we lack rights and sovereignty.”

With just an hour still to go before Israel’s polls close, turnout has already surpassed the total turnout in the last several elections. Israel’s central elections committee said that 74.3 percent of eligible voters had cast a ballot by 8 p.m., the highest rate for that time since 1988. The polls are set to close at 10 p.m. local time, and Israelis are already tensely awaiting exit surveys which will finally give them a sense of who might soon be in charge of their country.

The election in Israel on Tuesday is unlikely to yield quick answers about who might lead the next government.

Voters don’t directly pick a prime minister in Israel’s proportional parliamentary system. They choose a party or slate, the first step in determining who may be selected to try to form a government. The process is not guaranteed to succeed and can lead to another election if it does not. Here are four things to watch for.

This is considered one of the most consequential elections in Israel’s history, and voters turned out in record numbers.

Recent polls had suggested that voter turnout could be at least as high or higher than in the last general election, in 2022. That year, slightly more than 70 percent of eligible voters — nearly 4.8 million out of nearly 6.8 million people — participated. That election followed four other votes in quick succession from 2019 to 2021 in which turnout hovered near 70 percent.

This year, there are 7.3 million eligible voters, about 540,000 more than in 2022.

Parties must clear a threshold of 3.25 percent of the total vote in order to get a seat in the Knesset, or Parliament.

But no party is expected to gain a majority of 61 parliamentary seats, according to the polls. That means there will likely be lots of post-election negotiations among those parties who pass the threshold.

Parties or lists that do not reach the threshold lose those ballots. As a result, parties and candidates lobby voters not to waste their votes on a list that seems unlikely to meet this criterion.

At least one party has also faced repeated attacks from Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud party and the far-right Religious Zionism party led by Bezalel Smotrich, Israel’s finance minister.

The targeted party, People of Israel, is led by Ofer Winter, a former Israeli brigadier general with hawkish views on national security. Some polls show that much of Mr. Winter’s support comes from voters who would normally choose Likud. Mr. Winter has faced claims that a vote for his party will be wasted or may end up going to the leading challengers.

While Mr. Winter initially said that he would support Mr. Netanyahu, he has refused any deals to withdraw from the race and has since signaled the pledge may have been premature.

Israeli election officials have said there is no evidence of threats to the integrity of this election in terms of widespread fraud or vote counting irregularities. But experts are concerned about disinformation campaigns undermining the vote or thwarting turnout.

Critics fear that some moves by the governing right-wing coalition may cast doubt on the outcome even before the vote happens or may thwart turnout among some voters.

Absentee voting is mostly prohibited in Israel. But by some estimates, tens of thousands of Israelis living abroad were expected to fly in to vote, a factor that could potentially swing the outcome.

Likud had filed a lawsuit challenging an initiative to fly in Israeli voters from abroad — a move widely perceived as an effort to limit opposition voters from showing up. The party had said the nonpartisan program provided benefits that could sway voters in violation of Israeli law. But it withdrew the suit this month after the initiative’s organizers agreed to limit flights and not to defray travelers’ costs.

Likud and other right-wing parties also filed motions to limit participation of several Arab candidates in the election.

The Central Elections Committee can vote to bar candidates or lists on certain grounds, such as expressing support for armed conflict against Israel. But the nation’s Supreme Court gets the final say. This month, it rejected a challenge to the Joint List, a merger of three majority-Arab parties, and to Raam, a historically Islamist party led by Mansour Abbas, who is running with Yoav Segalovitz, a former deputy minister of national security who was previously in the centrist Zionist party There Is a Future.

In 2022, turnout in five of the largest Arab localities in Israel was lower, sometimes significantly, than the national figures. This year, officials in Arab communities worked to get voters to the polls in larger numbers and prepared for potential resistance.

Likud and the Religious Zionism party announced plans this month to deploy thousands of religious Jewish “observers” to “prevent fraud in voting and vote-counting in Arab communities.”

Critics contend that the presence of such observers, likely from far-right Zionist parties, was intended to intimidate Arab citizens of Israel, who make up 20 percent of the population and could be critical to the election’s outcome.

“The main issue we want to avoid is ‘fake news,’” said Abed Shahada, who leads voter turnout efforts for an organization representing mayors and other officials in Arab localities.

One major issue expected to drive voters is high crime in Arab communities. But if there are reports of or disinformation about criminal or violent episodes on Election Day that could keep people from the polls, Mr. Shahada said, teams are preparing by setting up a system to share verified information.

The Central Election Committee said it would not extend voting hours past the scheduled closing time. Earlier in the day, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud party said it had asked the committee for an extension, even as Netanyahu called on his supporters to head to the polls. His chief opponent, Gadi Eisenkot, was opposed to extending the hours. Less than two hours remain before polls close.

Turnout data may help explain why Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is worried about the pace of voting. With just over three hours until polls close, turnout in the five towns where his Likud party won its strongest support in 2022 was below the national rate by up to 10 percentage points.

Mr. Netanyahu has a long track record of appealing to his supporters by invoking the specter of the Arab vote. Arab citizens represent about 20 percent of Israel’s population, but have long complained that they are treated as second-class citizens and subject to discrimination.

Mr. Netanyahu was accused of race-baiting and was forced to issue an apology for the remark.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI Agents Are Creating a New Layer of Cybersecurity Spending

AI-focused cloud service provider Akamai launched a sandbox for AI agents on Tuesday, a sealed environment where code an agent writes can run without touching the rest of a company's systems. Agent Sandbox is in beta, alongside Akamai Inference, a service for running open AI models on Akamai's cloud.

The launch may be small but the problem behind it is not. Software is no longer just answering questions—it logs into accounts, writes code, moves money and acts on its own, and every one of those powers is something a hacker, or the software itself, can abuse.

This is what’s known as agentic AI, and it’s taking over the internet. A 2026 Palo Alto Networks survey of 2,930 security leaders found an average of 109 machine identities for every human, the logins and keys software uses to access other software. Of those, 79 are AI agents.

Agentic AI comes with a lot of perks, but also some serious dangers, especially when those agents have too much freedom, or are so intelligent they can bypass their own restrictions.

For example, Hugging Face, a major hub for open AI models, disclosed an intrusion on July 16, and OpenAI confirmed five days later that its own models were responsible. The research group METR later investigated the incident and found about 1,200 OpenAI agents had coordinated on a hidden message board, with roughly 700 joining the attack.

Turns out the agents escaped their sandbox and chained a previously unknown software flaw with stolen credentials to reach live systems. OpenAI called it a "warning shot." It said a monitor that reads a model's written reasoning was not running during the tests.

In simple terms, AI safety usually ends up being a tug of war game between alignment (the guys in charge of making sure AI is safe) and development (the guys in charge of making sure AI is powerful), with each team wanting the other to play a minor role in order to secure its own goals.

That is what Akamai wants to treat. “Enterprises should not have to choose between speed and control when they put AI into production,” Jon Alexander, Senior Vice President of Cloud Technology at Akamai said in a press release.

“Some teams want to ship an AI feature this quarter on a managed platform. Others need dedicated GPUs to run frontier-class open models on their own terms. Every team running agents needs a boundary around the code those agents write,” he said.

Hijacking is another fear. In July, Zscaler found web pages hiding instructions meant for AI agents, a trick called indirect prompt injection. One fake documentation page told agents to pay a $3 "license fee" in about 0.0012 Ethereum to a hardcoded wallet, and four of the 26 models Zscaler tested complied.

Back in May cybersecurity firm Bugcrowd saw submissions more than quadruple in three weeks in March, most of them fake. HackerOne and Nextcloud suspended paid programs in April.

The machines also find real bugs. Mozilla said an early version of Anthropic's Claude Mythos helped find 271 vulnerabilities in Firefox, all patched within the week. That flood of real and fake findings is what security companies are now racing to manage.

Palo Alto closed its CyberArk purchase on Feb. 11 to extend privileged-access controls to AI agents. In April it bought Koi, which secures AI coding agents running on employee machines. Cisco announced a planned acquisition of Astrix Security in May, a firm that governs the keys and tokens agents use, and completed the deal June 29.

Akamai's piece is the fence around the code itself. Its sandbox uses WebAssembly, a format that runs programs in a tightly walled box, to cap an agent's computing power, memory and outbound connections. Akamai's Alexander said every team running agents "needs a boundary around the code those agents write."

Palo Alto's survey found organizations expect AI agents to grow 85% over the next 12 months, against 56% for human identities.

#3507. S&P 500 Sell-Off: Is Now the Right Time to Buy the Dip?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The stock market looks a bit wobbly. After reaching a new record high on Oct. 6, the S&P 500 index (^GSPC +0.60%) has declined by about 2.5% since then (as of this writing).

On the one hand, a 2.5% drop in the S&P 500 can be unnerving to some investors. It might be a sign of economic trouble ahead. What if the stock market plunges even further into a 10% correction or a 20% bear market?

On the other hand, many investors might see a 2.5% price decline in the S&P 500 as a sign that "stocks are on sale" and that it's time to buy more shares of S&P 500 exchange-traded funds (ETFs). This is called "buying the dip" -- investing more money in the stock market when the overall market has "dipped" to lower levels.

Here's the thing: If you can patiently leave your stock portfolio alone for the next several years, asking if it's time to "buy the dip" is the wrong question. For consistent, long-term investors, there is rarely a bad time to invest.

Here's why patient, consistent, "boring" long-term investing is often more lucrative than waiting for the perfect moment to swoop in and "buy the dip."

The S&P 500 has delivered average annual returns of 9.98% since 1928. That's a remarkably long-term track record of wealth-building returns despite the massive stock market crashes, global wars, and short-term economic crises along the way. The stock market tends to keep chugging along. Companies make money and pass on some of those earnings to shareholders. Stocks go up.

The past few years for the S&P 500 have been even better than that long-term 98-year average. In the past 10 years, the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +0.58%) has delivered annualized returns of 15.3%. In the 16 years since the fund's inception, it has delivered annualized returns of 14.94%.

If you just bought shares of VOO and held onto them for five, 10, or 16 years, you would've earned significant returns. Even if you thought you were investing at a "bad time" or the "best time" on any given day.

When the S&P 500 is down by a few percent in a month, it's easy to think that "maybe I'll just wait to invest until stocks go down even more. Then I'll buy more shares at a lower price." But this is called market timing, and it doesn't work.

Research from Schwab found that, based on 20 years of S&P 500 returns from 2005 to 2024, someone who invested $2,000 per year as early as possible each year during those 20 years would have earned almost as high a return as someone who invested $2,000 per year at exactly "the right time," on the day of each year when S&P 500 levels were lowest.

Even if you perfectly "buy the dip" on the S&P 500 (which is impossible to do consistently), you still might only slightly outperform the returns you'd get from steady investing over many years.

Don't worry about buying the dip or timing the market or picking the perfect short-term moment to invest when the S&P 500 is volatile. Just focus on your long-term plans. Buy and hold a diversified portfolio of great stocks, and let the market work for you.
