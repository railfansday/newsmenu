#5485. Case study: Puerto Rican apartment complex relies on battery power

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It’s no secret that Puerto Rico has an energy problem. An aging electrical grid and frequent hurricane activity have left the island with an unreliable power supply. Frequent and prolonged blackouts have led many Puerto Ricans to turn to solar energy. Ricardo Fonseca, owner of Ingenious Solution Services, has been installing solar and battery storage systems for more than 20 years in Puerto Rico. He has seen his business grow by helping homeowners and businesses become more energy resilient.

“Our business began by offering services to the pharmaceutical industry in the area of ​​validations,” Fonseca said. “Later, demand grew for services such as electricity, plumbing, refrigeration, appliances, and thermal studies. After Hurricane Maria, our electrical system collapsed and a huge demand for photovoltaic systems began.”

Over the years, Fonseca has installed all types and brands of battery systems. Most recently, Fonseca installed a unique solar system with Briggs & Stratton SimplePHI 6.6 batteries for a two-unit apartment building in Bayamón, in northern Puerto Rico.

“The area’s electrical system suffers from frequent outages, and for that reason alone, the photovoltaic system was installed. Since the structure occupies the entire plot, it was impossible to install a generator,” Fonseca said. “The client was already familiar with our company, having previously hired us to install a 30 kW three-phase backup system with 46 kW of batteries for his business.”

For the apartment building in Bayamon, what appears to be a triple battery system is actually three independent systems powering three different locations. Each of the two apartments has a SimpliPHI 6.6 triple battery system. Additionally, a SimpliPHI 6.6 battery powers the four electric gates that control access to the building’s parking area and lighting in the common areas. It’s an ingenious design that not only looks good but also makes efficient use of space.

“The systems operate independently, they don’t communicate with each other, and there are no backup generators,” Fonseca said. “Each apartment has eighteen 550-W solar panels with 19.8 kWh of Briggs & Stratton batteries. The building operated in self-consumption mode for five months after installing the three systems, as we were waiting for electrical approval from Luma Energy.”

Each electrical system in an apartment places a considerable load on the system, as it supports an air conditioner, a heater, a stove, lights and all the other electrical components that a typical home has.

“The system works wonderfully,” Fonseca said. “They’ve never run out of electricity. If there’s plenty of sun, the batteries charge in 75 minutes with the panels. If there isn’t as much sun, it takes a maximum of 150 minutes. With the meters installed and the electrical certification, they’ll be able to start exporting energy to the grid.”

Fonseca has worked with Briggs & Stratton for more than eight years and has completed approximately a dozen installations with the company’s SimpliPHI 6.6 battery.

“SimpliPHI batteries are reliable, long-lasting, and fast-charging. I’ve never had any problems with them,” he said. “They’re smaller than Tesla batteries, so they take up less space, they’re lighter and easier to handle. Best of all, they’re easy to install, and now that the new controller can be connected from either side, it’s even more convenient.”

Fonseca isn’t the only one who notices the advantages of Briggs & Stratton. Recently, a customer who runs three air conditioners at night commented that he hasn’t had to use a backup generator because the SimpliPHI batteries handle the entire load.

“The only battery systems I install are from Briggs & Stratton,” Fonseca said. “I frequently receive calls about other products that aren’t working, reporting malfunctions, so I’ve seen all the problems other battery and inverter manufacturers have: they don’t last more than a few years and fail in various ways. Thanks to my reputation and the experienced team I’ve built, I plan to expand my business to other areas of Puerto Rico, from Mayagüez to Fajardo and possibly to the central part of the island, in Naranjito and Aibonito.”

#3925. Diablo 4’s War Plans Are Getting A Huge Upgrade In Season 16

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Diablo 4's War Plans system is getting a major upgrade in Season 16, giving players even more control over their endgame activities and rewards.

Developer Blizzard introduced War Plans earlier this year as part of the game's second (and last) expansion, Lord of Hatred. It's an endgame system where players can essentially create a playlist of endgame activities, along the way ranking up those activities via a skill tree to make them even more rewarding.

It was a welcome addition, but unfortunately only gave players minimal control over what activities would appear as part of their plans. Players could also only reroll their War Plans a limited number of times, meaning if you didn't like a particular activity, oftentimes you were stuck having to do it.

All of that looks to be changing in Season 16 with the addition of a new meta-progression system called War Plans Tactics. By completing War Plan activities, players will be able to invest points into the tactics tree to make it so certain activities are more likely to appear and specific types of rewards will drop. How players reroll their War Plans is also changing, with a new currency called Marks of Wisdom that will cap at 20 and should let players reroll their plans more often.

As detailed by Blizzard in a blog post ahead of the season arriving on the Diablo 4 public test realm October 27, players will be able to assign up to three points into each endgame activity and up to three points into different types of rewards. 

"Earn points from completing War Plans activities, then invest these points into nodes to increase the chance of preferred activities or rewards appearing," Blizzard said. "This allows you to tailor your War Plans more closely with both your goals and playstyle."

Putting at least one point into Helltides or Nightmare dungeons also grants the choice of increased chances for Greater Helltides and Escalation Nightmare dungeons, respectively. On the reward side, players will be able to make it so they are more likely to earn armor, weapons, jewelry, Obducite, salvage, gems, gold, activity keys, Horadric Cube materials, or Talisman items from activities.

The new tactics system isn't the only change coming to War Plans. A new seasonal War Plans tree, Tyrael's Boons, will also be coming with Season 16. There will be four Tyrael's Boons' paths to invest points into, with Blizzard stating players will be able to choose up to four of the six nodes within each path. Those four paths are:

  • Curious Addition: Upgrades the Purveyor of Curiosities to increase the chances for more and higher quality items.
  • Merchant of Mystery: Adds a new merchant in Torment difficulties that sell item caches in exchange for Forgotten Souls.
  • Scent of Gold: Increases the amount of gold, experience, and materials you earn.
  • Trash for Treasure: Reduces transmuting costs, increases chances to earn Obducite and salvage, and can even grant a new Horadric Cube recipe that allows players to upgrade a Tempered Affix on an item into a Greater Affix.

The changes to War Plans look to be the biggest features coming as part of Season 16, but there will additionally be a new type of gear to acquire, Horadric legendaries. These special legendaries will synergize with specific Talisman charms, with Horadric legendary jewelry items even counting for Talisman set bonuses. That should open the door to all kinds of new set and charm combinations that are sure to result in some wild new endgame builds.

Diablo 4 Season 16 will be playable on the PTR until November 3, with the full season set to launch December 1. Though Diablo 4 won't be getting additional expansions, it will get a new class, the Amazon, in the first half of next year.

As for what's next for the franchise, Diablo 5 is slated to arrive in 2029. Blizzard has already revealed three of the game's playable classes and promised it will have more classes at launch than any previous Diablo game.

Unless Harry Redknapp is about to be parachuted in as caretaker manager, I see little point in supporters calling for Füllkrug to return. West Ham’s priority should be to let him continue scoring and then demand a proper transfer fee.

#3582. What I Love (and What I Don't) About the New Una Fitness Watch

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve reviewed every type of fitness watch and thought I had seen it all, but my first glimpse of an Una watch had my jaw on the floor: There is a USB-C port on the side of the watch. Not a set of pins for a magnetic charger, or a socket that requires a proprietary cable—just a hole you can stick a regular old USB-C cable into. This is what watches have been missing.

The Una watch started popping up on my feed about two years ago, and finally started shipping this summer. I’ve been wearing a review unit for a few weeks. The Una watch is advertised as repairable and upgradable, with open source software. There are little screws that I can see around the watch face, and on the back panel. The Una website sells a replacement battery for $40 and a replacement screen for $50. They also sell the tiny screwdriver that you’ll need to make repairs, but if you’re the kind of person who gets excited about a repairable watch, you may already have your own.

Overall, I like it. The Una watch has an MIP screen (an older tech that is a cult favorite) and an app that's simple but effective. Not everything lived up to my expectations in testing, but no features failed outright. This feels like a solid start to the concept of “one watch for life” and I’m excited to see how the hardware and software offerings evolve over time.

Most wearables these days try to do as many jobs as possible, whether they’re the right tool for those jobs or not. I am honestly fine with a running watch measuring time, distance, and heart rate while I run, and not trying to score my sleep or pretend it can tell how fast I’m aging.

The Una watch app shows my steps, activity, and floors climbed for the day, as well as my average and resting heart rates. When I go for a run, it tracks my pace, distance, and heart rate during the run and gives me a nice page of data afterward. It reliably uploads this data to Strava, and can make little bar graphs of how these numbers vary from day to day.

There is a “store” (everything is free, as far as I can tell) where you can download extra watch faces and features. One I’m trying out is a habit tracker called HabitLab: It asks you every evening whether you had alcohol, cigarettes, a late meal, and so on. Every morning, it asks how you’re feeling. And then, even though the watch doesn’t normally track your sleep, the app takes note of your overnight HRV and resting heart rate. Over time, it will correlate the answers to those questions with the sleep data, to hopefully give results that are similar to Whoop’s journal analysis. (I haven’t been using it long enough to judge how well it works, but stay tuned.)

HabitLab appears in one area of the watch as a standalone app; another, called Gym Sets, gives me a strength training activity type, which the watch did not otherwise have. I just select Gym Sets instead of “Run” or “Workout,” and the app tracks my heart rate while also giving me a set timer.

The store is small, and the apps are simple, without many of the features that you can find on the apps for Apple Watch or other more fully featured devices. But the Una watch has only been out for a few months, so it’s not surprising that users haven’t created a plethora of apps yet. The company’s software development kit, and even its circuit board designs, are free for people to download and develop from.

I was worried that the emphasis on repairability, and the affordable price, meant that the Una watch would be made with components that maybe aren’t fully up to date. Heart rate sensors have been getting better over the years, so Una’s will have to be pretty good if it wants to even sit in the middle of the pack. Garmin, Apple, and now Fitbit have raised the bar quite a bit.

I will say the GPS track was reasonably good; on trail runs and road runs, it accurately followed the path I actually took. (It doesn’t seem to do quite as well as a new Garmin, but it’s still perfectly usable and its distance totals added up to reasonably accurate numbers.)

Heart rate accuracy disappointed me, though. When I first got the watch, the heart rate tracking was abysmal. I sent a few emails to my contact at Una, and almost had to publish this review with very bad news about the watch’s accuracy, but then a firmware update for the Una watch popped up on my phone. I installed it, and then tried another test run or two, and the heart rate accuracy is much better. If you get the watch, make sure it’s updated to at least 1.6.0.

That said, the update brought the heart rate accuracy to a level where I’d say it’s adequate, but it’s not good (and definitely not great). Its graph follows the ups and downs of my heart rate as measured by a chest strap, but sometimes the Una will spike and show a reading that’s significantly higher than my heart is actually beating. In the graph below, note the spike to around 165 when the true value was 155, and then later in the workout the Una watch detects another spike around 160 when my heart rate was actually around 145 and decreasing. (I saw similar anomalies in multiple workouts.)

In my time with the Una watch, many things made me happy. I still love the USB-C charging, and it was a delight to just plug it in to the same nightstand cable I normally use with my phone, no need to go looking for an adapter or dongle.

I like the MIP screen, which was brilliant in the sunlight and had a serviceable backlight for use indoors or in the dark. The small number of workout profiles and the small number of glances and apps is just fine if you’re not asking for much from a watch, and I wasn’t. It’s minimalist, low-distraction, and there isn’t a single AI-based feature anywhere. A true breath of fresh air.

And at $199, you’re not paying extra for these privileges. The price is just a little more than the new Fitbit Edge ($179.99), but less than a Coros Pace 4 ($249) or a Garmin 70 (also $249). The Una watch doesn’t have as many features or activities as these watches, but perhaps those pluses I mentioned make up for it.

The Una watch would be a good pick for someone who is excited about developing apps, wants a repairable watch, always loses charging adapters, or appreciates a minimalist approach. But would I recommend it for the average person who just wants a basic fitness watch? I’m not so sure.

The flaky heart rate sensor disqualifies it for a wearer who wants a more accurate, modern watch. The MIP screen is going to look old and weird to people who have gotten used to brighter, clearer AMOLED screens. The lack of sleep tracking or other convenience metrics may be hard to take when plenty of watches have these features.

So I like this watch today, with reservations, and I have high hopes for the future. If Una lives up to its vision, it’s going to be great to see replaceable components become available—perhaps there will be an option for an AMOLED screen for those who want it, and maybe there’s room for heart rate accuracy improvement in future firmware updates.

But a lot depends on the company itself. “One watch for life” implies that the company stays around long enough to make more than one model. I’m also hopeful that the future offerings will match what people want, and that the watch and app will stay uncluttered and subscription-free. But surely there will be competing interests that the company has to balance as they grow (if they grow), so time will tell whether the reality lives up to the vision. That said, I am liking the vision a lot.

#4537. 93,997 Range Rovers Recalled Because Water Can Fry the Backup Camera

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The drainage system in the tailgate isn’t doing a good enough job of extracting water, potentially causing the rearview camera to fail.

Land Rover is recalling 93,997 vehicles over a problem affecting the rearview camera. The recall includes Range Rover SUVs from 2022 through 2026 model years, regardless of which powertrain they were fitted with. The population of impacted SUVs left the Solihull, U.K., assembly plant between July 8, 2021, and June 4, 2026, according to documents filed with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).

The camera failures are caused by an issue with the water management system for the Range Rover’s tailgate, according to the recall documents. The drainage system apparently may not be doing a good enough job of clearing away water, causing moisture to pool around the rearview camera. Over time, the documents state, this can damage the camera and lead to a distorted image being displayed on the dashboard-mounted touchscreen when the car is put into reverse. It may even cause no image to be shown at all, and either scenario could increase the risk of an injury or a crash.

The fix is pretty straightforward. Land Rover will drill additional drain holes in the underside of the tailgate trim in which the rearview camera is housed, and the camera will be inspected and replaced if needed. This repair will be performed at no cost to the owners. Dealerships will be notified on October 29, while notification letters are set to be sent to owners on or before December 11.

This isn’t the first time we’ve seen a recall because of water taking out rearview cameras. In July, Honda recalled 325,588 Odyssey minivans for a similar issue, in which water was corroding the camera’s circuit board. This had followed a previous recall from 2020 where moisture entered the rearview camera mounting holes, causing the camera to malfunction.
