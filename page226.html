Keep your most-used apps accessible with this new feature.

Several years ago, Android introduced the ability to “bubble” a text conversation, or move that app to a floating bubble on your home screen. It’s a convenient way to keep a conversation easily accessible.

As part of the Android 17 rollout, users got the option to use app bubbles. When you bubble an app, it sits on top of everything else on your screen, making it easy to access when you need it. You can place up to five apps in your bubble.

A bubble only saves a tap or two each time you use an app, but it’s a fantastic multi-tasking tool if you have a handful of apps you use often. You could do something similar to this before, but only for Google Messages and Facebook Messenger chats.

The ability to bubble an app on Android is starting as a Pixel exclusive, but the feature will come to other manufacturers over time. There is an existing, similar feature for Samsung called Smart Pop Up View, and instructions for that are below.

To get started, choose the app you want from either your app screen or your home screen.

Press and hold on the app until a pop-up menu appears.

The menu will show options like App info, Add app to home screen, or Pause app. Select the Bubble option.

Your selected app will shrink to an icon that you can move around your home screen. You don’t have the freedom to place it anywhere, but it can go anywhere along the left or right side of your screen. The app will stay on top of everything else on your screen, and you can bubble multiple apps at once, but they will always move together.

Samsung has a similar feature called Smart Pop-Up View that lets you respond to notifications on selected apps without leaving the app you’re in. You might need to enable your “Multi window for all apps” setting first.

Navigate to your settings app, open it, and select Notification Settings.

Once you’re in advanced settings, find and choose Floating Notifications. Then select Smart Pop-Up view.

Now just select which apps you want to have priority.

According to Pilestedt, 95% of the studio is focused on Helldivers 2, with a "small team" now prototyping Arrowhead's next game. They currently have around 150 people on the books. "Studio-wise, we want to grow on our own terms and know when to stop," Pilestedt commented over email, noting that "plenty of teams hit big and immediately double their size".

The intrigue here, for me, is just how different Helldivers 3 would need to be to stand apart from its predecessor. Helldivers 2 is a very different game from the original Helldivers, swapping the old top-down view for third-person gunnery with a greater emphasis on physics and credible weapon handling. I'm not sure Helldivers would be entertaining as a first-person shooter – so much of the game's beguiling daftness depends on seeing your character. #3146. How the Rays and Brewers were made: 2026 World Series foes linked by roster-building philosophies

The 2026 World Series matchup between the Milwaukee Brewers and Tampa Bay Rays is rich with subplots. Neither team has won a World Series, so we're in for a first when it comes time to hoist the trophy. That hasn't happened since the Philadelphia Phillies and Kansas City Royals met in the 1980 World Series.

There's also the backdrop of the ongoing negotiations on a new collective bargaining agreement. MLB, its constituent team owners, and commissioner Rob Manfred -- who takes his marching orders from those owners -- are pushing for a cap on payrolls. Indeed, everything the management side has proposed in those negotiations has been tied to the players' acceptance of a cap, and everything has been rejected by the players' union as long as it is tied to said cap.

From a rhetorical standpoint, this World Series matchup is quite inconvenient for Manfred and the owners. These are two of the smallest markets in MLB with two bottom-tier payrolls. The Rays especially don't invest much in the on-field product. Consider:

Add those payroll rankings together, and it's the lowest on record for any World Series. It's the smallest combined market size for a World Series since the St. Louis Cardinals and Kansas City Royals met in 1985. These realities are even more striking once you consider that, in 2026, the Brewers swept the dynastic Los Angeles Dodgers in the NLCS, while the Rays vanquished the historically preeminent New York Yankees in the AL East race and then again in the ALDS.

How do you argue that super-spenders like the Dodgers and Yankees, or even disappointments like the Mets and Blue Jays, are breaking baseball when this is your World Series? MLB will, of course, argue it anyway, but the narrative packs less of a punch this October.

To be sure, there's a definite element of ownership neglect with those payroll figures. Both teams are revenue-sharing recipients on balance, and such clubs that run low payrolls make enough via revenue sharing to cover most or, in some cases, all of their cash payroll. Circumstance plays an obvious role, yes, but so does the will to compete and win on the part of owners.

Still, the question remains: How have the Brewers and Rays built championship-grade rosters without spending much at all?

In terms of a tactical approach, both have seen high contact rates on offense as a neglected trait across the league, and they've selected for that. Like most other clubs, they prioritize velocity and repertoire depth on the pitching front. For these purposes, though, we're talking about the paths to roster construction, and, unsurprisingly, the Brewers and Rays have a lot in common in their preferred means of player acquisition (and not just because Brewers president of baseball operations Matt Arnold spent nine years in the Rays front office under Andrew Friedman).

The temptation is to think that both of these small-budget teams are heavily homegrown. That's the best way to get cheap production across the roster, especially when many of those homegrown talents are in their salary-controlled years. As we'll see, though, that's not really the case. These two teams are among the very best at identifying and developing talent acquired via the draft and through amateur international signings. As noted, these two clubs don't invest as much as they should in player payroll, but they do have large and wide-ranging baseball operations and scouting staffs within their front offices. The Brewers and Rays know their own players, of course, but if there's a superpower, it's how well they appraise players, especially young ones, in other organizations. That skill informs much of their current success.

To begin digging into the matter of how the low-payroll, wee-market Rays and Brewers built their rosters, we'll look at how they compare to the other 10 teams that made the postseason in 2026 when it comes to the three most common paths to player acquisition: free agents, homegrown talents, and trade acquisitions. We'll not worry about waiver claims and Rule 5 draftees, as those typically provide less or even negligible value.

First, let's look at each playoff team's number of homegrown players -- i.e., those initially signed as amateurs by the club in question and who have never played for another club -- on the current 40-man roster (note that most teams do not presently have all 40 spots filled, so these roster totals will vary from club to club):

The Brewers and Rays, as you can see, both rank in the bottom half among 2026 playoff teams when it comes to homegrown players on the 40. In the Rays' case, the most notable fully in-house talents are Jonathan Aranda, Chandler Simpson, Taylor Walls, and Ian Seymour. Additionally, lefty starter and ace-when-healthy Shane McClanahan, who's been out since early September with forearm soreness and presumably won't be able to pitch in the World Series, came up through the organization.

As for the Brewers, they're not the most homegrown of playoff teams, but one can argue no one's collection of internal talents has moved the needle quite so much. Consider that the Brewers' position players and pitchers during the regular season combined for a WAR of 50.4. Making up slightly more than half of that tally were five homegrown players: Jacob Misiorowski, Jackson Chourio, Brice Turang, Garrett Mitchell, and Logan Henderson. This is to say nothing of Cooper Pratt and Aaron Ashby, whom the Brewers also drafted and developed.

Next up is free agents, and these two World Series combatants aren't high on that list either:

The Rays' highest-salaried free agent addition is 36-year-old starter Nick Martinez, who inked a one-year, $13 million pact for this season. His deal also includes a largely meaningless mutual option for 2027. Martinez, who put up a 3.9 WAR during the regular season and figures to make at least one World Series start against the Brewers, has more than justified the investment from the team's standpoint. Lefty Steven Matz is on a two-year, $15 million contract, which makes him the Rays' largest investment on the market. Needless to say, the Rays aren't built through free agency.

The same goes for Milwaukee. The Brewers' largest market investment is Brandon Woodruff, who accepted the qualifying offer this past offseason and is being paid $22 million this season. Only team captain and organizational stalwart Christian Yelich is being paid a higher salary by the Brewers this year ($26 million as part of a nine-year, $215 million extension signed in 2020). Woodruff, though, isn't what we think of when it comes to typical free agent additions. He's an organizational lifer who's been with the Brewers since signing with them in 2014.

Woodruff is one of the best starting pitchers in baseball when he's healthy, but injuries have been the throughline of his career. Maybe, though, he'll be a surprise contributor in the World Series, as he's been assiduously working his way back from dogged shoulder issues. In terms of more traditional free agents signed away from other clubs, catcher and bench thunder-wielder Gary Sánchez is the Brewers' biggest current investment. He's on a one-year, $1.75 million contract.

Finally, we arrive at trade acquisitions. The majority of each team's 40-man roster is populated at a majority level by trade pick-ups:

The Rays have the most trade pick-ups on the 40-man, not just among playoff teams but also among all 30 MLB teams. The Marlins are next at 23, and then come the Brewers. Such frequency speaks to the Rays' and Brewers' established knack for knowing which players to target in trade and, broadly speaking, which players to give up. The "never trade with the Rays" contemporary chestnut of recent baseball wisdom is overstated -- they can and do lose trades -- but there's no question that trades have done the heavy lifting in the present roster's high level of success.

The Rays made their boldest stroke in acquiring 22-year-old burgeoning superstar Junior Caminero via trade with the Guardians in November 2021, when he was just an 18-year-old in the Dominican Summer League. Today, even just two full seasons into Caminero's MLB career, it looks like a franchise-altering trade for the Rays. That's not all, though. Yandy Díaz was also acquired in a trade with Cleveland, this one roughly three years before the Caminero swap. Closer Bryan Baker came to the Rays in a 2025 deadline trade with the division-rival Orioles. Speaking of the deadline, lead executive Erick Neander earlier this season raised the Rays' ceiling quite substantially by going out and getting ALCS MVP Freddy Peralta from the Mets, catcher Liam Hicks from the Marlins, and key setup man Tyler Wells from, again, the Orioles.

The Rays accumulated a WAR of 41.2 this season, and their trade additions make up 23.9 WAR of that total. It's a team, both in raw numbers and overall contributions, built mostly through perhaps the most direct test of an organization's faculties -- some of mine for some of yours.

There's some connective tissue here, too. Peralta, of course, was a long-time Brewer before he was traded to the Mets ahead of his walk year (a year in which he was making $8 million), and he'll be taking the mound against his former team in the World Series, likely for multiple starts. The big one, though, is right-hander Drew Rasmussen, who was the Rays' ace for the bulk of the regular season. The Rays acquired him from the Brewers back in May 2021 as part of a four-player deal that sent shortstop Willy Adames to Milwaukee. Adames was one of the Brewers' best players for his three-plus seasons there before signing with the Giants as a free agent. As for Rasmussen, he's been one of MLB's top starting pitchers over the last two years, and his overall body of work with the Rays is exceptional. Coincidentally, the Rays drafted Rasmussen with the 31st overall pick out of Oregon State in 2017. However, they failed to sign him, and then the Brewers secured his services the next year by drafting him in the sixth round.

As for the Brewers and top decision-maker Matt Arnold, trades have also left a deep footprint on their current roster. The most famous trade in recent Brewers history was one made by Arnold's predecessor, David Stearns (coincidentally, he now leads the Mets, who traded for Peralta in the 2025-26 offseason). That was the 2018 blockbuster that brought Yelich to Milwaukee. Yelich is post-peak, but the newly named captain is still a vital presence for Milwaukee. Elsewhere -- and more germane to the current Brewers model -- trades for William Contreras, Jake Bauers, Andrew Vaughn, Trevor Megill, Robert Gasser, and Chad Patrick played a vital role in the team's success this season. Arnold, like the Rays' Neander, was also active at the deadline, as he fortified the pitching staff with Dustin May, JoJo Romero, and Antonio Senzatela.

Trade additions claimed both LCS MVP awards (Peralta for the Rays, Bauers for the Brewers), and it likewise wouldn't be shocking if a player acquired by the Rays or Brewers via trade winds up earning top individual honors in the World Series. That's a predictable consequence when the two World Series clubs have combined for, oh, almost 50 trade acquisitions.

In broad terms, the Rays and Brewers have built their championship rosters in the ways that low-spenders must. Milwaukee has made quite a lot of trades, but extracted most of its value in 2026 from homegrown players. The Rays have made even more trades, and most of their 2026 contributions have indeed come from outsiders.

When it comes to World Series teams of a recent vintage, this is something of a departure -- one worth appreciating and, no doubt, emulating if you're a team that aspires to reach the heights that the Brewers and Rays presently occupy.

#340. Fewer people are flying as airfare soars. Here's why airlines aren't worried

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fewer people are flying this year, but with airfare this high and climbing, airline executives say that's just fine.

At the New York Hilton in Midtown Manhattan on Friday night, the aviation industry held its annual Wings Club gala, honoring Ed Bastian, CEO of profit-leader Delta Air Lines.

North American airline CEOs and other executives at the black-tie bash told CNBC that demand trends aren't worrying them, but persistently high, profit-crimping fuel prices are.

The number of people screened at U.S. checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Major U.S. carriers have said capturing higher fares matters more than fully filling planes or flying record numbers of travelers.

"We're actively prioritizing yield versus load factor and really happy as we see it. It fits right in with the premium investments that we're making," American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said on the company's earnings call Thursday.

American's load factor, a gauge of how full planes are flying, fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% in the same quarter of 2025.

Plane ticket prices have skyrocketed as airlines grapple with a spike in jet fuel prices, their biggest expense after labor, and pass more of those costs on to customers.

Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation read. Fare tracker Hopper said it expects this holiday season to come with the most expensive ticket prices in years.

The average Southwest Airlines fare in the third quarter, for example, was $231.69, up close to $50 from a year earlier. That doesn't include Southwest's new fees, like its extra charges for checked bags or a seat closer to the front of the plane.

The airline's revenue passenger count, however, fell 3.2% from a year ago to about 33.5 million people.

Still, both fares and the passenger counts exceeded Southwest's expectations.

"Passenger fares came in about $3 above what our forecast was and the more important thing is that passenger volumes also exceeded our expectations," Southwest CEO Bob Jordan said on an earnings call Thursday.

Since April — when airlines began warning in earnest about profits taking a hit from the spike in jet fuel — through Thursday, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened at airport checkpoints than in the same period of 2025, according to the Transportation Security Administration. That's down less than 2% from last year, but it follows years of growth.

With jet fuel prices more than double year-ago levels — as seen in U.S. Gulf Coast spot prices at $4.63 a gallon, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — airlines have focused on recovering as much of the fuel spike as possible.

Despite some drops, airline executives said during their earnings calls that overall travel demand is robust, especially for profitable premium cabins like business class.

And not every carrier is seeing a decline. United Airlines flew 2.4% more passengers in the third quarter than a year ago and said its unit revenue rose 16%, the biggest increase since the pandemic.

"I've been pleasantly surprised at the industry's ability this year to raise yields and pass through much of the increase in fuel prices," CEO Scott Kirby said on the company's earnings call Wednesday. "So it's possible that airlines will continue to focus on yields at the expense of load factor and that alone could get us to double-digit margins next year."

Most airlines expect fuel costs to keep biting, but executives say they're confident in their pricing power.

United, for example, said it expects to pay an average of $4.80 per gallon for fuel in the fourth quarter, up from $3.90 per gallon in the third.

American, United, Delta and Southwest all cut their 2026 profit forecasts this month when they reported earnings. American is the only one of the four that expects to post a loss this year.

American and others have also reduced growth plans or warned the industry could dial back its capacity expansion goals, which could keep fares elevated. Reduced capacity can make bookable seats scarcer, driving up prices even more if demand holds.

The carriers said demand has been particularly strong for premium seats, which command hundreds, if not thousands of dollars, more than coach tickets.

"Today, we find ourselves yet at another inflection point for our industry and the world. ... Geopolitics affecting our business, driving the price of jet fuel to historic highs," Bastian said in his award-acceptance speech, adding that artificial intelligence makes it hard to predict months or weeks ahead.

"It causes many people to question yet again the durability of our industry. But I'm not worried because I know the people in this room, and I know your resilience and how you've demonstrated that time and time again," he said.

Smaller airlines set to report results in the coming weeks, starting with JetBlue Airways next week, followed by Frontier Airlines and Allegiant Air, have traditionally focused on more price-sensitive customers, which could show if those travelers are pulling back at all.

One factor that could also affect air travel is the demise of Spirit Airlines, which went out of business in early May.

"Some of its customers have likely stopped flying," Bank of America said in a research note Thursday.

Many of the problems that plagued the airline industry for years are starting to abate.

In some cases, aircraft deliveries are still years behind schedule. But Boeing, whose CEO, Kelly Ortberg, attended on Friday night, and its main rival, Airbus, have ramped up production. Boeing will update investors on its production outlook when it reports quarterly results Tuesday.

The Federal Aviation Administration has approved Boeing's Max 7, a key smaller jet for Southwest Airlines, and the Max 10, which Alaska Airlines and United Airlines have been waiting for for years.

Pratt & Whitney parent, RTX, and GE Aerospace say they're improving engine durability and shop visit turnaround time, though customers say more improvements are needed.

#4885. Athletes From Every Sport Are Talking About ‘RED-S.’ Is Workout Hustle Culture to Blame?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

YOU NEED TO fuel your body properly for peak performance—that's not news. But hitting the right balance of fuel to energy output can be tough, especially if you're trying to lose weight in the process, are on a GLP-1 medication, and/or recently switched up your workouts. That's where you fall into danger of RED-S, a scary sounding condition where your body reaches a dangerous stage of energy depletion

Also known as “Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport,” RED-S can do everything from slow down your progress to mess with your body on a cellular level. "The simplest explanation is it's an energy mismatch," says Albert Matheny, RD, CSCS, co-founder of SoHo Strength Lab.

RED-S has always been a potential issue with athletes, but the rise of GLP-1s and a NO DAYS OFF workout hustle culture has pushed the condition into the fore.

Trainers say every active person should know the symptoms of RED-S—and what might cause them to show up.

RED-S HAPPENS WHEN you're not fueling your body properly. "It essentially means running your body's engine on empty," explains Dani Singer, CPT, founder of Fit2Go Personal Training. "It's a mismatch between the energy you take in through nutrition and the energy you expend through exercise and daily living."

While a slight calorie deficit is needed to lose weight, RED-S is more severe than cutting a few calories. It's chronically under-fueling while also trying to train at a high level. "You are demanding a level of output from your body that you aren't giving it the fuel to sustain," Singer says.

A FEW THINGS can go down. "Deep, lingering fatigue is usually the first red flag," Singer says. "You'll feel like you're moving through molasses." At the same time, because your body isn't dedicating energy to recovery, Singer says you become "highly susceptible" to overuse injuries and stress fractures due to decreased bone mineral density.

"You'll also likely see thermoregulation issues—always feeling cold—compromised immunity, hormonal imbalances, and ironically, a complete plateau in your fitness progress," Singer says. "You can't build muscle or acquire new physical skills if your body doesn't have the building blocks to do so."

With RED-S, your body does what it can with reduce energy it has. "It really prioritizes key metabolic functions and the things that require your body to function [in daily life]," Matheny says. He compares RED-S to being in low power mode—your body is going to try to support basic functions, while forgetting about things like muscle growth and fitness performance.

"We are constantly taught that 'eat less, move more' is the holy grail of fitness, but your body needs energy not just to exercise, but to simply keep the lights on for things like breathing, hormone production, and cellular repair," Singer says. "When you severely under-fuel while keeping your physical output high, your body goes into triage mode."

THERE ARE LIKELY a few things behind this. "For the general fitness population, it often comes from a toxic combination of diet culture and high-intensity workout trends demanding maximum output on minimum calories," Singer says.

And then there are GLP-1s. "These medications are incredibly effective at blunting the natural hunger cue," Singer explains. "A user might only be taking in 800 to 1,000 calories a day simply because they feel completely full." If someone on a GLP-1 decides to pair their weight loss journey with a rigorous new workout routine, they may not realize how little fuel is in the tank, Singer says.

BECAUSE EVERY PERSON’S energy needs are different, avoiding or finding a solution to RED-S can be complex. "The threshold in literature is 30 calories per kilogram of body weight—that's the lowest level," Matheny says. (That means a 180-pound man should be taking in 2,448 calories per day at a minimum.)

Singer also recommends flipping the way you look at food. "We have to stop viewing food purely as a metric to minimize and start viewing it as fuel for the capabilities we want to build," he says. Singer suggests being "brutally honest" with your baseline food intake, making sure you're eating enough to support your activity.

"From there, listen to the biofeedback your body gives you," he says. "If you are constantly exhausted, sore for days after a basic workout, or losing your motivation to train, you are likely under-fueled." Singer recommends prioritizing protein for recovery, along with getting in enough carbs to fuel the work you're asking of your body. "A good routine should improve how you move and feel in daily life, not break you down," he says.

If you're struggling to get the balance right, consider talking with a sports-minded registered dietitian. They should be able to help you figure out what you're going wrong and put you on the path to fueling your body properly in the future.

#617. One of Earth’s most powerful supervolcanoes is refilling with fresh magma

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

One of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly refilling its enormous underground magma reservoir, thousands of years after producing one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recent geological history. Scientists at Kobe University have discovered evidence that fresh magma is accumulating beneath Japan's Kikai caldera, which unleashed a catastrophic eruption about 7,300 years ago.

The discovery offers a rare look at how giant volcanic systems rebuild their magma supplies after massive eruptions. It could also provide important clues about the behavior of other supervolcanoes, including Yellowstone in the United States and Toba in Indonesia, potentially helping scientists improve their ability to recognize warning signs of future eruptions.

How Supervolcanoes Store Enormous Amounts of Magma

Some volcanic eruptions are so immense that they dramatically reshape the landscape. The amount of magma released can be staggering, enough to bury all of New York City's Central Park beneath a layer roughly 12 kilometers (7.5 miles) thick.

When such enormous volumes of magma escape from underground, the rock above the emptied reservoir can collapse, leaving behind a broad, relatively shallow depression known as a caldera. These features are associated with some of the most powerful eruptions in Earth's history.

Famous examples include Yellowstone, Toba, and Japan's Kikai caldera, most of which lies beneath the ocean. Kikai last experienced a giant eruption approximately 7,300 years ago, producing the largest known volcanic eruption of the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago and continues today.

Although scientists know that giant caldera volcanoes can erupt more than once, they still have an incomplete understanding of how the enormous quantities of magma required for these events accumulate underground. This makes it extremely difficult to anticipate when another major eruption might occur.

"We must understand how such large quantities of magma can accumulate to understand how giant caldera eruptions occur," says Kobe University geophysicist Nobukazu Seama.

Scientists Discover a Massive Magma Reservoir Beneath Japan

Kikai's underwater location might seem like an obstacle to studying the volcano, but it actually gives researchers an important advantage. The surrounding seafloor allows scientists to carry out extensive geological surveys across the volcanic structure.

"The underwater location allows us to implement systematic, large-scale surveys," Seama explains.

To investigate what lies beneath Kikai, researchers from Kobe University collaborated with the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). They used powerful airgun arrays to generate seismic pulses that traveled through the crust beneath the ocean floor.

Instruments called ocean bottom seismometers recorded how these waves moved through the underground rock. Because seismic waves travel differently through solid rock and regions containing molten material, scientists can analyze their behavior to investigate structures that cannot be observed directly.

The findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, revealed a substantial region containing a high proportion of magma directly beneath the site of Kikai's massive eruption 7,300 years ago.

By examining the seismic measurements, the team was able to determine the approximate size and shape of the underground reservoir. Its position and extent suggest that it occupies the same magma storage system involved in the ancient eruption.

"Due to its extent and location it is clear that this is in fact the same magma reservoir as in the previous eruption," Seama says.

Fresh Magma Is Recharging the Ancient Supervolcano

Perhaps the most intriguing finding is that the magma currently beneath Kikai does not appear to be simply leftover material from its catastrophic eruption thousands of years ago.

Previous investigations revealed that a new lava dome has been developing near the center of the caldera over approximately the past 3,900 years. A lava dome forms when relatively thick, viscous lava accumulates around a volcanic vent rather than flowing freely across the surrounding landscape.

Chemical analyses also showed that volcanic material from this dome and other more recent activity differs in composition from the material released during Kikai's giant eruption.

That difference provides an important clue. It suggests that the magma now stored beneath the volcano was supplied by new injections of molten rock from deeper within Earth's crust or mantle, rather than remaining unchanged since the previous eruption.

"This means that the magma that is now present in the magma reservoir under the lava dome is likely newly injected magma," summarizes Seama.

Together, the seismic observations and chemical evidence help researchers develop a clearer picture of how a giant volcanic system can gradually rebuild its underground magma supply following a massive eruption.

This replenishment process does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent. Instead, it reveals how a magma reservoir can be recharged over thousands of years, an important process that scientists need to understand before they can reliably assess the long-term behavior of supervolcanoes.

What the Discovery Could Reveal About Yellowstone

The findings could have implications far beyond Japan.

Researchers believe the process identified beneath Kikai may help explain why other enormous caldera systems, including Yellowstone and Toba, also contain large magma reservoirs relatively close to Earth's surface.

"This magma re-injection model is consistent with the existence of large shallow magma reservoirs beneath other giant calderas like Yellowstone and Toba," says Seama.

The proposed model suggests that, following a giant eruption, new magma can gradually enter and replenish an existing underground reservoir. Understanding how this happens could reveal important patterns in the long-term cycles of magma accumulation, volcanic activity, and major eruptions.

Researchers now hope to improve the seismic techniques used at Kikai so they can investigate the movement and storage of magma in greater detail.

"We want to refine the methods that have proved to be so useful in this study to more deeply understand the re-injection processes. Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."

By revealing how fresh magma accumulates beneath a volcano responsible for one of Earth's most extraordinary eruptions, the research brings scientists closer to understanding the processes that eventually make such colossal events possible.
