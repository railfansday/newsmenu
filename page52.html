#195. Oleshky truce appears to hold as two European leaders have ‘frank’ call with Putin

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Frontline near Russian-occupied city in southern Ukraine quiet for first time in years ahead of planned evacuation

A local ceasefire in a southern Ukrainian city under Russian occupation appeared to be holding on Tuesday, with both sides ceasing drone attacks ahead of an apparent evacuation of about 2,000 trapped and desperate civilians.

The temporary truce began at 3am in Russian-controlled Oleshky. The city sits on the Dnipro River and is about 3 miles (5km) from Kherson, a larger city on the opposite bank. Ukrainian troops recaptured Kherson in autumn 2022.

Witnesses reported that the deadly frontline across the river was quiet for the first time in four years. The pause in fighting is due to last until 11pm on Sunday, Ukrainian officials said, and represents a significant if limited moment of de-escalation.

Finland’s president, Alexander Stubb, and Norway’s prime minister, Jonas Gahr Støre, held what they described as a “frank and substantive” phone call with Vladimir Putin about efforts to end the war in Ukraine. Støre indicated afterwards that big differences remained.

The two Nordic leaders said the call – a rare instance of direct contact between Putin and western leaders – was linked to upcoming talks between Russia, Ukraine and the US, expected to take place in Abu Dhabi next month.

The ceasefire in Oleshky appears to be an attempt by the Kremlin to curry favour with Donald Trump before the trilateral meeting and next week’s US midterm elections. The first lady, Melania Trump, requested the humanitarian mission, and in a post on X said it was brought forward by two days to allow the delivery of critical aid.

Its geographical scope had been extended beyond Oleshky “within the Kherson region”, she added, to provide safe passage for convoys. Calls for a ceasefire were also issued by Pope Leo, the United Nations, EU officials and the Council of Europe.

Between 1,500 and 2,000 people in the city have been living under a complete blockade by Russia. The last delivery of food to stores was in May, and many people are now starving and eating weeds. The Ukrainian military has airdropped food using drones but locals say Russian security forces detain anyone who takes it.

Russian soldiers have mined roads and use ruined buildings to coordinate round-the-clock strikes against Kherson. Some Oleshky residents have managed to escape, walking or cycling past bodies lying on the street and the burnt-out shells of vehicles.

Ukrainian observers say the Kremlin is planning a major propaganda tour this week in which Kyiv is blamed for Oleshky’s destruction. Trump-supporting US media channels have been invited to witness the evacuation, in which buses will take residents to occupied Crimea and Russia.

Ksenia Arkhipova, who fled the city in 2022 and now, as a volunteer, helps others leave, said some residents have told her they fear Russian officials may “filter out” evacuees with pro-Ukraine views.

“People say they are genuinely afraid that the Russians will check their phones and look for evidence of Ukrainian relatives or ties to the Armed Forces of Ukraine – something they have done repeatedly in other occupied territories,” Arkhipova told the Associated Press.

The ceasefire does not extend to other parts of Ukraine. Overnight, Russia bombarded Kyiv with drones and ballistic missiles, killing at least one person and injuring 15. It hit a university campus, wounding students, as well as a bridge over the Dnipro and metro infrastructure. Ten other regions came under fire.

The bridge remains closed to traffic, but some commuters walked across it to get to work. Writing on social media, Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia “must stop dragging out this war” and said he welcomed European attempts to end it.

He said Stubb and Støre had briefed him on their call with Putin. “Alex and Jonas clearly conveyed to the Russian leadership the need for genuine steps towards de-escalation, particularly when it comes to our energy sector and infrastructure,” Zelenskyy wrote.

The Finnish-Norwegian initiative comes amid growing efforts by some European nations to establish a direct channel of communication with Moscow and secure a stronger European voice in negotiations. Stubb and Støre said they took part in the call as leaders of their respective countries and were not speaking on behalf of Europe as a whole.

European governments remain divided over whether direct engagement with Putin is worthwhile, given Russia’s maximalist demands remain stubbornly unchanged. There is little agreement on who should lead such talks. Stubb is seen by some EU officials as a candidate.

The divisions were laid bare shortly after news of the call emerged, when Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, criticised the move. “I am very sceptical about this independent initiative by Norway and Finland. It was not agreed with any of us,” Frederiksen said.

She added: “I see no signs that Russia wants peace with Europe – neither in the hybrid warfare it wages every day, nor in Ukraine, where brutal and aggressive strikes continue daily, claiming many lives.”

Russia’s foreign minister, Sergei Lavrov, said the Europeans had offered “nothing new”, and were simply trying to insert themselves in the negotiation process.

This month Putin said he was willing to resume peace talks, but only on Russia’s hardline terms. They include the handover of the entire eastern Donbas region, including parts Russia has been unable to seize since 2014, as well as further territory in Kherson and Zaporizhzhia provinces.

Last week Zelenskyy said he was open to a peace agreement that would freeze the war along the current frontlines. “Yes, we are ready to stop this war as soon as possible,” he said in an interview with ABC News. He made clear that Ukraine would not recognise land seized and occupied by Russia as Russian.

#63. Apple Creator Studio adds new AI-powered tools, upgrades to Final Cut Pro and Logic Pro

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple is giving the public a taste of its new AI features and powerful devices ahead of tomorrow's "Pro And Tell" event.

The tech giant already announced a new M6-powered, 14-inch MacBook Pro and a new iPad Mini 8, complete with an OLED screen and an internal A20 Pro chip.

And for creators, Apple is sprucing up its Creator Studio to bring more integrated editing tools and even AI assistants to its creative app lineup.

Final Cut Pro will be getting a major upgrade with the addition of MotionVFX, a library of effects, templates, graphics, and tools for rotoscoping, tracking, and color grading, the company announced. Users will be able to access the Content Hub directly in Final Cut Pro, including "Apple-curated stock videos, photos, and backgrounds to fill gaps, add polish, or spark new ideas without leaving their workflow."

Final Cut Pro's workflow will be streamlined, too, including new keyframe editing options and the ability to combine edit sessions across multiple apps.

Apple will bring its new Apple Intelligence model to its suite of creative apps, including Pixelmator Pro's new Generate Fill, which allows users to generate automatic edits and content fills with text-based prompts.

Music editors in Apple's Logic Pro should prepare for a new Liquid Glass interface and an OpenAI collaboration introducing a music-intelligent AI assistant.

The AI, a combination of Apple and OpenAI models, "automatically analyzes, classifies, and tags added sounds so creators can easily search their entire collection or drag in a loop to surface matching audio," Apple explains. There's a new Browser tool to match that centralizes clips, edits, and effects, as well as a new Voice Memos Studio Sound that automatically reduces ambient noise in iPhone recordings and uploads them to Logic Pro.

Apple is also adding new "Producer Packs" with clips and sounds from famed producers, like Sergio George.

The company's AI assistant will also be available on Keynote, Pages, and Numbers to help design and animate slides, draft and edit written content, and build custom charts or graphs.

#564. Studios Get Millions From California’s Tax Credit. But They Balk at the State’s Diversity Initiative

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Criticism of the Golden State's DEI program has emerged as a roadblock amid a global battle to host Hollywood projects.

Opposition is mounting among some major Hollywood studios against the California Film Commission’s diversity initiative designed to encourage more inclusive hiring on movies and TV series.

At the heart of their frustration: concerns that compliance could violate federal law as the government cracks down on diversity programs hailed not too long ago as representing a much-needed step forward in getting films and TV shows to more accurately reflect audiences in California and the rest of the world. In recent years, Disney, Amazon Studios and Skydance, among others, have quietly retreated from diversity efforts, like tying staffing goals to race, ethnicity and gender.

Participation in the state’s diversity initiative, which gives movies and TV shows a small bump in their overall tax credit, isn’t mandatory. Productions, however, risk leaving money on the table by choosing not to opt in. Over the course of multiple films, it can net studios a hefty sum. For California, criticism of the program has emerged as yet another roadblock to restoring production in the state amid a global tit-for-tat battle to host Hollywood.

The pushback to the initiative climaxed in June at a commission meeting, reviewed by The Hollywood Reporter, in which board members employed by the studios and unions expressed significant reservations about whether it should move forward as currently constructed because of legal and administrative issues. Arturo Barquet, a board member and CFO of Global Production Operations for Universal Filmed Entertainment, said that Universal has “many times considered whether we should opt out.”

“There are regulations — there are things in here that are in conflict with federal law,” he added.

Universal exec Barquet pointed to Skydance, which he said isn’t participating in the initiative as studios look to avoid clashing with the government. “My understanding is that Paramount is not going for the diversity program,” the exec noted at the June 5 meeting.

Skydance does not have a blanket policy against participating in the diversity initiative and a number of shows under review by the commission are being considered, a person familiar with the situation tells THR.

Under the construction of the California film incentive program, movies and TV shows must opt into the initiative to maximize filming subsidies. Gov. Gavin Newsom has championed the incentives and signed a bill to double them from $330 million to $750 million last year. The program doesn’t carry diversity quotas and rewards good-faith efforts by studios to boost minority representation on productions. It was constructed, in part, to sidestep federal law believed to prohibit hiring targets by rewarding productions that comply rather than punishing those that do not.

During the June meeting, Fanshen Cox, former manager of the diversity program, stressed that minority representation in Hollywood has taken a step back in recent years.

“Instead of raising the bar, we are now seeing productions fall below it,” she said. “This is not progress. This is a reversal.”

UCLA documented that backslide in a report earlier this year when it noted, for the first time in three years, that the proportion of minority lead actors on streaming fell below their proportion of the U.S. population, from a high of 51 percent in 2024 to 36 percent in 2025. Streaming films featuring casts with a majority of minorities also slipped from 41 percent to roughly 26 percent.

Another study from ¡Pa’lante!, a Latino representation initiative from the USC Norman Lear Center, found that Latinos only accounted for six percent of all roles across the top broadcast series, despite making up nearly 20 percent of the country’s total population and 41 percent of California’s population.

“We’re seeing what happens when our finger gets taken off the pulse,” said Colleen Bell, executive director of the California Film Commission, about the figures.

The productions that drop out of the diversity initiative also do so because of the administrative process, which requires attending orientation sessions and filling out voluminous paperwork, among other things. It’s believed to be overly burdensome by several of the studios.

Still, 105 of 115 productions opted into the diversity provisions across the last eight application windows, according to the commission.

California began collecting information in 2020 about diversity for films and TV shows tapped to receive filming subsidies, requiring productions to report the racial, ethnic and gender makeup of their workforces. The state last year expanded the requirements, making productions eligible for the full value of their tax credits only if they also comply with a more extensive process setting and assessing diversity goals.

Under the expanded rules, productions must submit a checklist covering hiring, workplace education, workforce development and supplier diversity. They must also hand over a plan outlining specific efforts to broaden access to jobs, an interim assessment before filming begins and a final assessment after completing production. The initiative does not include racial quotas or hiring targets; instead, it evaluates whether a production has made a good-faith effort to pursue the goals in its blueprint. Productions that opt out of the additional requirements, or fail to meet the standard, still qualify for 96 percent of their allocated credit rather than the full amount.

Diversity programs requiring applicants to come from certain racial or ethnic groups have long been on legally tenuous ground, but the nail in the coffin came in 2023 when the Supreme Court struck down race-conscious admissions in colleges. Since then, there’s been heightened scrutiny around such initiatives. A year after the decision, a script coordinator for SEAL Team who was denied a staff writer job sued CBS Studios and Paramount for allegedly carrying diversity quotas that discriminate against white men. General counsels at studios have pushed to keep race out of hiring decisions. Policies meant to encourage more inclusive hiring now often employ read-between-the-lines placeholders like “underrepresented” or “underserved” or “unique experiences.”

Part of the Barquet’s grievance also stemmed from its belief that an untitled movie from Daniel Kawn and Daniel Scheinert had complied with the program’s requirements but was rejected three times. Universal Pictures’ Snoop Dogg biopic, he added, was rejected twice before a recent greenlight. The exec indicated that he didn’t know why these titles were initially rejected from qualifying for the state’s diversity initiative. Snoop is expected to receive $17 million in tax credits from the California Film Commission, though the figure will come in lower if the production fails to complete the program (the commission hasn’t approved Snoop yet but is expected to do so, according to a person familiar with the matter).

Some studios believe they should get credited for complying with California’s overall goal to boost diversity in the Hollywood workforce because they still maintain diversity program even though particular productions may not have sufficiently diverse cast and crew or made good-faith efforts to achieve diversity goals.

At the board meeting, Andrew Davis, Sony Pictures’ president of production administration, echoed Universal’s criticism of the diversity initiatives’ construction, saying that studios want to participate in the program but that compliance is a “tremendous burden on both television and features.” Thom Davis, the head of IATSE Local 80, argued that diversity initiatives clash with the longtime process of placing members into productions.

“All this work and all these resources put into [studio diversity] programs are getting no credit and are actually being discouraged,” Davis said. “We’ve got people that come in through the union side who tell us they aren’t getting on” because productions need to prioritize compliance at times.

By Cox’s thinking, however, increasing minority representation in Hollywood means giving a share of work to Californians looking for a foothold in the industry who also reflect the diversity of the state.

“All these jobs are coming back, and they’re still going to the same people,” Cox, who no longer works with the commission, says. She adds, “This is a system-wide problem. Ultimately, it’s the studios that need to do the work.”

In a statement, Bell stressed that the commission is in regular talks with the studios on the issue. “Our goal hasn’t changed: keep California the best place to make film and television, with opportunity open to everyone who wants to build a career here,” she said.
