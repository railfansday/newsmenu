#5457. All aboard! Massachusetts is getting 10 new electric transit trains

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) board of directors voted unanimously Thursday to approve the purchase of a new generation of battery-powered public transit trains – and they can’t arrive soon enough.

“(MBTA) has, obviously, an immediate need for locomotives,” explains William Wolfgang, the MBTA’s senior director of vehicle engineering. The new machines will replace the oldest locomotives in the MBTA fleet, GP40s built more than 50 years ago and F40s that are well over 30 years old. In a presentation to the MTBA board, MTBA said 13 of the GP40s have, “exceeded their useful life,” while the F40s will reach the end of their life between 2029 and 2035.

The MBTA will put in an order for 20 Siemens-built “Charger” locomotives; 10 Tier 4 diesel-powered hybrid electric units and 10 dual-mode battery/catenary electric units – and, beyond trackside power for charging the batteries, there are other challenges presented by going electric, most notably using existing cab cars to control the battery-electric units. “We’re going to have to work with railroad operations, engineering, and the Brotherhood of [Locomotive Engineers and Trainmen] to try and determine the best way to handle that,” adds Wolfgang. “That will be an upcoming change order once we get into the design review process.”

The total bill comes out to $310.4 million. A combination of state and federal funding will pay for the order, including a recent $200 million transfer from the Massachusetts Department of Transportation.

The contract includes an option for additional locomotives, although details were not discussed during the meeting. Delivery of the new Siemens Charger trains is set to begin in 2029, with all 20 set to be in service by the end of 2030. Siemens has delivered 440 Charger model hybrid- and battery-electric locomotives globally at the time of this writing.

#5343. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/bon-secours-mercy-health-acquire-majority-stake-surgery-partners" hreflang="en">Bon Secours Mercy Health to acquire majority stake in Surgery Partners in $5.7B deal</a>

Bon Secours Mercy Health to acquire majority stake in Surgery Partners in $5.7B deal" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Bon Secours Mercy Health is acquiring a majority share in healthcare services company Surgery Partners in a take-private deal valuing the acquiree at $5.7 billion, the organizations announced Monday morning.

The transaction is expected to close in the first quarter of next year, pending approvals, and with over 200 locations in tow would help the major nonprofit health system create “one of the country’s largest outpatient surgical care platforms,” they said.

“As healthcare continues to evolve, it is important to grow in ways that allow us to serve more people, in more communities and through a broader range of care settings,” John M. Starcher, Jr., president and CEO of Bon Secours Mercy Health, said in the announcement. “Our relationship with Surgery Partners represents an important step toward advancing that commitment."

Currently, affiliates of Bain Capital Private Equity control about 39% of the public Surgery Partners. Under the terms of the deal, Bain Capital would retain and rollover those shares, while HealthSpan Partners, an affiliate of Bon Secours, would purchase the remainders with cash at $23.50 per share.

Surgery Partners’ short-stay surgical platform includes ambulatory surgery centers, surgical hospitals and about 5,000 affiliated physicians spanning 30 states. It reported $3.31 billion in revenue during 2025 and $0.47 adjusted earnings per share.

The company has been public since 2015, with a 2017 merger making it the largest standalone, independent surgical facilities operator at the time. Bain Capital made an offer in January 2025 to fully acquire the shares it didn’t own for $25.75 apiece (roughly $6.7 billion enterprise valuation), though that offer was rejected a few months later.

Surgery Partners has also been slimming down some of its acute care holdings in recent months, a strategic move its leadership said would better position the company as a purely short-stay surgical provider while limiting its exposure to Medicaid.

“We believe this transaction represents the best path forward for Surgery Partners, delivering an attractive all-cash premium to our shareholders while creating lasting benefits for our patients, physicians and other stakeholders,” Surgery Partners CEO Eric Evans said Monday.

Bon Secours Mercy Health is a Catholic nonprofit with 47 hospitals, more than 1,200 care sites and nearly 60,000 employees. It reported just shy of $14 billion in total operating revenue through 2025, as well as $324 million of recurring operating income (2.3% recurring operating margin) and a $1.1 billion bottom line. Of note, its holdings include 41% ownership in revenue cycle management company Ensemble Health Partners, for which a summer strategic recapitalization transaction with a new private equity investor injected $670.8 million cash into the system, per recent financial statements.

Bon Secours’ HealthSpan Partners is financing the Surgery Partners purchase with a combination of cash on hand and newly secured debt. Upon closing, the parties plan to keep Surgery Partners’ operations, brand and headquarters in place, while also keeping the business as a secular organization.

Strategically, the deal is evidence of large health systems’ growing investments in ambulatory care networks, where many expect to see rising demand as consumers prioritize lower-cost care settings. Healthcare executives have bluntly described these investments as a “top priority,” particularly as regulatory and policy activity such as the wind-down of Medicare’s inpatient-only list pushes care outside of the hospital.

“As more care moves beyond the hospital setting, patients are looking for high-quality care that fits more seamlessly into their lives,” Wael Haidar, M.D., Bon Secours Mercy Health’s president of clinical operations, said in the announcement. “Surgery Partners understands that care should be designed around the needs of patients, and that approach aligns closely with our mission. Together, we have an opportunity to expand access to high-quality outpatient care while supporting physicians and helping patients receive the right care, in the right setting, at the right time.”

The most apparent analogue for Bon Secours’ deal comes from Ascension, an even larger Catholic nonprofit, which closed a $3.9 billion deal over the summer to acquire AMSURG, an ambulatory surgery management services company with over 300 locations. Ascension CEO Eduardo Conrado has described that deal as both a means to flesh out his system’s existing markets—improving overall volumes and allowing hospitals to focus on high-complexity patients—and a way to strike new business partnerships with other local health systems in markets beyond its prior footprint.

#399. Exercise doesn't just strengthen your heart. It rewires it

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Exercise does more than make the heart stronger. New research suggests it also physically reshapes the nerves that control the heart, revealing an unexpected difference between the left and right sides of the body.

Scientists at the University of Bristol (UK) discovered that moderate aerobic exercise changes the structure of nerve cells involved in regulating heart activity. Even more surprisingly, the changes were dramatically different on the two sides of the body. The findings, published in Autonomic Neuroscience, could eventually help doctors develop more precise treatments for irregular heartbeats, chest pain, angina, and stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome.

The discovery was made in rats, so further research is needed to determine whether the same changes occur in humans. Nevertheless, it offers a new perspective on how physical activity influences the nervous system that keeps the heart functioning.

Exercise Changes the Heart's Hidden Control System

The heart is regulated partly by the autonomic nervous system, a network of nerves that automatically manages essential functions such as heartbeat, breathing, and digestion. One branch of this system helps the heart beat faster when the body needs more oxygen, such as during exercise or stressful situations.

Small clusters of nerve cells called stellate ganglia play an important role in this process. Located on both sides of the lower neck and upper chest, these nerve hubs send signals that influence heart rate and other aspects of heart function.

The new research suggests that regular exercise changes these nerve clusters in ways scientists had not previously recognized.

Study lead author Dr. Augusto Coppi, Senior Lecturer in Veterinary Anatomy at the University of Bristol, said: "The discovery points to a previously hidden left-right pattern in the body's 'autopilot' system that helps run the heart.

"These nerve clusters act like the heart's dimmer switch and we've shown that regular, moderate exercise remodels that switch in a side-specific way. This could help explain why some treatments work better on one side than the other and, in future, help doctors target therapies more precisely and effectively."

Just 10 Weeks of Exercise Produced Striking Nerve Changes

To investigate how exercise affects these nerve clusters, the researchers examined rats that underwent 10 weeks of moderate aerobic training. Aerobic exercise is physical activity that increases heart rate and breathing, such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling.

The study involved researchers from University College London (UCL) in the UK, the University of São Paulo (USP), and the Federal University of São Paulo (UNIFESP) in Brazil.

The team used advanced three-dimensional analysis techniques known as stereology to examine the nerve cells. This approach allows scientists to estimate the number, size, and distribution of cells within tissues.

The results revealed an unusual imbalance between the two sides of the body. In the trained rats, the cardiovascular nerve cluster on the right contained roughly four times as many neurons as the cluster on the left, a striking difference from the pattern observed in untrained rats.

The nerve cells also changed in size, but in opposite directions. Neurons on the left nearly doubled in size, while those on the right became slightly smaller.

These findings suggest that exercise does not affect all the nerves controlling the heart equally. Instead, it produces distinct structural changes on each side, potentially providing clues about how the nervous system adapts to regular physical activity.

Could the Discovery Lead to Better Heart Treatments?

The findings may have important implications for treating conditions involving abnormal nerve activity around the heart.

Arrhythmias occur when the heart beats too quickly, too slowly, or irregularly. Angina is chest pain or discomfort typically caused by insufficient blood flow to the heart muscle. Another condition, commonly called 'broken-heart' syndrome, involves a sudden weakening of the heart muscle that is often triggered by intense emotional or physical stress.

In some patients, doctors treat these problems by reducing excessive nerve signals reaching the heart. Procedures such as stellate ganglion nerve blocks temporarily interrupt nerve activity, while denervation involves disrupting certain nerve pathways.

Understanding how the left and right nerve clusters differ could eventually help physicians determine which side should be targeted for the greatest benefit.

Dr. Coppi explained: "Irregular heart rhythms, known as arrhythmias, stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome, and certain types of chest pain are often treated by dialing down overactive stellate ganglia -- the paired small nerve hubs in the lower neck/upper chest area that send 'go faster' signals to the heart.

"By mapping how exercise changes these ganglia on each side, the study offers clues that could one day fine-tune procedures like nerve blocks or denervation to the side most likely to help. The findings are early-stage and in rats, so clinical studies would need to follow."

What's Next for Exercise and Heart Research?

Although the structural changes were substantial, scientists still need to establish exactly how they affect heart function.

The research team plans to investigate whether the differences between the left and right nerve clusters influence how the heart behaves during rest and physical activity. They also intend to look for similar patterns in other animal models and eventually in humans.

For human studies, the researchers hope to use noninvasive markers, which could provide information about nerve activity without requiring surgery or tissue removal.

If the same left-right differences are found in people, the discovery could help guide more personalized treatments for arrhythmias, difficult-to-treat angina, and stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome.

Dr. Coppi added: "Understanding these left-right differences could help us personalize treatments for heart rhythm disorders and angina. Our next step is to test how these structural changes map onto function and whether similar patterns appear in larger animals and humans."

#4079. 32-year-old "ellipsoid" horror game Ecstatica gets a PC re-release today, showing us an alternative timeline in which polygonal 3D never became dominant

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One of my favourite hobbies is digging up older pieces of gaming tech or software know-how and asking myself: "man, what would the world have been like, if every GPU were made out of Gorgonzola?" Or less silly words to that effect. Nostalgia aside, it's useful to revisit historical alternatives in the face of very rich people who would have us believe that only their trillion-dollar boondoggle is "the" future.

The code running 1994 horror game Ecstatica probably isn't going to topple Unreal Engine anytime soon, but look at this article's header image, and tell me you aren't fascinated. Why is everything so… pert? I will tell you why: Ecstatica's graphics make heavy use of ellipsoids. They're the basis for everything from werewolf buttocks to the grain of castle flagstones. You can experience this terrifying bubble-wrap reality for yourself in today's Steam and GOG re-release of both Ecstatica and its sequel, care of SNEG Games.

I myself know about Ecstatica thanks to Jasper Byrne, developer of the brilliant Lone Survivor, whom I spoke to yonks ago about "de-making" and monolithic progress narratives. Byrne mentioned it as an example of a road-not-taken in videogame graphics production and aesthetics. Or at least, a road-not-taken-very-far.

Created by Andrew Spencer Studios, Ecstatica uses ellipsoids with actual volumes for characters, most objects, and much of the environment - all of it running on the CPU. It schlops and schlurps the ellipsoids together into more complex, rounded masses. Look at the slightly nauseating ovoid grit of those dungeon walls. It's like somebody wallpapered their kid's bedroom with xenomorph eggs. Ugh, I'm getting Yedoma Globula flashbacks.

As such, Ecstatica cuts against what would become the dominant trend of using polygons for 3D visuals, though the game's engine is capable of rendering polygons for square or flat objects such as doors and crosses. As for what you actually do, in this appallingly bulbous simulation: you are an unfortunate wanderer who's strayed into a village full of monsters. There are many ways to die. Released two years before the original Resident Evil, Ecstatica is an early example of fixed-perspective survival horror, splintering off from adventure games. It's puzzle-driven with a lot of "absurd British humour", as the Steam and GOG storepages for the re-release explain.

I'm not sure who exactly worked on this port, but publishers SNEG were founded in 2020 by former GOG staffers, with the goal of restoring or, at least, republishing and resurfacing various forgotten PC games. The Ecstatica rebuild includes controller support and three graphics modes, changeable on the fly: one for the original DOS version of the game, one for the 1997 re-release on Windows 95, and a "real-time high resolution render". I'm not sure if any of the original devs are involved - they broke up after Ecstatica 2, midway through development of their third game Urban Decay. The latter was also ellipsoid-based, but the devs switched to a more familiar polygonal look before the project ran out of gas.
