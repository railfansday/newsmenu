#5045. Anthropic IPO Filing Puts “ANT” on Nasdaq: Could the Claude Maker Debut Bigger Than Bitcoin?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic IPO: the Claude maker files its S-1 to list on Nasdaq as ANT. Polymarket gives 74.5% it passes Bitcoin’s value this year.

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, publicly filed for its IPO with the SEC on Monday, October 26, applying to list Class A shares on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker “ANT”. It could be the largest stock market debut on record, yet the filing leaves the price range blank. The numbers inside it are anything but.

The Anthropic IPO prospectus shows revenue of $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier. And prediction markets now price a 74.5% chance that Anthropic will be worth more than Bitcoin by the end of the year. So what did the S-1 reveal, and what could a listing this size mean for crypto?

Anthropic has filed an S-1 registration statement with the SEC related to its proposed IPO.

A company growing faster than its losses. The S-1 shows revenue rising from $4.6 billion in all of 2025 to $16.3 billion in the first six months of 2026, a fourteen-fold jump on the same half a year earlier.

The headline loss looks alarming: $80.7 billion for the half. But $72.4 billion of it is a non-cash charge, the rising fair value of convertible notes and SAFEs issued to early backers as the company’s valuation climbed. The operating loss, what the business itself lost, narrowed to $693 million from $2.1 billion a year earlier.

Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and J.P. Morgan top a list of 22 underwriters. The price range, share count and the size of the raise will come in a later amendment.

Not the buyers of the shares. Public investors get Class A stock with one vote per share, but a single Class F share, held by a company owned by the seven co-founders, including CEO Dario Amodei and President Daniela Amodei, carries 50.1% of the votes.

A separate body, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, will elect a majority of the board through its own Class T share. Anthropic will also be a “controlled company” under Nasdaq rules. As a public benefit corporation, it must weigh its mission of developing AI safely alongside shareholder returns, and it has already placed 5% of its shares in a new Public Benefit Fund.

The IPO date will be most likely in November. On Polymarket, traders give an 83.5% chance that the shares start trading by November 30, and about 50% by November 20.

The valuation is the bigger question. Anthropic was last valued at $965 billion in its $65 billion Series H round in May. Polymarket’s most likely IPO valuation range is now $1.5 trillion to $1.75 trillion, at 58%, close to double that private price only five months later.

The market thinks so. Bitcoin’s market capitalisation stands at about $1.61 trillion at $80,100, and Polymarket gives 74.5% that Anthropic’s value passes it by December 31. Traders put the most weight on a first-day closing value of $1.75 trillion to $2 trillion.

For crypto, the filing was a non-event on the screen. Bitcoin slipped 0.3% on the day and AI tokens barely moved: Bittensor’s TAO was up 1.2% and Render 2.2%. The risk comes later. A raise of this size will need buyers, and the same growth investors who hold Bitcoin and AI tokens may sell some to make room for ANT.

Bitcoin is trading just below its 50-day EMA at $80,801. Resistance sits at $82,850, where every bounce stalled last week, and support at $79,221, Saturday’s low. A close below that floor would put the 200-day EMA at $76,347 in play.

The Anthropic IPO will be the first time public investors can buy a stake in a frontier AI lab directly. If it prices near the top of Polymarket’s range, the company behind Claude will open its first day of trading worth more than the world’s largest cryptocurrency.

#5061. 'Privacy Horror’: Researcher Uncovers Extent of Samsung Smart TV User Tracking

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

'Privacy Horror’: Researcher Uncovers Extent of Samsung Smart TV User Tracking

A researcher found that their Samsung smart TV was tracking them through several functionalities, including an "always on” microphone—and experts warn that similar tools could exist on other "everyday devices."

X user log4j claims to have reverse engineered his Samsung smart TV's firmware and discovered what they call a "privacy horror." One file called 'security-policy.xml’ featured functions including an always-on microphone, automatic content recognition, geo-positioning, network mapping, a targeted ads service, and user profile building, while Samsung TVs without a built-in microphone will look for one on connected devices such as a soundbar or Bluetooth speaker.

But a community note on the post points out that Samsung openly advertises some of these features. Its Pet Care and Family Care tools on Vision AI TVs listen for sounds like barking or crying and alert owners through the SmartThings app.

i own a relatively new (2025) samsung tv.

i tried reversing the firmware but it was aes encrypted and no keys unless tear it apart and do uart/jtag magic.

zero days it is. root shell on new samsung TVs!

discovered PRIVACY HORROR. write up? pic.twitter.com/EsxxCy1b6a

James Bore, a cybersecurity and technology consultant, told Decrypt that this isn't a new finding but just adding awareness to something that most people weren't aware of.

"The amount of monitoring that smart devices, from TVs to voice assistants to cars, do isn't secret as such, just not something promoted," Bore said. "If you read privacy policies and [End-User License Agreements], it's all in there in almost every case, but most people don't read those."

Jake Moore, Global Cybersecurity Advisor at cybersecurity firm ESET, told Decrypt that this could be the start of a wave of similar findings, as a trend of AI-powered reverse engineering has swept the internet.

"AI-assisted reverse engineering may make it easier and faster for researchers to uncover and explain how devices collect, process, and share data," Bore agreed, "but the underlying collection itself is generally not new or unknown."

Decrypt has reached out to Samsung for comment and will update this article should they respond.

Bore added that smart TVs are monitoring user behavior to advertise to you better and persuade you to spend money. He added that this is "far from unique” to smart TVs.

Moore noted that this might be why TVs haven't seen a noticeable price increase in recent years.

"TVs haven’t really increased in price like some products over the last two decades and that may be because customer data collection is keeping those costs down," Moore said. "Smart TVs are doing far more behind the scenes than most people realise, as many can monitor what we are watching and collect our data to build profiles for targeted advertising."

Last week, smart TV owners started using AI coding agents like Claude Code to strip ads and tracking software out of their sets. One open-source project, Cleanyourtv, now lists tested setups for 107 TV models from 37 brands, though it only fully works on Android-based TVs—not Samsung’s Tizen operating system. However, as one Decrypt writer discovered, vibe-coded solutions can end up backfiring.

Both cybersecurity experts say that the public shouldn't be uniquely concerned about the functionalities revealed on Samsung TVs. Bore said that we should be more worried about how "blasé” we've become about accepting companies "constantly profiling us to manipulate us into spending more money on things we don't need."

"The concern is how much of our data is being collected and who it’s being shared with," Moore said. "It is possible to go through TV settings to minimise the amount of data access points are being used and analysed, but saying that, privacy shouldn’t be something you have to hunt through dozens of settings to find."

Those more anxious about security claim that this form of invasive data collection is enough for them to change their behavior.

Muneeb Ali, the co-founder of Stacks, a prominent Bitcoin layer-2 network, said that he doesn't handle private crypto keys in rooms with devices.

"We were paranoid that a hacked device could record or listen in," he tweeted. "Didn’t realize the TVs were literally 1st party programmed for full-on spying. Only the paranoid survive."

#2279. Who should win the Ballon d'Or 2026? Harry Kane, Lamine Yamal, Michael Olise backed for glory

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Ben Knapton | 26 Oct 2026 15:00

Ballon d'Or day is finally upon us, and in just a few hours time, fans across the globe will know just who the best footballer in the world is - at least according to the esteemed France Football panel.

To celebrate the 70th anniversary of Sir Stanley Matthews winning the inaugural accolade, the 2026 Ballon d'Or has been moved from its usual home of Paris to the English capital of London, and one of the Three Lions' own could fittingly waltz up to the stage to perform his winner's speech.

Indeed, Bayern Munich marksman Harry Kane is the bookies' favourite to be crowned England's first Ballon dOr winner since a fledgling Michael Owen in 2001, but if some of us had our way, he would miss out on this individual honour.

Here, the Sports Mole team selects who they think should be crowned the winner of the 2026 Ballon d'Or.

Not since Lionel Messi's 2019 triumph has the Ballon d'Or been awarded to a player who failed to win any of the World Cup, Euros, Copa America or Champions League, but I am fully expecting that streak to be snapped this time around.

While not quite matching Lionel Messi's astronomical 91 goals from the 2012 calendar year, Harry Kane racked up an outrageous 73 for club and country in the 2025-26 campaign, 61 of which came in Bayern colours.

Taking home a second European Golden Shoe and becoming the first English player in 98 years to hit a 60+ goal season - emulating the iconic Dixie Dean - Kane reaffirmed his status as the best goalscorer on the planet, even if his World Cup numbers did not suggest to.

Bayern's UCL failure, England's World Cup disappointment and Kane only finishing joint-fourth in the Mundial scoring charts will all work against him, but so will Lamine Yamal's underwhelming individual World Cup campaign and Kylian Mbappe's Champions League and La Liga-less season.

The World Cup has not come home for England in the 21st century, but the Ballon d'Or surely will for the leader of the Three Lions.

After more than a decade watching Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo dominate the football award landscape, the race for the Ballon d’Or has opened up in recent years and there is no undisputed frontrunner for the coveted 2026 prize.

However, Harry Kane stands out ahead of players including Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe as someone who deserves recognition for his remarkable influence as Bayern Munich and England’s talisman.

While winning a World Cup or Champions League carries a lot of weight in the Ballon d’Or votes, I think the influence a player can have on a team should also be recognised for this prestigious accolade, and with someone like Kane, you just wonder whether Bayern and England would have been so successful without him and his goals? Probably not.

At 33 years of age, Kane is at the peak of his powers, scoring a whopping 54 goals for club and country this calendar year, helping Bayern win back-to-back Bundesliga titles and inspiring England to the World Cup semi-finals.

His leadership, match-winning reliability, footballing intelligence and ridiculously clinical goalscoring record at the highest level makes him a deserving winner – potentially the first Englishman since Michael Owen in 2001 to claim the golden trophy.

As impressive as Harry Kane, Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal all were for club and country, I think Michael Olise should win the Ballon d’Or. He stepped up in nearly every game, big or small, for Bayern Munich and France and was rarely, if ever, shut down. Not only did he have a record-setting World Cup campaign for assists, but he was the catalyst for his team’s success, just as he was in Bayern’s marvellous campaigns in Europe and domestically.

I feel the number of goals you score often matters more than creativity in voting, but the difficulty of many of Olise’s passes to Kane and Mbappe was often extraordinary and should be taken into consideration. You can reasonably argue that neither of those players would have been as potent in front of goal without Olise’s hard work in providing them with the perfect service and placing the ball in the ideal position for them to score. Without Olise, I don’t see either of them being quite as strong.

I don’t know if he’ll get the votes because he doesn’t score as much, but I don’t think anyone in the world is as good on the ball, as precise in tight spaces, or as tough to dispossess as Olise. If you evaluate what he did for his two teams and how successful his team’s were, than it should be Olise winning this award. I don’t think either Bayern or France would have achieved as much without Olise on the pitch. If we're evaluating a player’s overall game, their impact on the team, and the trophies they've won, Olise ticks all those boxes and deserves the Ballon d’Or.

You could make a case for and against each of the perceived top three contenders, making this year’s award rather interesting. Lamine Yamal had a top season but did not pull up trees at the World Cup, and Barcelona could not go all the way in the Champions League, while Kylian Mbappe had an outstanding individual season, culminating in becoming the top goalscorer in World Cup history, but won no major title with club or country. Harry Kane had a storming season with Bayern and did okay to a point at the World Cup, but the Bundesliga is often looked down upon, potentially hurting his prospects. Nonetheless, yours truly has always had a soft spot for the Bayern forward and would want him to claim the award. Heart vs. head. Heart wins.

Despite having a less-than-exemplary World Cup, he did still contribute to Spain's victory despite rushing back from injury, and he was central to Barcelona's La Liga success. He is arguably the most naturally gifted footballer in the world at the minute and so it makes him the best currently playing.

The Bayern Munich marksman netted 73 goals in 65 appearances across all competitions for club and country last season while also winning a league and cup double.

He also captained England to a third-place finish at the World Cup with six goals in seven matches.

The 2026 Ballon d'Or feels like one of the most open races ever but I think Kane is the most deserving winner.

The Ballon d’Or has historically been awarded to players that have won one of the game’s biggest trophies in the selection period, but Harry Kane deserves to claim the prize without either the Champions League or World Cup to his name.

Lamine Yamal made headlines as he lifted the World Cup with Spain, though the reality is that the 19-year-old was largely ineffective in the summer, scoring just once during the 4-0 group victory over Saudi Arabia and failing to provide a single assist after heading into the tournament with an injury.

By contrast, Kane bagged six goals and created another for his teammates, despite falling short of the crown.

Yamal has a case for having made the best start to the 2026-27 campaign, but for the selection period, it is difficult to overlook Kane’s staggering 73 goals and eight assists in 65 matches - even if football is about more than just raw output.

For comparison, the Englishman found the back of the net 61 times for Bayern Munich last term, far beyond the 42 that Kylian Mbappe mustered for Real Madrid and 38 Erling Haaland converted for Manchester City, while eclipsing Yamal’s 24 strikes for Barcelona.

Plus, Meyers-Shyer had always been a visual creature: Before taking a professional break to care for her young children, she served as an accessories editor at Harper’s Bazaar and then as West Coast public relations manager at Chanel. “I put up a little thing and I said, ‘I created this account. Maybe I’m going to post, maybe I’m not. Come follow.’ That was the end of it.”

Spoiler alert: That was not the end of it. Three years later, This Oak House has more than 254,000 followers and counting—as well as a newly published book, This Oak House: A Renovation Story With Interiors to Inspire Your Own Home.

In this late-stage social media era, where Instagram is beyond saturated with influencers clamoring for likes, it’s difficult to build such a large following in a short amount of time. Yet it’s no surprise that Meyers-Shyer found her niche. For starters, she had zero interest in spon-con. “I wasn’t motivated in the way I think a lot of traditional influencers are. I was just sharing the process. People didn’t see me hawking products and things like that. I think there was an authenticity which people appreciated,” she says. Secondly? She’s a Meyers.

Annie Meyers-Shyer and her husband, Robby Koch's, home in Los Angeles. It inspired Meyers-Shyer's Instagram account, This Oak House.

Her mother is Nancy Meyers, the director behind films like The Holiday, Something’s Gotta Give, It’s Complicated, and The Parent Trap, and cowriter of Father of the Bride. They’re beloved by millions—both because of their delightful rom-com plots (who didn’t fall in love with Jude Law in The Holiday?) but also because of the worlds they create. Meyers’s movies are known for their cozy, quintessential California interiors, with bright, airy kitchens and gently sumptuous living rooms of white and cream. They’re perennially trending: In 2024, Pinterest announced that searches for “Nancy Meyers living room” were up 2,090%. In September, TikTok sent out a press blast announcing that searches for “Nancy Meyers Aesthetic” had recently risen 145%. “Nancy Meyers aesthetic is gaining traction on TikTok, with users embracing the cozy, nostalgic feel of her iconic films,” said a statement from the social media platform. Meyers-Shyer gets it. “I always found it interesting in her movies how the sets really further told the story of the character. You could learn so much more about the character by their home. What did the Father of the Bride house tell you about that family, or what did the in-laws’ house tell you about them? You could really learn. And so I think that’s what great houses do. They tell you about the person who lives inside of them.”

But here’s the thing. Nancy Meyers makes sets—temporary structures dismantled after a director yells, “That’s a wrap.” Meyers-Shyer, however, knows how to build the permanent thing. “I’m sure there are people who love my mom and her style and they came to me [because] she doesn’t really talk about that world that much.” And This Oak House gets down to the granular: There’s a whole page, for example, dedicated to the best paints. (Like Farrow & Ball Slipper Satin or Benjamin Moore Swiss Coffee.) Another section focuses on the best types of marble and the different types of wood.

Meyers-Shyer wasn’t looking to intentionally mimic her mom’s interiors. She emulates her mom in the way we’re all influenced by the way we grew up: Meyers brought her to markets in Provence, so she, too, fell in love with markets of Provence, and so on. Her parents, as many do, passed down some furniture and paintings. (“Every time I open the front door, I see it and it reminds me of my dad,” she says of one piece of art.)

This Oak House is half a how-to on how to achieve the elevated, cozy California style her mother Nancy Meyers often shows in her movies.

And it should be said the now famous Nancy Meyers aesthetic was influenced by someone else: Nancy’s mother (and Annie’s grandmother) who was an interior designer. “She was always refinishing something or moving furniture around,” Meyers-Shyer recalls. And her great-grandmother ran an antique store. Their decor taste has been passed down through the generations, descendant by descendant. “It’s a long lineage,” Meyers-Shyer says. So perhaps the “Nancy Meyers aesthetic” isn’t quite right: the “Meyers aesthetic” is.

Meyers-Shyer and I met up in West Hollywood, with a very specific agenda: Could she show me how one gets the Meyers aesthetic?

Not in a “do it for less” or “dupes” kind of way. Where does she shop? Where does her mom shop? What secret places—and people—make up the backbone of these magical California worlds the Meyerses create? (Look, if I was part of the Meyers female empire and the financial boon that came with it, I’d want the good stuff.)

Our first stop is Rose Tarlow House. Rose Tarlow is an IYKYK name—she might never have appeared on Million Dollar Decorators or had a line with Crate & Barrel, but she’s quietly one of the most influential names in interior decorating, designing the homes of David Geffen and Oprah Winfrey. (And not many more: Tarlow is known for keeping an extremely small client base.) “Tarlow has solidified her industry status as a true decorator’s decorator,” Architectural Digest says of her, and she is known for her use of wood and embrace of solid, neutral colors.

During Meyers-Shyer’s renovation, she used to spend hours at Rose Tarlow, skimming through the calm, earthy fabrics. (She selected Avignon Alpine Stripe Natural for her dining room curtains.) “She is the master. The absolute master,” says Meyers-Shyer. Last summer, Meyers-Shyer, her mother, and Tarlow went shopping in Provence. “Basically everything she pointed at and told me to get, I purchased,” she said.

It’s a short drive to Mike Moser’s studio on La Cienega. A minor character in This Oak House, Moser is Meyers-Shyer’s interior designer. She learned of him through her friend, Jamie Mizrahi, who styles celebrities like Adele, Jennifer Lawrence, and Jeremy Allen White. “What I loved about Jamie’s house was that it felt like Jamie, and that’s kind of Mike’s secret sauce,” she tells me as we meander through his space, which is half design office, half shop. Moser’s husband, Tyler Thomas, greets her warmly. They make small talk about their landscaping. Meyers-Shyer jokes that on a recent Ask Me Anything, she was asked to describe her landscape. “I wanted it to be like, ‘Pain in the ass, weedy, impossible to keep up,’” she says, laughing. “[I have to be] out there every day.” They then both ooh and ahh over a rare Warren McArthur sofa that Thomas and Moser have tracked down—their studio both designs furniture and sources it. In the coming weeks, they’ll be in Italy for a friend’s wedding. They’ll sneak in antique shopping when they are there. A lot of their stuff, however, they get at auctions. (As does Meyers-Shyer, who laughs when she tells me she has a terrible penchant for buying something on the cheap at a European auction—but then paying a small fortune in shipping. “The more difficult and expensive it is to get here, usually the better. I buy the item for $15,” she says.)

Her mom, meanwhile, is a client of Mark D. Sikes, known for his work redecorating the East Wing under first lady Jill Biden. “I think I hesitate to do color because I don’t know that I’d ultimately be happy with it,” Meyers told Architectural Digest of her light and creamy Los Angeles home. Sikes commended her attention to design details: “We may have looked at 50,000 different ivory linens.”

Then we’re off to Nickey Kehoe, the cult home store founded by Todd Nickey and Amy Kehoe. Nickey Kehoe sells things you didn’t even know you needed: a bronze bottle opener shaped like a mermaid, vintage steel lamps, a mirror with ceramic lemons by artist Charlie Dumais, and a 1950s red Chinois bar cabinet. “I love these chair paintings, by the way. This is from an artist in Provence whose work I just am crazy about,” Meyers-Shyer says as we pass a wall of oil paintings. She pauses by a collection of neutral welcome mats. “Do they shed?” she asks. “Once they shed, they shed.”

She points out the milky white ceramics of Astier de Villatte. “Almost every time I’m in here, I buy some little piece, like a little dish for my rings or a vase. I inherited a set of dishes from my father when he passed away,” she says.

Finally, we settle at Meg Turner Gallery. It’s a monastic, calm space, sparsely furnished with things like 19th-century Belgian console tables and beige cashmere blankets. Meyers-Shyer learned of it because it was one of the few (if not the only) places that sold John Julian silverware, which is based in rural Wiltshire, England. She loves John Julian, and wandered in one day to see it in person. Although today the shop assistant has something new for us to look at: Denis Colomb cashmere and camel hair blankets. I look at his website later: The throws retail from around $2,700 to $10,000 dollars. “I can see that she has Gustavian furniture with all these beautiful muted browns,” Meyers-Shyer says as she looks around, referring to the Swedish interpretation of 18th-century French neoclassical design. It’s a deep design reference—the type not taught in a rudimentary art history class but a master’s degree one—and shows just how much Meyers-Shyer has embedded herself in the design world.

“I realized I had a passion for something I didn’t know that I had, which is kind of cool at this age,” she says. Like great-grandmother, like grandmother, like mother, like daughter.

So far, the Iran war has cost the United States 800 casualties, tens of billions of dollars, hundreds of damaged or destroyed buildings on U.S. military bases in the region, and a drawdown of munitions that is straining supplies. Fuel prices have risen sharply since the start of the war, pushing U.S. inflation to a level last seen in 2023. According to polls, most Americans do not believe there was “sufficient reason” for the United States to attack Iran, and public opposition to the war has increased over time. And there is little sign that the fighting is coming to a close. As of now, the Iranian regime seems disinclined to cease its attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, stop its regional proxies from attacking elsewhere, or curb its nuclear program.

In broad strokes, there are three options. First, ramp the war back up. Some—including U.S. President Donald Trump—have floated the idea of returning to a full-scale bombing campaign similar to what we saw during the conflict’s initial phase. Others have proposed escalating the war, for example by using ground forces to seize Iranian territory.

At the other end of the spectrum lies a second option: The United States could walk away from the conflict. Even Republican supporters of the Trump administration have increasingly called for an end to the war.

The third option is to stay the current course: Maintain the blockade and economic pressure campaign, respond with force when Iran or its proxies strike U.S. or partner targets, and push for negotiations that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and end the Iranian nuclear program. Of the three options, this may be the least satisfying; it almost certainly will not bring quick results. But it is strategically prudent.

As messy as the conflict has been, the U.S. blockade of Iran and the wider Operation Economic Outcast are going in the direction that the United States wants. Iran is imploding economically. Its currency recently traded at 2.5 million rials to the dollar, a dramatic depreciation from about 630,000 to the dollar in 2024. Inflation has recently reached almost 90 percent, with food inflation at almost 130 percent. Moreover, Iran’s floating oil storage—a shadow fleet of tankers anchored outside the Strait of Hormuz, whose cargo has mitigated some of the effects of the blockade—is now depleted. That further increases the pressure on the Iranian economy.

Although the U.S. spot price has risen above $100 per barrel at times, oil is still priced well below previous peaks once you adjust for inflation. Middle Eastern crude is also again flowing out of the region at near prewar levels, although other critical commodities—liquefied natural gas, refined fuels, fertilizer—remain bottlenecked.

To be fair, economic coercion—even when it causes a full economic crisis—has a mixed track record in effecting regime change. Major economic dislocations have sometimes led to dramatic changes in government, as in Yugoslavia following years of economic problems in the 1980s. Inflationary crises can facilitate democratization, as happened in much of Latin America in the 1980s. But in other cases, such as the U.S. pressure campaigns against Cuba and North Korea, the regimes were still able to use what resources they had to maintain their hold on power.

The history of the last Iran nuclear deal shows that economic pressure campaigns have a better record when they are aimed at achieving more modest objectives. In that case, mounting economic duress forced even the Iranian regime’s hard-liners to the negotiating table. This time around, the regime seems even less inclined to make concessions, at least judging from its public statements, but the pressure campaign is also more extreme.

The primary downside to maintaining the current approach is that it is unlikely to lead to a swift resolution of the conflict. Strategies built around economic pressure generally take months or even years to succeed. But time is on Washington’s side. The blockade is continuing to bite under economic conditions that some deem “unbearable,” with unemployment rising and this year’s GDP projected to shrink by 7.6 percent. If Iran can stabilize its economy, the United States will need to alter its approach. But if not, the Iranian regime—as fractured and hard-line as it may be—will sooner or later need to compromise to avoid collapse.

Moreover, the alternatives to staying the course are worse. Resuming a full-scale war to “blow the entire nation up” is unlikely to be feasible. Even if we bracket concerns over U.S. weapons stockpiles, air campaigns alone have rarely succeeded in overthrowing a government. Plus, given the intensity of the bombardment in the opening weeks of the war, it’s hard to imagine what more the United States could do from the air that would tip the balance. Removing the Iranian regime by ground power would be a Herculean undertaking: Compared with Iraq, the site of the last major U.S. war in the region, Iran has nearly double the population and almost quadruple the territory.

Returning to large-scale conflict would also come at a cost. Despite the effectiveness of the bombardment from February to April, Iran still has the ability to produce missiles and drones. U.S. military casualties stand at 15 killed and more than 800 800 wounded, a figure that would almost certainly rise if the conflict intensified. Similarly, the war has already taken a toll on military equipment and bases in the region, and more damage would be incurred if the conflict flares up again. Then there are the less tangible costs, such as the erosion of Washington’s global reputation, especially if military strikes hit civilian infrastructure.

Then there is the global economic cost of a renewed large-scale conflict. Analysts estimate that it could take years to fully repair the damage to the Middle East’s energy infrastructure that has occurred so far. A return to hostilities would risk further damage to these already battered facilities, not to mention the prospect of even higher energy prices.

More modest military options also come with significant risks. The most widely discussed option is the seizure of Kharg Island, an island around 20 miles off the Iranian coast that serves as the country’s hub for 90 percent of its oil exports. This would aim to either destabilize the regime or use the island as a bargaining chip in future negotiations. Although the United States certainly has the capacity to seize the island, the challenge would be holding the territory afterward. The island is well within range of Iranian missiles and drones, leaving any troops stationed there and resupply efforts vulnerable to attack. Air defenses provide only a partial solution, so the United States would likely sustain casualties. This would also create political pressure on the United States to abandon the island prematurely—before it could pressure Iran into a lasting settlement.

Walking away from the war, by contrast, carries its own costs. Iran has so far rejected proposals to allow the free passage of global exports through Hormuz in exchange for an end to the blockade. Unilaterally dropping the blockade would effectively leave the Strait of Hormuz in Iranian hands, allowing the regime to tax transiting ships as it sees fit. Iran has reportedly stated that it wants to charge oil tankers approximately $1 per barrel—or about $2 million per ship—for safe passage. Iran could use that revenue to strengthen the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps’ coercive control over the state and fund its proxy militias across the region. This would also grant Iran a powerful economic weapon to wield in the future and leave the United States with less leverage in negotiations over Iran’s nuclear program.

Furthermore, a unilateral U.S. withdrawal would have repercussions across the region. The Iran war is not the only violent conflict in the Middle East today. Others include the Israeli-Iranian conflict, the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, the Yemeni civil war, the Saudi Arabia-Houthi war, ongoing Iranian attacks on Iraqi Kurdistan, and Sunni jihadist terrorism. None of these go away if U.S.-Iran war ends, and some may even intensify. After all, Iran would perceive a U.S. withdrawal as a win against its superpower archrival, leaving it emboldened to act with greater freedom across the region. With the blockade lifted, Tehran could resume funding its various proxy groups, which have been weakened by the effects of the war. That would be bad news for regional stability, global energy prices, and U.S. inflation.

The fallout wouldn’t stop there. Iranian malign activity spans the globe. Hezbollah, an Iranian proxy group, is active in Latin America. Iranian officials tried to assassinate senior U.S. officials inside the United States. Most recently, Iran was allegedly behind an attempt to attack a Royal Air Force base in Britain and a number of other plots targeting civilians in Europe. Unbound and bent on revenge, Iran is unlikely to be contained to just one corner of the globe.

Walking away from Iran could also exacerbate the United States’ other strategic problems. As China, Russia, and North Korea become increasingly aligned with Iran and each other, a victory for Tehran would benefit them all. Iran has supplied Russia with drones for the latter’s war in Ukraine and recently deepened its military cooperation with the Kremlin. Iran also enjoys close ties with China, providing it with energy and buying Chinese dual-use technology. After a perceived victory over the United States, Iranian cooperation with both countries would only strengthen. Adversaries would also point to this as evidence of the United States’ abandoning its allies in the Middle East, which would certainly not help rehabilitate Washington’s international reputation.

Sticking with the current approach is unlikely to result in a clean and quick end to the war. But sometimes an imperfect option is actually the right one—if only because the others are that much worse.

#4927. South Korea weighs fourth delay on its crypto gains tax, pushing it to 2029

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

South Korea is considering delaying its tax on cryptocurrency gains for a fourth time, postponing the introduction until 2029, Newsis reported.

The presidential office is leaning toward a delay amid concerns that the tax burden would fall largely on users of South Korea-based exchanges because authorities would struggle to track activity on U.S. platforms, the report said, citing unidentified officials.

“Almost half of the crypto market is the U.S.,” an official told the news outlet, calling the lack of U.S. tax data the biggest obstacle.

The discussions signal a shift from the government's stance in July, when Deputy Prime Minister Koo Yun-cheol told lawmakers that the tax would take effect in January 2027. Koo has since been replaced by Lee Hyung-il, who told lawmakers at an Oct. 6 parliamentary audit that he would gather views from parliament, experts and industry.

Under the current rules, annual net income from crypto transfers or lending will be taxed at 20%, or 22% including local income tax, after a 2.5 million won ($1,887) deduction. The National Tax Service still lists Jan. 1, 2027 as the start date.

The concern stems from differences in when countries will begin sharing crypto trading data under the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF).

#308. Anthropic’s IPO Prospectus Reveals What’s Under the Hood

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic filed key paperwork on Monday ahead of a highly anticipated initial public offering, providing new details about its finances and the risks facing its business.

According to the filing, the artificial intelligence company plans to go public on Nasdaq under the ticker symbol ANT. The IPO could come later in November, The Wall Street Journal reported. The S-1 prospectus does not include details on how much the company plans to raise or its targeted valuation; The New York Times reported in August that it was looking to raise more than $100 billion with a valuation around $2 trillion.

“The advent of machine intelligence is one of the seminal moments in human history,” Anthropic wrote in the filing. “We encourage you, as a prospective owner of our stock, not to think of your purchase in purely transactional terms. We hope you will see it instead as an opportunity to be part of something greater.”

The paperwork offers a window into the company’s finances through the first half of 2026. Its revenue grew to more than $16 billion, up from about $4.5 billion in 2025. The Claude maker emphasized its growing enterprise business, noting that more than 11,900 customers spent at least $100,000 in the past twelve months; more than 1,900 spent $1 million or more; and roughly 250 customers spent $10 million or more.

Despite its growing revenue, Anthropic has yet to turn a profit. It posted a net loss of $80.7 billion in the first half of 2026, compared to a net loss of $13 billion in the same period last year.

While it’s expected that a company accurately reports its risk factors, the prospectus also features a particularly thorough list of potential business quagmires, noting that investing in the stock carries “a high degree of risk.”

Those risks range from the ability to secure adequate compute to intense competition from rival labs to advanced AI systems’ potential to “pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.”

The filing warns about the possibility that sophisticated AI could be abused by threat actors to launch cyberattacks and develop novel biological weapons, or by authoritarian governments to surveil their citizens. Anthropic also pointed to the risk that it could lose control of the AI models it develops.

It also highlighted the volatility of government action, specifically noting the Trump administration’s decision to designate the company as a supply chain risk.

“While we continue to work with government agencies, we cannot be certain that the resolution of these lawsuits will not adversely affect our ability to sell to government agencies and other customers that sell products and services to the US government, and in turn adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations,” the filing concluded.

In September, Anthropic’s rival OpenAI—which filed confidentially for IPO in the summer—announced that it no longer intended to go public this year, citing concerns over the risks posed to humanity by AI.

Anthropic was founded in 2021 by seven former employees of OpenAI, who had reportedly lost faith in the lab’s ability to develop advanced AI models safely. Anthropic markets itself as highly safety-conscious; its CEO, Dario Amodei, has repeatedly commented on the risk that AI could eliminate scores of jobs, and act against human interests.

The filings also outline Anthropic’s ambition to develop sophisticated AI models capable of accelerating scientific discovery, curing disease, and spurring economic growth.

“To build AI is to harness cognition itself, and anything so powerful can be used for ill as well as good,” the company wrote.

The company did not immediately respond to WIRED’s request for comment.
