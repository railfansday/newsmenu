#620. France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

The other primary candidates questioned Glucksmann's political experience. Segolene Royal, a previous presidential candidate, compared his bid for the Elysee with that of an "intern" wanting to take charge of a company. She said Glucksmann had no experience in government or even running a local authority.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. A poll by Toluna Harris Interactive, published the day after his win, projects that he would win 10–11% of the vote. The same poll put Melenchon on 18%, meaning the far-left man would be much more likely to reach the runoff. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann has his work cut out for him — especially with young people. In an Ipsos survey in September, which posited Glucksmann as the social-democratic candidate and Edouard Philippe as the only candidate from the political center, Melenchon scored 41% among the under-25s. Meanwhile, 20% of this age group supported the far-right nationalist Marine Le Pen. France's young electorate is politically divided, but Melenchon still dominates on the left.

The 75-year-old is currently rallying alongside the students whose protests have drawn international attention. At a rally near Clermont-Ferrand in central France, he expressed solidarity with their movement and reiterated his calls for lowering the voting age to 16 and abolishing Parcoursup, France's national admissions procedure for higher education.

In an analysis for the Fondation Jean-Jaures, a Socialist-aligned think tank, the political scientist Antoine Bristielle distinguishes between a core group of committed LFI voters and the broader left. Melenchon's most loyal supporters, he says, are often young, educated city-dwellers, and many are under financial pressure. Four in 10 households in this group live on a net monthly income of less than €2,000 ($2,200).

Bristielle estimates that just under a third of the left-wing electorate in his study care more about a left-wing victory than their allegiance to any particular party. Almost half of the people in this group are younger than 35 and would consider voting for the Socialists, the Greens or LFI. Redistribution and an active government are important to them, but most would prefer politicians who are willing to compromise.

The pattern recently observed in Germany, where young women are more likely to vote for the left and young men for the right, does not apply in France. Political scientists Anja Durovic, Nonna Mayer and Noemie Piolat found that in the 2024 European and parliamentary elections, young French women were not significantly more likely to vote for the left, or less likely to vote for the far right, than men of the same age. It remains to be seen whether this pattern will be replicated in the 2027 presidential election.

Glucksmann says he would ease the burden on earned income by reducing France's CSG social security tax while increasing taxes on inheritances worth more than €2 million. He also presents himself as a committed supporter of European integration, while Melenchon has called the EU a neoliberal institution. Glucksmann's political profile combines social policy demands with foreign policy positions that could also appeal to voters from the camp of the current president, Emmanuel Macron.

France's Ukraine policy is one such issue. Glucksmann favors decisive support for Kyiv. Manon Aubry, a member of the European Parliament for Melenchon's LFI, says her party supports military assistance only under certain conditions, namely: The provision of aid to Ukraine must first be subject to democratic debate; it must not enable attacks deep inside Russian territory; and it must not weaken France's own defense capabilities. In 2024, LFI MPs voted against the French–Ukrainian security agreement.

The tension between the parties of the left was once again apparent in the aftermath of the flour attack in Belleville. The LFI MP Sebastien Delogu posted a declaration of support on X for the taxi driver who was going to have to clean his car.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

#4193. Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

Researchers then examined whether these differences translated into protection against major cardiovascular problems. The outcomes included strokes, heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic heart disease, and heart failure that resulted in hospitalization or death.

The findings revealed a striking contrast. Among patients who consistently followed their treatment plans, the intervention group experienced an 11% lower incidence of major cardiovascular events compared with the comparator group. However, among patients with lower adherence, there was no statistically significant reduction in these events.

"Notably, our results reinforce the saying that 'drugs don't work in patients who don't take them.' Patients with lower adherence derived little or no cardiovascular benefit, whereas those with higher adherence had greater reductions in blood pressure and prevention of cardiovascular events," said Miss Yang.

Helping Patients Get Lifesaving Heart Protection

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. They recommend more routine assessments of medication adherence, along with better patient education and practical strategies to make treatment easier to follow.

Possible approaches include simplifying medication schedules, combining multiple treatments into a single pill, and prescribing longer-acting drugs that can help limit the consequences of an occasional missed dose.

Professor Felix Mahfoud, Chair of the ESC Communication Committee, emphasized that missed medication has received too little attention as a contributor to poorly controlled blood pressure.

"For years, we have neglected non-adherence to medication as a cause for uncontrolled hypertension. Assessment of adherence should become a routine part of hypertension care so patients do not miss out on life-saving benefits. As healthcare providers, we are here to have open, non-judgmental conversations with our patients about any barriers they may have to taking medication, to help improve adherence."

#5232. Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.
