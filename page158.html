The company is offering a voluntary exit to 60,000 eligible employees while betting billions on the AI infrastructure buildout.

Following billions in AI investments last fiscal year, Microsoft announced Monday that it is offering buyouts to roughly 60,000 employees, about a quarter of its workforce, a person familiar with the matter told CNBC. The voluntary exit offer applies to staff at experience level 70 or below across the US, Canada, India and the UK.

According to Microsoft, the move is tailored to give “eligible employees who may be considering a future outside the company a new choice to make that transition now with support.” Microsoft didn’t say how many employee buyouts it will accept, though it’s likely fewer than those offered the deal. The company didn’t immediately respond to CNET’s request for comment.

Microsoft’s operating income increased by about $30 billion between the 2026 and 2025 fiscal years.

In September, Bloomberg reported that Microsoft is planning on tripling its computing power in the coming years, which could result in the company using more energy than all of New York City at peak hours.

The buyout affects teams including Xbox, Windows, Copilot and finance, but excludes certain data-center, sales and marketing staff, according to Bloomberg. Xbox already cut 1,600 employees in July in one round of layoffs, and another round is expected before June 2027. The voluntary exit offers show that Microsoft is prioritizing infrastructure buildout, automation and capital efficiency over hiring.

Though AI might not yet be eliminating jobs at scale, companies are spending billions on compute while becoming far more selective about adding personnel.

There is no sign that China’s export juggernaut is slowing down. China’s auto exports, for example, have grown more than tenfold since 2020 and are estimated to reach 10 million vehicles this year. The country posted a record trade surplus of $119.1 billion in August alone, putting it on track to surpass the $1.2 trillion surplus of 2025. The “China shock” is now an everyday reality in multiple sectors, from electric vehicles to power generation.

Accusations that China is exporting excess capacity have echoed across the United States, the European Union, and Southeast Asia, though China has vehemently rejected this. The concerns are understandable: It is hard to compete with Chinese industrial might, and local industry stakeholders worry about everything from job losses to dependence on Chinese suppliers.

But the conventional view that China’s dominant position in the global market is the result of the central government’s long-term strategic planning and top-down industrial policy tells only half the story. Beijing is not fully in control of the bulldozer of Chinese manufacturing, and the results have sometimes been far beyond what the government wanted or planned for.

In 2004, for instance, when the State Council, China’s central government, announced the launch of the high-speed railway program, its original plan was to build 12,000 kilometers of track by 2020. China ended up with nearly 38,000 kilometers by the end of that year.

The explanation lies at the local level. Once the policy initiative was launched, persistent, intensive lobbying by provinces, cities, and counties ensued, driven by their own interests in securing railway lines within their jurisdictions. The central government subsequently revised its railway construction plans several times, effectively accommodating local demands.

Beijing’s original ambitions for its domestic auto industry differed markedly from what emerged in practice. The original plan was to consolidate the market around a small number of large state-owned enterprises, such as First Automotive Works (FAW), and their joint ventures with foreign partners, positioning them as industry leaders. The superstars of China’s auto industry today, particularly in the new energy vehicle market, are mostly private firms like Geely, BYD, and Nio, each backed by a local government.

If anything, the original plan to strengthen state-owned automakers was a debacle. Leaders in Changchun, FAW’s home city, acknowledged in July that the city faced “unprecedented difficulties”: Citywide industrial profits had fallen 20 percent in 2025, while the traditional auto industry faced mounting pressure to modernize.

As much as Beijing might want to control everything from the top, implementation of its plans depends on officials whose incentives, in practice, can differ wildly from what the center wants. Local bureaucrats are appointed from above, rather than elected by constituents, with promotions tied to tournament-style evaluations. This competition is built into the system: Leaders at each level pit officials below them against one another and promote those believed to be doing a good job, based on a specific set of key performance indicators, whether explicitly stated or tacitly understood.

This gives Chinese localities strong incentives to stand out among their peers. For a long time, economic growth was the primary metric in the Chinese Communist Party’s evaluation system, so localities competed fiercely over key indicators such as GDP, investment, foreign direct investment, and revenue growth. Over the past decade, the criteria for official evaluation have expanded, as the party now prioritizes a broader range of issues, including environmental protection, industrial upgrading, social welfare, and poverty alleviation. GDP growth is no longer a guaranteed path to career advancement. Localities have had to impress their superiors—in one way or another.

The attention of senior leaders, however, is scarce. The country has more than 30 provincial-level units, over 330 cities, nearly 2,800 county-level units, and numerous bureaucratic agencies. Local governments accounted for 85 percent of China’s general budget spending in 2025. The central government still holds significant regulatory power and resources that can make or break local development.

Local governments have very few formal ways to advance their interests all the way to the top. They need to get creative to draw the attention of senior leaders to their localities. There are multiple ways to do this. Some rely on influential retired officials with hometown ties to bring their requests before top party leaders. Others compete to host high-profile mega-events like the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit or the Asian Games so that national leaders will pay a personal visit to the locality in question—creating a camera-ready photo opportunity for both the leader and the locality.

This all sounds absurdly superficial, but within the Chinese system, a single line of special written instructions from a top leader, or a favorable verbal comment made during a local inspection tour and propagated across the domestic media ecosystem, can mean real money is on the table—sometimes helping a locality secure billions of dollars in investment projects or fiscal transfers.

This “attention economy” reflects the severe information deficit in any multilayered bureaucracy with numerous subdivisions. Ideally, top leaders should promote those who actually solve real problems and contribute to national initiatives. Actual performance, however, is difficult to measure and verify from the top. It is a classic principal-agent problem.

With performance measures that are easy to game, local bureaucrats find that making a good impression on superiors becomes more important than actually solving problems. The surest way to impress Beijing, obviously, is to invest in whatever the center has signaled as a priority—especially projects that are visible, look good on camera, and are low-risk. EVs, robotics, and AI all fit the bill.

When each locality comes up with its own strategy for standing out, it is more concerned with outbidding, outspending, and outshining its peers than with the long-term health of the industry—or, for that matter, whether the national or global market can absorb the resulting output. Rational individual behavior thus adds up to a market coordination failure that produces overcapacity.

Interestingly, the central government is aware of the problem. Chinese President Xi Jinping himself has repeatedly warned against disorderly local development drives in areas such as EVs and AI, including in a public speech in 2025. But the problem is structural. And the leader’s warning, unlike his praise or a photo opportunity, bears little meaning when no consequences are attached to the actions of specific localities.

Another critical and often overlooked aspect is that the bureaucratic appointment system is structured as a series of relatively short tenures. Provincial and city leaders (party secretaries and government executives) serve an average of only three and a half years in a position. This means most officials need to demonstrate results within that window. They have to act quickly once they take office, and tunnel vision and the pursuit of quick, short-term gains take precedence over the consideration of long-term consequences.

It is not surprising that many rush to launch large investment projects to boost local economic figures, even if the projects are a poor fit in the long term or financially unsustainable. Their successors, in turn, repeat the dynamic, with one extra layer: They have every incentive to launch new projects to distinguish themselves from their predecessors, showing that they are not simply riding on the shoulders of the previous leader. This leads to further duplicative construction.

This goes beyond industrial policy and seeps into every aspect of China’s chronic economic ailments. China’s mounting local government debt—a long-standing problem that has proven extremely thorny to unwind—is, in effect, not only a result of the mismatch between revenue and expenditure (local governments are responsible for about 80 percent of spending but collect only about 50 percent of revenue), as analysts correctly point out. It is also exacerbated by cycles of ever-greater overinvestment that accompany each leadership turnover and compound over time. The perverse consequence of short tenure is that the costs of failed investment decisions, such as debt and bankruptcies, often emerge long after an official has left office, making it effectively impossible to hold anyone accountable. And if officials cannot be held accountable for these wasteful decisions, bad behavior persists—moral hazard becomes rampant.

It’s true that China’s overcapacity could be addressed by strengthening the domestic market, and the central government has indeed sought to boost consumption in recent years. Yet proponents of consumption-led rebalancing overlook the fact that China’s weak consumption may have the same root cause as its manufacturing glut. Pro-consumption policies, such as subsidies to households, are slow to take effect, and their effectiveness is difficult to measure—how do you assess how financially secure households feel and whether they are ready to open their purses and spend?

And even if the “investment in people” measures make people feel more secure, consumption is slow-moving. It takes months, if not years, for these effects to show up in actual consumption data. Further, unlike building infrastructure or investing in manufacturing, consumption is hard to tie to a particular locality (consumers receiving a subsidy may well spend it elsewhere), which means local leaders cannot easily claim credit for it even as they commit scarce resources.

Naturally, consumption does not make the list of priorities for local leaders facing fierce competition—despite their public statements echoing Beijing’s calls to promote consumption, exactly as instructed. On the ground, investment takes clear priority, while consumption is put on the back burner, especially under local financing constraints.

At the heart of the issue is a structural problem in China’s central-local relations: The central government relies on local competition to advance its policy goals yet has struggled to rein in the very incentives that drive excessive investment. Washington and Brussels might call out Beijing for not doing enough to rein in excess capacity. But even when Beijing is fully aware of the problem, and arguably willing to address some aspects of it, it still runs up against the same local incentives that created the problem in the first place.

Restructuring these incentives, much like changing corporate culture, is as much an organizational challenge as a political one. Half a century ago, organizational theorist Steven Kerr described precisely this problem as “the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B.” Beijing is still grappling with it today.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission and the National Basketball Association have agreed to put in place standards to protect the integrity of the game amid concerns of insider trading and manipulation in prediction markets.

The CFTC and NBA signed a memorandum of understanding to share information and open lines of communication about maintaining prediction markets while preserving integrity, according to a statement from the CFTC. Both Kalshi and Polymarket will be the league's "preferred prediction market exchanges," the NBA said in a statement.

"As this category develops, we believe it is important to work constructively with regulators and operators to help ensure that appropriate safeguards are in place," said Scott Kaufman-Ross, NBA head of media distribution & partnerships, in a statement. "These partnerships represent an important step in establishing a framework that creates new opportunities to engage our fans."

The NBA marks the third sports league to sign an MOU with the CFTC. Earlier this year, the National Hockey League and Major League Baseball signed similar agreements. The National Football League has not teamed up with the CFTC and has pressed the agency for tougher rules on game integrity, consumer safety and insider trading controls.

The NFL has also diverged from the CFTC over when the federal or state government should regulate prediction markets. Who should regulate those billions of dollars worth of bets has become a major point of contention. The CFTC has said that most contracts on prediction markets are swaps and so fall under its "exclusive jurisdiction." However, states have argued that prediction market companies are operating illegally, violating their gaming laws and avoiding paying taxes.

The fight could potentially make its way up to the U.S. Supreme Court after New Jersey asked the high court to take up the issue. Earlier this month, the NFL filed an amicus brief with the U.S. Supreme Court and said that sports contracts are gambling, so they should be overseen by states.

Insider trading has also been a repeated issue in prediction markets. An active-duty U.S. Army soldier was charged in April for using confidential information to place bets on Polymarket ahead of former Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro's capture earlier this year. In another case, a former White House teleprompter agreed to pay more than $172,000 to settle charges that he used his advance access to President Trump's speeches to profit on Kalshi.

Kalshi and Polymarket have both said they have taken certain steps, including mandatory employment verification for traders in sensitive markets to deter insider trading.

President Donald Trump’s threat to veto everything that a possibly Democratic-run Congress might send him next year is igniting a debate in the opposition party as it contemplates a legislative agenda in a divided Washington.

The Republican president, who has spent his 11 years in national politics touting deal-making chops he contends are rooted in his business and real estate career, revealed Friday he views his veto pen as leverage. But that does not mean it would automatically overcome his lame-duck status.

In the event Congress does not address the debt limit in the lame duck-session, if both chambers flip and Trump’s gambit doesn’t cause Democrats to fold, the government could shut down again and the country could breach its debt limit.

Asked Friday as he departed the White House for a campaign rally in Florida about how he would work with a Congress under Democratic control, Trump said he would try vetoing Democrats into submission, predicting that its leaders would get desperate and seek deals.

“What happens is I veto everything, and then the Democrats come and they want to make a deal. So we’ll work on that. I mean, if you look, historically, when you have a situation where that would happen, if it happens, you veto, veto, veto, and then they come and they say, ‘How do we get it done?’ And you make a deal,” he told reporters as he left the White House for a campaign rally in Florida.

The president stopped short, again, of predicting a big night for Republicans on Nov. 3, saying Friday his party could retain control of the chambers “if I can get my voters out.”

With eight days to go before Election Day, Trump has shifted the blame game — onto those same supporters. On Sunday in Port Huron, Mich., he told rallygoers they would have no one else to blame — including him — if Democrats prevailed next week.

“All of this, if they win, will be all this junk,” he said of his second administration’s policies. “Open borders, men in women’s sports, transgender for everybody, it’s all gonna be put right back in as soon as they win. They win, you got it right back.

“So it’s you, you will only have yourselves to blame,” Trump said. “And I don’t think there’s any coming back from that, either.”

Democratic candidates largely have run on an anti-Trump platform, while vowing to block his agenda during his final two years. Some have made promises about lowering the cost of gasoline, diesel, groceries and more.

Trump’s veto bonanza threat already has created fissures among Democrats about their legislative agenda, should they take the gavels in both chambers.

Sen. Brian Schatz, a Hawaii Democrat who is expected to take a bigger leadership role in the 120th Congress when it’s seated on Jan. 3, said last week that his party should realize Trump’s lame-duck years likely would not mean a sudden flurry of bipartisanship in Washington.

“The question in a midterm [election] is: Have you had enough? Do you think that there should be a check against unified control of Washington D.C. among Republicans? And so, I think we should frame it that way as an institutional check against an out-of-control president,” he told CNN on Oct. 22. “And then, if we do take power, we should use our leverage to try to bring this [Iran] war to a conclusion and to reverse the Medicaid and SNAP cuts.” (The latter was a reference to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and cuts included in Republicans’ 2025 sweeping tax and spending law.)

“I do not think it is time to propose things that the president of the United States is guaranteed to veto. I think that is somewhat of a bit of wasted energy,” Schatz added. “And then, if we want to be more aspirational, if we want to make more bold policy changes, we’re going to have to take the presidency and both chambers — and that’s for 2028. Right now is about stabilizing the country itself and providing some check against … an out-of-control presidency and a corrupt presidency.”

But Rep. Ro Khanna, a California Democrat who has been mentioned as a potential 2028 presidential candidate, fired back by reposting a clip of Schatz’s comments with a clear message.

“Make him veto Medicare for All. Make him veto a $25 living wage. Make him veto free childcare. Make him veto Block the Bombs to Israel,” he wrote on X of Trump. “Make him veto zero aid to Israel. Make him veto free college. Make him veto erasing student loans. Make him veto taxes on billionaires.”

That was a much more aggressive approach than what Schatz prescribed for his party: “I think people want to see us do not everything, but a few things that will take the pain out of what they’re experiencing.”

Democratic strategist Jim Manley said Monday that Trump needs “two things” from Congress during his final years: a debt-ceiling increase and keeping the government open, adding, “He could care less about the farm bill, FISA going dark, etcetera.”

“Democrats should spend the next two years spending as little time as possible wondering how to negotiate with Trump. If the House flips, they should just send bills over to the Senate and see if they die,” he added in an email. “If Democrats flip the House and the Senate, then you are playing with real ammunition. Given the rules of the Senate, Democrats will have to negotiate with Republicans if they hope to get anything done. If a wildly unpopular president wants to veto popular legislation two years before the next presidential election, by all means go for it.”

#5327. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/new-female-specific-framework-cardiovascular-disease-prevention-and-care" hreflang="en">Medical societies release female-specific framework for cardiovascular disease prevention, care</a>

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Medical societies release female-specific framework for cardiovascular disease prevention, care" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Four major medical societies released a female-specific framework for cardiovascular disease prevention, detection and management.

The framework, published as three papers in the American Heart Association (AHA) journal Circulation, was developed by the AHA, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine. The framework calls for identifying risk earlier and improving care coordination between specialties.

Cardiovascular disease, including heart disease and stroke, remains a leading cause of death in women. These conditions kill more women annually than breast cancer, lung cancer and chronic lung disease combined, according to the AHA. It is also the leading cause of medical-related deaths during pregnancy. Yet only 41% of women recognize it as their greatest health threat.

Progress on prevention has stalled over the past two decades, while care gaps remain. For example, only about 60% of U.S. women who experience hypertensive complications during pregnancy get blood pressure follow-up care within six months of delivery, per the AHA. The association forecasts that if things continue, the portion of women aged 20 years and older living with high blood pressure will increase from 49% in 2020 to 59% in 2050.

The newly published Presidential Advisory introduces the Female Life Stage framework and the impact distinct biological transitions have on cardiovascular health in women. For example, menstrual cycle patterns, pregnancy complications like preeclampsia and menopause are identified as important markers of cardiovascular risk. The framework takes a holistic approach by considering cardiovascular, hormonal and emotional health together.

“Heart health begins long before adulthood,” Amanda Marma Perak, M.D., vice-chair of the collaborative writing group and a pediatric cardiologist at Lurie Children’s Hospital in Chicago, said in an announcement. “As pediatric clinicians, we have the opportunity to help every girl start strong and to ensure that what we learn about her health follows her as she grows.”

The Scientific Statement offers a clinical rationale for the Female Life Stage construct and clinical guidance based on a science advisory group spanning specialties convened by the AHA. The paper identified existing guideline recommendations to determine evidence-based, stage-specific guidance. This includes ongoing assessment of menstrual cycle regularity; screening for cardiovascular risk at diagnosis of PMOS; documenting age at menopause; and routine depression and anxiety screening starting at 12 and 13, respectively.

“Sometimes it feels like these problems are so big,” Sadiya Khan, M.D., chair of the collaborative writing group and professor at the Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, told Fierce Healthcare. “Oftentimes, we’re not thinking about heart health until somebody has an event … let’s take stock of where your heart health is right now and what are the potential things that could be influencing it.”

Meanwhile, the Science Advisory proposes a national infrastructure of Comprehensive Centers of Excellence for Women’s Cardiovascular Care. These would be interdisciplinary care models reaching across clinical care, professional training, research and community engagement to tackle the issue.

“One of the things that I think is really important is that the clinicians and patients have this shared playbook and shared language, and that it’s understandable for any clinician of any specialty and any patient,” Khan said.

These centers’ goal would be to reduce fragmentation, enable earlier risk identification and increase representation of women in clinical research. These centers could also encourage surrounding health systems to promote greater awareness of female risk. This concept was based, in part, on a survey of clinician leaders at women’s cardiovascular centers or programs. The proposed national infrastructure builds on the benefits, challenges and gaps identified in the survey.

The group has also begun developing educational materials, including how to apply the Female Life Stage framework to a case-based approach, according to Khan. To track outcomes, the group is considering establishing a network to evaluate and implement specific changes in care pathways. THE ADIOS PRO 5 swaps the underfoot “EnergyRods” Adidas had used in its previous shoe models to a new “Energyrim” stiffening feature—a carbon-infused element sandwiched between layers of Lightstrike Pro foam that rings the foot, also used in the Pro Evo 3. I like the new carbon rim vs. the standard underfoot plate design of Adidas’ previous iterations and models from other brands; the propulsion was still there, but I felt more stable.

The lacing took time to find a knot that worked for me, but once I did I felt super locked in. I ran 13- and 16.5-mile sessions as part of the testing, and the shoes felt light, peppy, and (mostly) comfortable going the distance.

Not everything was ideal. The exposed carbon rim looks cool, but on my first run wearing the Pro 5, I was a bit sloppy with my form and clipped the inside of my calf with my other foot a few times, which really hurt. The pads in the heel lock the foot in, but every time I wore the shoe I wound up with a blister. The first time was bad, but then I made sure to wear reinforced socks every other time I laced up. My heel still showed some irritation after my runs, but it wasn’t so bad that it affected my form or made me want to stop running.

ADIDAS SET THE bar high for this model, and in most ways, it clears. The Adios Pro 5 is lighter and more responsive than the Pro 4, which has been one of the more popular (and accessible) super shoes on the market since launching in 2024. Lots of runners gunning for a race PR will love the Pro 5 for their goal event and their fastest training days.

The outer forefoot did show some wear—but nothing I wouldn’t expect, and it didn’t degrade to the point that it affected performance. If you’re planning to get the Pro 5, you should have other shoes for the other types of sessions you’ll need as part of a balanced run practice.

Turo will soon let US renters search specifically for Teslas with “Full Self-Driving” (Supervised) turned on, using a direct integration with Tesla to verify each car’s FSD status. The feature goes live on October 28.

For Tesla, it’s a new way to put FSD in front of people who don’t own a Tesla, and a new source of the FSD subscriptions that now count toward Elon Musk’s pay package.

Hosts link their Tesla account to Turo, and the platform pulls the car’s FSD status through Tesla’s API. Eligible listings then show that FSD is active, and guests can filter for it.

Turo says “thousands” of FSD-enabled Teslas are already listed. Not a shocker. The Model 3 and Model Y are the third and fourth most popular vehicles on Turo so far this year, according to the company.

Eligible US hosts can also get a free one-month FSD (Supervised) trial. After that, it’s Tesla’s regular $99 per month subscription, a cost hosts will want to recover through their daily rates. Turo didn’t say whether FSD listings will carry a premium.

Turo CEO Andre Haddad called it an “industry-first integration with Tesla” and said that “most travelers have had limited opportunity to experience driver’s assistance technology, but the demand is clear.”

The “industry-first” part is generous. Tesla has been renting out its own cars with FSD included in Southern California since last year, a program we first spotted in the works back in 2023. The new part is verified FSD on a third-party marketplace with far more cars in far more cities.

Tesla went subscription-only with FSD in February, and since then it has been pushing hard to get people to try it, including a free one-year subscription on new Model 3 and Model Y orders.

Turo’s release leans heavily on “self-driving” and “driverless” language. It cites its own research claiming that 55% of Americans are interested in “driverless technology” for travel, rising to 71% among Gen Z, and that over a fifth of interested Americans would be comfortable spending seven or more hours in a self-driving car.

But FSD (Supervised) is not driverless. It’s a level 2 driver assistance system, and the person behind the wheel is responsible at all times. Even Haddad calls it “driver’s assistance technology” in the same release.

Renters will be getting into a car they’ve likely never driven, with a system they’ve likely never used. Turo’s safeguard is to encourage guests to review Tesla’s FSD documentation and safety guidelines before pickup. That’s it.

We saw what over-trust looks like just two weeks ago, when a driver told the CHP he was asleep with FSD engaged before his Tesla hit a patrol car inside a lane closure in Fairfield, California. Two officers were seriously injured.

Turo also didn’t say who’s on the hook when a guest crashes with FSD engaged: the renter, the host, or Turo’s protection plans.
