Yamal has emerged as one of Barcelona’s most exciting young players, achieving milestones at an age when many players are still developing in academy systems. Mbappe and Haaland have followed similarly remarkable paths, becoming prolific goalscorers and central figures for their respective clubs while still relatively young. Their achievements suggest they have the talent to define the next era, but it raises a different question: how long can they maintain their current standards?

The modern soccer calendar places enormous demands on elite players through domestic leagues, continental competitions, international fixtures, and expanded tournaments. Reaching the top early can mean facing years of physical and mental pressure before many previous generations had even reached their peak.

Kylian Mbappe, Lamine Yamal and Erling Haaland.

Lewandowski shared his perspective in an interview with The Athletic’s Paul Tenorio, reflecting on the differences between his generation and the teenagers already competing at the highest level. The Polish striker believes modern players reach elite status much earlier, creating challenges that may become more apparent as their careers progress. “For the future it will be impossible to stay on the top for the long term, as we did or we are doing,” Lewandowski said.

He explained that young players now have access to opportunities and rewards much earlier than previous generations, leaving them with less time to develop gradually. “Our generation, to get something, we needed more time, we need to be more patient,” he added. “Now it’s too fast for the young player… they’re starting much earlier.”

“Even though they are amazing players, they reach faster the highest level than our generation did, but probably because of this also it will be more tough for them staying on the top level,” the 38-year-old striker explained.

He also stressed that the challenge extends beyond mentality, pointing to the physical demands of a long career at the highest level. “It’s not easy to play on the top level so many years,” he said, suggesting that the combination of early success and accumulated fatigue could make sustained excellence increasingly difficult.

#4705. Play ‘Doom’ on this tiny, uterus-shaped period tracker

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 3:42 PM EDT

Doom is much more than a classic, bloodsoaked first-person shooter known for its chaotic action, over-the-top weapons, and challenging boss fights. For some odd (but very enjoyable) reason, it’s served as a benchmark for testing basic computing capabilities for well over 30 years. Name a device——calculators, ATMs, digitally enabled tractors, and even biochips built with E. coli bacteria—and someone has likely coded it to run Doom.

The latest entry in the ever-growing list of Doom compatible contraptions is a double-dose of DIY ingenuity, however. The Linux software engineer known as Miss Molerat recently began tinkering with a pocket-sized medical gadget made specifically to keep track of her menstrual cycle. But unlike many similar commercial products, “Clotty” keeps all personal data strictly on the device itself. It never connects to an external cloud or offloads sensitive information to a company’s servers, thereby preventing any unwanted data harvesting. And it does all this on a custom, uterus-shaped minicomputer running on a Raspberry Pi microcontroller.

“I have one thing that has been bothering me for as long as I’ve been menstruating: despite my cycle being very regular, I somehow manage to get surprised by PMS each and every month anew,” Clotty’s inventor wrote on her website’s blog.

Clotty “does the boring stuff” for her by tracking her period via a Tamagotchi-like digital pet that requires attention and maintenance throughout each month. The pixelated creature’s mood shifts depending on the cycle phase, but it also can go on little quests and offer various minigames in the meantime.

“[It’s] basically some sort of adorably shaped mirror version of my own moods that serves as a reminder that I, too, am just some sort of creature that tries to stay alive and happy,” explained Clotty’s creator.

But why stop there? Clotty is powerful enough to run Doom on its itty-bitty LCD screen, which is thematically perfect given the project’s overarching, bloody motif. This means that, when not keeping up with personal health, a user can also blast through wave after wave of space demons using small onboard sensors.

The inventor admitted that Clotty is still in its “fetal stage,” but she plans to add more features like a menstrual cycle history archive, more games, and even some reproductive health trivia. It’s also a completely open-source project, so anyone can follow her online tutorials and download files for Clotty (and Doom).

#3686. Why the Holidays Are Actually the Best Time of Year to Start Working Out

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you have even the tiniest inkling that you might want to get in shape in the new year—or anytime really—don’t wait until January. This isn't a "winter arc" self-improvement pitch; it's just facts. The best time to start a gym habit is before the holiday season. Heck, in December, you’ll even have the place mostly to yourself.

Sure, technically “right now” is always the best time to start exercising, but the holiday season is special. If you wait until January, gyms will be packed. The first two weeks of the year, everybody thinks they’re at the start of an amazing new fitness habit...and by February, most of them have forgotten all about it. Don’t make life harder for yourself by starting during the big rush. Classes will fill up, you’ll have to fight people for the squat rack, and it will be hard to have any real fun. So start now.

In October and November, gyms and classes are at more or less their normal capacities. This is a good time to feel out the vibe of a new place. But it gets better: Once December rolls around, the fair-weather gymgoers will be off shopping for gifts after work and attending holiday parties on the weekends. They won’t feel like getting out of their warm beds in the morning, either. When you arrive, the squat rack will be free. You’ll have your choice of treadmills. The gym will be your oyster.

If you start now, you'll have about two months to get up to speed before the rush starts in January. And that is a perfect little time frame to set a small goal and keep it. Vow to hit the gym three times a week, and you’ll be able to get in 24 workouts before Christmas, even if you take a few days off for festivities. You can get a lot stronger in 24 workouts. Or run, if that's the fitness journey you're most excited about. If you start a couch-to-5k running plan now, you’ll be routinely running 30 minutes at a stretch by the time we get to January.

Yes, the holiday season can be busy and stressful, but remember that working out tends to help our brains manage stress. Mark workouts on your calendar, and keep those appointments with yourself.

Don’t worry about missing out on deals. Any gym or studio that offers a January special probably offers sales year-round anyway. And ultimately, if you get into the habit of actually using the gym, you're getting a lot more value for your money than somebody who joins in January and quickly stops showing up.

The Mercedes-Benz W124 E-Class ‘Hammer’ brought AMG into the world’s view, and now HWA looks to continue that car’s story.

The folks at HWA are expanding their restomodding footprint. A new teaser from the German company on Instagram suggests that, on top of their awesome Mercedes-Benz 190E Evo reincarnation, it’s now working on a W124-gen “AMG Hammer”’ recreation.

Created by AMG back in the 1980s, the modified W124 E-Class got its “Hammer” nickname from its brutal acceleration on tap thanks to the big V-8 engine under the hood. This vehicle is seen by many as the defining car for AMG as an independent tuning house, before it officially went into business and became one with Mercedes-Benz. Now, HWA—which was started by one of the original people behind AMG—looks to be readying a reboot.

“Following the HWA Evo, we are revisiting another extraordinary chapter of our engineering heritage,” HWA writes in its Instagram post. “Staying true to its origins while exploring the possibilities of modern technology, a familiar idea is taking on an entirely new form.”

That heritage traces back to AMG co-founder Hans Werner Aufrecht, who is also the founder of HWA—his initials being the origin of the name. The teaser seen here sure looks a whole lot like the original W124 Hammer’s silhouette; we can already see some of the modern touches being applied to the recreation in the form of LED headlights and taillights. HWA did the same for its 190E remake, bridging the gap between the original and modern tech.

The Evo uses a turbocharged V-6 engine, but we suspect any Hammer redux would need to house a V-8 under the hood, much like the original did. HWA doesn’t explicitly say anything about the powertrain, but suggests “more will be revealed” in due time. Road & Track asked HWA for more information, and were told that additional information will be coming in the ensuing weeks. We sure are hopeful to see this next project from HWA take shape.

#4738. How to Winterize a Garden: Ready Your Plants to Weather the Cold With These Expert Tips

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

As fall winds down, you’ll pull sweaters out of storage and crank the thermostat, but what about your outdoor plants? Knowing how to winterize a garden is key to welcoming spring with an abundance of thriving greenery—as opposed to a sad brown patch of lawn. If frost, snow, rain, and freezing temperatures are in the forecast, know that your outdoor spaces don’t have to face the elements without a little assistance.

To prepare your garden for winter, you’ll need burlap; straw; garden tools like pruning shears and a pruning saw or lopper; a compost pile or ready source of nutrient-rich matter; and, ideally, some winter-ready plants that can take the place of flowering annuals and edible plants.

One more item to add to your list (in potentially large quantities): mulch. “One of the biggest things we do is mulch, and then mulch some more,” says Christina Stembel, founder and CEO of D2C e-commerce flower company Farmgirl Flowers. “We bring in what feels like an absurd amount of mulch every fall and use it just about everywhere. It helps insulate roots, suppress weeds, protect the soil through rain [and snow], and eventually adds organic matter back into the ground. We mulch our garden roses, peonies, perennial beds, [and more].”

Amy Enfield, who has more than 30 years of experience in the lawn- and garden-care industry and is currently a senior horticulturalist at Miracle-Gro, offers thoughts on a mindful first step ahead of digging in: “Before pulling out tools or buying materials, walk your garden and take stock of what you have. Note which plants are annuals that will not return, which are perennials you want to protect, where the soil looks tired or compacted, and which areas might benefit from a cover crop over winter. This assessment keeps you from doing more work than necessary or missing something important.”

Burlap adds a layer of protection against the elements for this outdoor plant in the wintertime.

“Do not begin with applying mulch,” says Jose Lopez, the founder and owner of Williams Landscaping Inc. “First, walk through your fall garden to pull up dead foliage. Pull any foliage that shows signs of disease as well. Next, apply appropriate care to the plants that will remain. Finally, cover your soil. If you do not complete these first three steps, you’re simply burying the problems of the growing season.” To cover that soil, there are three primary materials, each of which plays a different role, Lopez says. “Compost supplies nutrients to the soil. Mulch serves as insulation [and you can] use the leaves that are readily available, shredding them for mulch. Straw has no place in most flowerbeds but is suitable for vegetable gardening.” Just be mindful of where you are placing covering materials, and “do not layer large amounts of wet leaves over a newly planted shrub because the water can cause root rot at its base,” Lopez notes.

Raised garden beds tend to be used for annual flowers and edible plants, and these typically need to be removed, says Dr. Enfield. “Leaving their debris in the garden over winter creates a cozy habitat for overwintering pests and fungal disease.” Pull any spent annuals and finished vegetable plants, disposing of or composting them. “Diseased plant material should go in the trash rather than the compost pile,” Dr. Enfield says.

Once the beds are cleared, Dr. Enfield says to “amend the soil before it freezes. Working organic matter, like compost, composted manure, or a bagged soil into garden beds in fall gives it the entire winter to work into the soil structure, so it is ready to support planting in spring. Spread three inches over cleared beds and work it into the top six inches of native soil before winter sets in.”

Some plants are tough enough to make it through the winter without much support, but others can benefit from some TLC in the autumn. For those plants that aren’t hardy enough to withstand winter in your climate, you’ll need to break out some of the aforementioned materials: burlap, straw, frost cloth, and horticultural fleece. Amanda Webb, head gardener at Windy Hill Farm and Preserve, notes that “burlap is particularly useful for protecting exposed evergreens from winter wind, while straw can provide insulation around the crowns of marginally hardy perennials. I prefer breathable materials over plastic, which can trap moisture and create problems when temperatures fluctuate.”

Shrubs and other borderline hardy plants also need to take cover from the cold. “Some plants, particularly broadleaf evergreens like rhododendrons, hollies, and boxwoods, as well as any shrubs or perennials that are on the edge of their cold hardiness zone, benefit from physical protection over winter,” says Dr. Enfield. Burlap is the most commonly used material for this purpose; it can be wrapped loosely around individual plants or used to construct a simple windscreen that protects evergreens from the desiccating winter winds and sun, which can cause browning and leaf scorch even in plants that are otherwise cold-hardy. “Avoid wrapping plants in plastic, which traps moisture and heat and can do more harm than good. Burlap breathes, which is what makes it the material of choice.” For young or newly planted trees, wrapping the trunk with tree wrap or burlap protects against sun scald, “which is the cracking of bark caused by rapid temperature changes on the south and southwest sides of the trunk during winter thaws.”

Frost protection will help shield these potted plants from freezing temperatures.

Gardening doesn’t have to stop in winter; you just have to focus on plants that thrive in the cold. “I can’t stand seeing a vegetable bed sitting bare all winter,” says Mirela Bajic, senior garden designer at House Designer. “If you’re not growing anything in it, put something in for the soil’s sake. Winter rye is brilliant for that, [as] the roots hold everything together through the bad weather and then you just cut it back come spring.” She adds that many plants are more than rugged enough to make it through frosty, even snowy seasons. “Kale, cabbage, leeks, chard, [and more] all keep going through the cold if you let them.” Winter-ready crops like these help hold the soil together, provide some lovely greenery amid the gray backdrop of wintertime, and can even be used in hearty cold-weather meals.

It’s imperative that you tend to your property’s sprinkler systems and hoses, says Webb. “Drain irrigation systems and hoses if freezing temperatures are expected,” she says, or you risk catastrophic damage once water in the lines freezes and expands.

First, you’ll need to shut off the water supply to your sprinklers and hoses, removing the latter and putting them away for winter. Then, blow out sprinkler lines using compressed air to expel any residual water from the pipes. You should use a compressor set to 80 PSI or lower to avoid damaging sprinkler heads in the process. Once the lines are clear, leave the water shut off at the source inside your home (or garage or greenhouse or wherever the main water connection point is found) while leaving external valves partially open so last drips can fall and trapped water has a place to expand if it freezes.

Just because it’s too cold outside to grow colorful flowers or tasty tomatoes, peppers, or herbs doesn’t mean you can’t keep the growing going on indoors, and no dedicated greenhouse is needed, either. With a self-watering hydroponic growing system like a Gardyn Studio 2, you can grow full-sized plants indoors, including the same flowers and edible plants you’d place in a raised garden bed outdoors. If indoor space is at a premium, you can also use a smaller countertop system like a LetPot that can be used with a smaller number of flowers or food plants.

All those leaves you painstakingly rake or blow into a pile need not be bagged up and sent off to the dump; you can recycle it all to make your own mulch, which can preserve and nurture your garden throughout the winter. It’s best to use a leaf shredder to break down fallen leaves into smaller bits, rather than simply spreading collected leaves out atop your beds. You can also use a lawn mower to break down leaves, though this will dull the blade with extended use. If you have a wood chipper, you can use branches and brambles to make mulch as well. Even grass clippings can make great mulch, provided you dry it in the sun first and mix it with some thicker material, like those shredded leaves or chipped branches.

You don’t have to remove every dead flower, be it a truly finished annual or a dormant perennial, says Stembel, of Farmgirl Flowers. “One thing I’m trying to get better at is not cleaning everything up just because it looks messy. There are flowers that went from stunning to eyesores everywhere, seed heads, and so many perennials that are better off being left alone for a while so the foliage can die back naturally and the plants can store energy for next year. A lot of those seed heads and stems also feed birds and give beneficial insects places to overwinter.” Sometimes, the best thing you can do to winterize your garden is to leave it alone.

You should plant bulbs in the autumn when soil temperatures are consistently holding at around 55 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, and about six weeks before you expect the first frost. “[It’s] my absolute favorite fall ‘chore,’ planting the bulbs,” says Stembel. “This fall we’re planting thousands of daffodils, tulips, and drumstick alliums around the farm. I know thousands sounds excessive [but] bulbs might be my favorite kind of delayed gratification. By spring, I’ll mostly have forgotten that we trudged outside on cold, rainy fall days and dug what felt like endless trenches to plant all of them until the little green sprouts start popping through the soil. Those first flowers bring us so much joy right when we need it most [after] months of cold, gray weather. In fall, I wonder why we planted so many, but by spring I’ll wonder why we didn’t plant more.”

MILWAUKEE — The Brewers and Rays have bullied their way through the postseason. Sure, they've had their share of fights. Tampa Bay needed six games to get past the Guardians. Yet while their presence here is widely viewed as a triumph for two of baseball's smaller spenders, who entered the postseason with a combined payroll of just $241.8 million, their success comes against the backdrop of a brewing labor war.

And depending on which side of the bargaining table you sit, this World Series is being viewed as either an argument for a salary cap or evidence that baseball doesn't need one.

"I think the fact that two teams like the Brewers and the Rays, we hear all the time that we play baseball the right way, that it's exciting to watch," Brewers owner Mark Attanasio said during Thursday's news conference when asked about a salary cap. "I think they probably hear the same. I think that's good for baseball.

"So whatever that means relative to revenue systems or otherwise, I think if you have a more competitive set, you can have more exciting baseball."

"This has been our goal for 22 seasons, to compete," Attanasio added. "And that said, I think the system that's being proposed, which is similar to other leagues in terms of revenue-sharing system, not only will help us compete but more importantly all of the teams with lower payrolls too, which is something also I think the fans want."

Mega-agent Scott Boras sees things differently. He believes the current system works, as evidenced by the success of the Brewers and Rays. A salary cap, in his view, isn't the answer. Rather, it's about ownership groups making smart decisions, investing in talent and fully committing to building a winner, regardless of market size.

"Look, in the major-league pools, there are a lot of ownership groups that swim, and there's a lot of owners that just choose to wear their floaties all the time," Boras said. "And so, how do they get rid of their floaties? And the answer is, they either have to do it through talent, or they have to do it through investment and acquisition of talent to go out and compete."

Boras pointed to the NBA as a cautionary tale, arguing that basketball's salary-cap system has limited owners' ability to reach deep into their pockets to retain star players, even homegrown talents such as former Celtics star Jaylen Brown, who was traded to the 76ers in July in part because of salary-cap constraints and the financial implications of keeping multiple stars on massive contracts.

"We don't want things like what happened in the NBA, where LeBron James is making $4 million, worth $60 or $70 million," Boras added. "And the Boston owner wants to keep his stars, but he has to sell them, and they can't reward their talent accumulation and intellect as owners because of a false system."

Investment goes beyond the product on the field. The Rays and Brewers have shown that establishing the right culture and organizational infrastructure is just as important to sustained success.

The Rays, for example, have Kevin Cash, a Tampa Bay native and the longest-tenured manager in baseball, in their dugout. Then there's Pat Murphy, who spent eight years in the Brewers organization before taking over as manager ahead of the 2024 season. The culture of both organizations runs through those two men.

But the identities of these franchises extend well beyond their managers. Both teams know exactly who they are, and their commitment to scouting and analytics reflects that. According to MLB Network, the Rays (80) and Brewers (64) have two of the largest scouting departments in baseball, while both rank among the top seven in analytics department size. Those figures don't necessarily reflect how much each organization spends, but they underscore the emphasis both place on identifying, evaluating and developing talent.

"It starts with people and process," said Matt Arnold, the Brewers' president of baseball operations, who spent nearly a decade with the Rays before joining Milwaukee in 2015. "[The Rays] have had an incredible run. Really good people that are scattered all throughout the industry at this point. We have a little bit of a different formula here, and they have a really good formula there obviously, and we learned a lot from each other."

The two sides have learned plenty from one another. And in the looming labor war, they'll find themselves at the center of the debate, too.

For Boras, the heat should be on the owners wearing floaties, not the economic structure of the sport.

"The mere fact that some owners choose not to invest, and they're profiting at high levels, making $100 million a year, and owning a franchise and not being successful. That is not a reason to change the system," Boras said.
