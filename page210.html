#5225. Bitcoin ETFs Record $773 Million in Two-Day Outflows as BTC Price Tests $80,000

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

U.S. spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds (ETFs) recorded $137.94 million in net outflows on Oct. 23, according to SoSoValue data.

Combined with the previous day's $635.49 million in withdrawals, the largest single-day outflow since late June, total net outflows over the two sessions reached $773.43 million.

BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) recorded the largest outflow on Oct. 23, at $110.34 million. Fidelity's Wise Origin Bitcoin Fund (FBTC) saw $31.78 million leave the fund, while Morgan Stanley's MSBT recorded net inflows of $4.19 million.

Despite the withdrawals, weekly Bitcoin ETF flows remained positive at approximately $228.2 million.

The outflows came after news that Franklin Templeton, an asset manager with $1.8 trillion in assets, received Nasdaq approval to list two Bitcoin-focused ETFs, according to U.Today.

The Franklin US Equity Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF (FBEQ) and Franklin US Innovation Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF (FIBT) are designed to combine exposure to U.S. equities with a strategy aimed at gradually accumulating Bitcoin.

Unlike conventional spot Bitcoin ETFs, the funds are not primarily designed to track BTC's price directly.

Bitcoin trades near $80,550, down 1.32% from the previous day, leaving the cryptocurrency under pressure despite the news of Franklin Templeton's new funds.

On the daily chart, BTC remained above its major moving averages. The 100-day moving average stood near $77,500, while the 200-day average was around $76,200. Both indicators were rising, providing support for the broader trend.

The immediate support level is around $80,000, followed by the 100-day moving average near $77,500. A break below $80,000 could expose Bitcoin to further selling pressure.

Elevated open interest adds to the risk. If BTC loses the $80,000 level while leveraged positions remain high, forced liquidations could accelerate the decline toward the 100-day moving average.

For now, Bitcoin faces mixed signals. Positive weekly ETF flows and rising moving averages offer some support, while substantial recent withdrawals and the potential for leveraged liquidations could weigh on the price.

#4665. Why volunteers are gluing seeds to clam shells in Connecticut

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 11:18 AM EDT

Environmental restoration sometimes requires out-of-the-box thinking. Along the waters of Long Island Sound in New York and Connecticut, experts are tackling the challenge of restoring crucial eelgrass habitats by gluing the plant’s seeds to clams.

Eelgrass (Zostera marina) is an important member of the ecosystem. It provides foraging areas and shelter for young fish and invertebrates, diminishes coastal erosion, draws in excess nutrients, filters water, and releases oxygen, for instance. Compared to the 1930s, eelgrass meadows in and around Long Island Sound have decreased by more than 90 percent, according to regional environmental action group Save the Sound.

To make matters worse, eelgrass is difficult to restore and there have been a lot of failed attempts, Bill Lucey, a Long Island Soundkeeper from the organization, tells Popular Science. In 2025, the team tried a different approach—recruiting clams as underwater gardeners. The strategy was originally ideated by Robert Vasiluth, the founder of Submerged Aquatic Vegetation EngineersEnvironmental (SAVE) .

The approach consists of attaching eelgrass seeds to the clam’s shells. When the clams are released into the sound, they bury into the sediment, taking the seeds down to the bottom and effectively planting them. When they conducted drone imagery to check in on their results this year, they found that the eelgrass meadow coverage had increased by 178 percent.

“It needs to survive for three years before we can really trumpet our horns and say it’s working, but the actual method of putting clams out and seeds attached to them really worked. The generation of the plants was incredible,” Lucey explains.

During their 2025 planting, the team used glue typically used in coral reef restoration to attach seeds to the clams. But then Connecticut College biologist Maria Rosa yielded better germination rates when she used wet pieces of rice paper (the kind you make spring rolls with) to seal the seeds against the shell. The Save the Sound team asked its volunteers to do the same earlier this week.

“The problem with the spring roll wrappers is yesterday [Monday, October 19], when we deployed them for the first time in scale, they weren’t all staying on,” says Lucey. “So what we’ve started doing today [Tuesday, October 20] is putting a little pocket of seeds, 10 to 20 eelgrass seeds on the top of the clam, putting a little circle of this [coral] glue around it, and then putting the spring roll wrapper stamp over that and wetting it. And so it keeps the seeds moist, and the glue holds the spring roll wrapper to the clam till it dissolves, and then it’ll release the seeds next to the clam.”

Save the Sound is aiming to release 10,000 claims and 300,000 seeds this season.

#627. Scientists discover a hidden switch that burns fat and blocks new fat cells

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a remarkable protein switch that influences whether cells burn fat or store it. Disabling this protein increases fat consumption and interferes with the formation of new fat cells, raising the possibility of a different approach to treating obesity. Subsequent research has strengthened the discovery, revealing that the same protein helps regulate how much energy the body burns.

What if one of the keys to fighting obesity lies not in suppressing appetite, but in changing how the body's cells use energy?

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a protein called MTCH2, nicknamed "Mitch," that plays an important role in controlling metabolism. When scientists disabled this protein in laboratory experiments, cells began consuming more fuel, including fats and carbohydrates. They also found that removing Mitch could interfere with the process through which immature cells develop into fat cells.

The discovery builds on earlier experiments in mice, in which switching off Mitch in muscle tissue increased energy expenditure, improved endurance, and protected the animals from diet-related obesity.

The findings, published in The EMBO Journal in January 2025, suggest that manipulating this protein could eventually offer a way to influence both fat burning and fat accumulation. Additional research published later that year has provided further evidence of its importance in regulating body fat and metabolism.

Why Scientists Are Searching for Better Obesity Treatments

Over the past decade, medications designed to promote weight loss have transformed obesity treatment. Drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have helped many patients achieve substantial reductions in body weight, largely by influencing appetite and the body's responses to food.

But losing weight does not necessarily mean losing only fat.

Weight reduction can also involve a decline in lean body mass, which includes muscle and other tissues. Preserving muscle is especially important because it supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

A 2026 analysis of 20 clinical trials involving 15,782 participants found that lean mass accounted for approximately 25% to 39% of the weight lost with several widely used weight-loss medications. Importantly, the researchers found that comparable proportional losses also occurred with lifestyle interventions, while adding resistance training helped preserve more lean tissue.

These concerns have encouraged scientists to investigate ways to reduce body fat while protecting the muscles needed for long-term health.

Professor Atan Gross and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute arrived at a possible avenue through an unexpected discovery involving Mitch.

Switching Off One Protein Protected Mice From Obesity

In earlier experiments, Gross's team reduced the activity of MTCH2 in the muscles of mice. The effects were striking.

The genetically modified animals showed increased metabolic activity and were resistant to obesity caused by their diet. Their muscles also developed more fibers with a high capacity for using oxygen, helping improve physical endurance.

The mice performed better in exercise and stress tests and showed improvements in heart function. In addition to resisting excess fat accumulation, they experienced favorable changes in their overall body composition.

The results suggested that Mitch was doing much more than controlling a single metabolic reaction. Somehow, this protein appeared to influence the body's ability to use energy, store fat, and sustain physical activity.

That raised an intriguing question: How could disabling one protein produce such widespread effects?

To investigate, the researchers turned their attention to mitochondria, the microscopic structures responsible for supplying much of the energy that cells need to function.

The Mitochondria Connection Behind Fat Burning

Mitochondria are frequently described as the powerhouses of cells because they convert energy from food into a usable form called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate.

ATP acts as a cellular energy currency. Muscles use it to contract, cells need it to grow and repair themselves, and countless biological processes depend on a steady supply.

However, mitochondria are not static structures. They constantly change shape, joining together into larger networks or separating into smaller units.

When mitochondria fuse, they can form interconnected systems that help coordinate energy production. When they divide, the resulting structures may operate differently, altering how efficiently cells produce and consume energy.

Gross's team had previously established that Mitch plays a major role in mitochondrial fusion, the process that allows separate mitochondria to merge.

Without this protein, the mitochondrial network becomes fragmented. Although the cells can still generate energy, they experience changes in energy efficiency and demand that can force them to consume more fuel.

This offered a possible explanation for the unusual metabolism observed in the mice. Instead of conserving energy efficiently, their cells appeared to be operating in a more energy-intensive state.

But would disabling Mitch have a similar effect in human cells?

Human Cells Begin Burning More Fat Without Mitch

To answer that question, researchers led by doctoral student Sabita Chourasia used genetic engineering to remove MTCH2 from human cells grown in the laboratory.

The change disrupted the normal structure of their mitochondrial networks. Individual mitochondria became more separated, and the balance between energy production and energy demand shifted.

The cells developed a persistent shortage of readily available energy relative to their needs, even as their mitochondria increased their oxygen-consuming activity.

To compensate, they began consuming more nutrients.

"After deleting Mitch, we examined, every few hours, the effect that had on more than 100 substances taking part in metabolism in human cells," Chourasia explains. "We saw an increase in cellular respiration, the process in which the cell produces energy from nutrients, such as carbohydrates and fats, using oxygen. This explains the increase in muscular endurance in previous experiments using mice."

Cellular respiration is the series of chemical reactions that allows cells to extract usable energy from nutrients. Increasing this activity generally requires a greater supply of fuel.

The researchers found that cells lacking Mitch consumed more fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Fat metabolism was particularly revealing.

Rather than relying on their usual mix of energy sources, the altered cells increased their use of fatty acids for fuel. This indicated that MTCH2 influences not only how much energy cells consume, but also which nutrients they use to meet that demand.

"We discovered that deleting Mitch led to a major drop in fats in membranes," Gross explains. "At the same time, we saw an increase in fatty substances used to produce energy, and we realized that the fat was being broken down from the membrane to be used as fuel. In other words, we showed that Mitch determines the fate of fat in human cells."

Cell membranes are thin, flexible barriers made largely from fatty molecules called lipids. They protect cells and organize many of their essential functions.

The findings suggested that cells deprived of Mitch were changing how they distributed and used these lipids, drawing on some of their available fatty resources to support increased energy consumption.

Detailed analysis also revealed changes in stored lipids, demonstrating that the effect was more complex than simply eliminating cellular fat. Overall, the results showed that MTCH2 is a powerful regulator of how cells allocate nutrients between energy production and other biological needs.

The researchers then investigated whether Mitch also influences how fat cells develop.

This question was particularly interesting because previous studies had found elevated MTCH2 levels in fat tissue from women with obesity.

Fat cells, also known as adipocytes, do not appear fully formed. They develop from immature precursor cells through a process called adipogenesis, or fat cell differentiation.

As these precursor cells mature, they must build new cellular structures, produce fats, and activate genes that allow them to specialize in storing energy.

All of this requires considerable energy and raw materials.

Gross's team suspected that removing Mitch might disrupt the process by creating an environment in which cells were too busy consuming fuel to build the components needed for fat storage.

Experiments using mouse precursor fat cells supported this idea. When the researchers deleted MTCH2, far fewer cells successfully developed into mature fat cells.

In the published experiments, approximately 80% to 90% of normal precursor cells differentiated within six days, compared with only 5% to 10% of cells lacking MTCH2. The altered cells also accumulated substantially fewer lipid droplets, the structures used to store fat.

"When we deleted Mitch from the progenitor cells, we discovered that the environment created in these cells was not conducive to the synthesis of new fats," Gross explains. "Reducing the ability to synthesize membranes prevents the cells from growing, developing and reaching the point where differentiation is possible. The process of fat accumulation requires a large amount of available energy, but in cells without Mitch, there is a shortage of energy. In addition, the expression of genes necessary for differentiation is suppressed, and there is a shortage of the substances vital for this process to occur. As a result, differentiation of new fat cells is reduced, along with fat accumulation."

In other words, disabling Mitch appeared to affect fat metabolism in two distinct ways. It increased the consumption of energy-rich nutrients and interfered with the cellular machinery needed to develop new fat-storing cells.

Importantly, the fat cell differentiation experiments used a mouse-derived cell line, while the detailed analysis of nutrient consumption was conducted in human cells. The combined results suggest a broader role for the protein, but they do not establish that blocking MTCH2 would prevent new fat cells from forming in people.

Follow-Up Research Reveals Another Fat-Burning Mechanism

The importance of MTCH2 became even clearer in research published on October 3, 2025, in Nature Communications.

An international research team, which included Gross and Chourasia, investigated how MTCH2 influences metabolism in fat tissue. By analyzing human and mouse fat samples, studying cultured fat cells, and conducting experiments in genetically modified mice, the scientists identified another mechanism linking this protein to energy expenditure.

They found that MTCH2 helps regulate the movement of fatty acids into mitochondria, where these molecules can be broken down to produce energy.

The process involves an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, or CPT1. This enzyme acts as an important checkpoint, helping determine how much fat can enter mitochondria for use as fuel.

Normally, CPT1 activity is regulated by a molecule called malonyl-CoA, which signals when cells should limit fatty acid consumption.

The researchers discovered that MTCH2 physically interacts with CPT1 and influences how strongly this regulatory signal affects the enzyme.

Removing MTCH2 altered that control system, allowing mitochondria to increase fatty acid utilization and energy expenditure.

This provided a more detailed molecular explanation for why disabling the protein could encourage cells to burn additional fat.

Mice Burned More Energy Without Eating Less

The follow-up study also produced an especially promising result.

When researchers removed MTCH2 specifically from the fat cells of mice, the animals burned more energy without increasing their physical activity or changing how much food they consumed.

Under a regular diet, the genetically modified mice gained weight at a rate similar to that of normal mice.

However, when the animals were fed a high-fat diet, a difference emerged.

Mice lacking MTCH2 in their fat tissue gained less weight and accumulated less body fat than untreated mice, despite consuming similar amounts of food.

Their lean body mass remained comparable to that of the control animals.

The genetically modified mice also demonstrated improved glucose tolerance, indicating that their bodies were better able to manage blood sugar after receiving glucose. In addition, their cholesterol levels were lower.

These findings strengthened the evidence that MTCH2 influences whole-body metabolism, rather than simply changing the behavior of isolated cells.

The scientists also found that its metabolic effects could occur independently of UCP1, a protein commonly associated with heat production in brown fat. Brown fat is a specialized form of body fat that helps generate heat by burning energy, whereas white fat primarily stores excess energy.

Discovering an additional pathway for increasing energy expenditure could be important because it offers researchers another potential target for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Could This Protein Lead to Better Weight-Loss Treatments?

Together, the findings suggest that MTCH2 may be an important molecular switch linking fat storage, energy consumption, and the development of fat cells.

The original experiments showed that removing the protein could increase nutrient consumption in human cells and interfere with fat cell formation in mouse cells. The later research demonstrated that selectively removing MTCH2 from fat tissue could increase energy expenditure and protect mice against excessive weight gain on a high-fat diet.

That combination raises the possibility of developing treatments that change how the body handles energy, potentially complementing medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.

However, several major questions remain unanswered.

Scientists have not yet demonstrated that inhibiting MTCH2 produces safe, sustained weight loss in humans. The protein also participates in other important biological activities, including mitochondrial regulation and pathways involved in cell survival. Blocking it throughout the body could therefore produce unwanted consequences.

Preventing fat cells from forming is not necessarily beneficial in every situation, either. Healthy fat tissue plays an essential role in storing excess energy, maintaining blood sugar balance, and producing hormones. Disrupting its function could potentially redirect fats into organs where they may cause harm.

Researchers will need to determine whether MTCH2 can be targeted safely, which tissues would benefit most, and whether reducing its activity would preserve muscle health while promoting fat loss.

For now, established strategies such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake remain important for protecting muscle during weight reduction. A 2026 review of clinical trials found that combining lifestyle changes with resistance training produced a more favorable pattern of lean mass preservation than weight-loss interventions without such exercise.

The research nevertheless points toward a compelling possibility: Rather than focusing exclusively on how much food the body takes in, future obesity treatments might also influence how cells decide whether to burn energy or store it.

And one protein, Mitch, appears to be deeply involved in that decision.

The original study, titled "MTCH2 controls energy demand and expenditure to fuel anabolism during adipogenesis," was published in The EMBO Journal on January 3, 2025.

The research was led by Sabita Chourasia and Professor Atan Gross at the Weizmann Institute of Science.

It involved an international collaboration, including researchers affiliated with ETH Zurich, Weizmann Institute of Science, and other institutions.

#515. 9 Unique Apps to Try on the iPhone Duo

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Despite seven years of Android folding phones, Apple's iPhone Duo launch has spurred developers to create quirky, weird, and fun apps that experiment with the folding design (leaving Android owners feeling like second-class citizens yet again).

I've reviewed almost every folding phone in the past decade, and my inbox has never seen this many app pitches from indie developers wanting to show off their app for Apple's first foldable.

Some of these apps aren't necessarily new but use the phone's hinge to offer fresh experiences. You'll also find a “Made for iPhone Duo” badge on apps that make best use of the larger screens on the App Store. From a game that has you closing the phone to grill a piece of toast to a clever way to memorize flash cards, here are nine unique apps to try on your new Apple handset.

Board games will have a field day on the iPhone Duo, and Battlefleet Duo is the best example. On the larger 7.6-inch screen, you get a sprawling canvas, but in tabletop mode, you can view your ships on the bottom screen and the enemy's fleet above. That's especially great if you have a friend with the iPhone Duo, as you can play local multiplayer and sit across from each other just like you would with the old-school Battleships. You can play two free battles every day, or unlock unlimited battles with a one-time $5 purchase—no ads or subscription.

Reminiscing about the long-lost days of the pre-smartphone era? Flipcorder offers simple pleasure, turning your iPhone Duo into a camcorder, so you can make new memories that feel like the old days. Open the app, and the outer display will show a “closed lens”—open the Duo to “take the cap off,” then start recording. The user interface is retro, and video clips have a filter to emulate VHS (it has a MiniDV filter too). I really like that you can view your “tapes” and everything is cataloged by day, so you can see 30 minutes of footage in one stitched-together clip.

As you’re recording, the person on the other end can see a preview of the video on the outer display—that kind of dual-display experience is only available to camera apps, so if you were hoping to read a book and have the outer display appear as the book cover, that's sadly not possible at the moment.

Flashcard apps are a dime a dozen, but nothing quite captures the physical experience of flipping around a flashcard like RectoVerso. This $13 app lets you import your own text—your classroom notes or even a PDF—and uses Apple Intelligence to automatically create flashcards on the subject matter. Alternatively, you can import decks from Anki, another popular flashcard app, or write your own. The magic is when you're actually studying. Don't know the answer? Slightly open the iPhone Duo, and the answer will gradually appear—fully open, and you get the full answer. That way you can bang your hand on the desk and say, “Ah, I totally knew that.”

Who doesn't love a rhythm game? Pop your iPhone Duo into a 90-degree angle and let the blocks in PianoFold reach the middle crease—that's when you tap the corresponding column. You need to time your taps perfectly, unless you enjoy listening to Bach get butchered in real time. There are 40 levels across eight chapters, and the game rates your performance (out of three stars) at the end of each song. You can retry as many times as you'd like. The first two chapters are free, though you can also play one song for free every day. For full access, you'll have to pony up $5. There are no ads and no subscription.

When I showed my wife Toastie, she remarked that it might be the first time she ever wanted an iPhone (she uses a Pixel). That's high praise! This delightful $3 app asks you to construct a sandwich on the iPhone Duo's inner screen. Each recipe has specific instructions you have to follow, and when you're done, you'll need to close the iPhone for a few seconds—however many the recipe says—to “grill” your sandwich. Open the phone back up, and it's ready to serve! That is, if you haven't burned it. There are only 10 recipes, so you'll probably get through this quickly. But hey, at least you can say you've used the world's most expensive toaster.

Want to focus? Or relax? Portal lets you play relaxing soundscapes—listen to a volcano's bubbling lava or waves crashing on a beach—but you can also see these sights on the Duo's 7.6-inch canvas, in tabletop mode, or on the outer screen in tent mode. It's very relaxing and an excellent companion for travelers who rely on a sound machine to fall asleep. You can also follow calming breath exercises, or set focus timers to get stuff done. The app boasts more than 100 vistas, from Iceland's black beaches to enchanting UK woodlands. The app is free, though you can only access three portals refreshed monthly. Full access costs $3 a month, $30 per year, or $100 for a lifetime membership.

If you like the idea of Portal, you might want to check out Fold Focus, though it's designed to help you get work done rather than relax. Close the Duo to focus on a task, and the pixel animation on the outer display will start building a city. Open your phone before the timer ends and everything will come crashing down.

Want to try a new calendar app? Maybe your current one isn't optimized for the Duo (ahem, Google Calendar). Try FirstSeed Planner. The developer has fully enabled Duo support, letting you view the calendar on one screen and a daily planner on the other. It supports drag-and-drop actions between the two halves of the screen, and I also love the space at the bottom for writing notes for the day. And when Apple enables stylus support on the Duo later this year, you can handwrite in FirstSeed, making your Duo feel more like a paper planner. It's free, but the Pro version costs $3.49 per month, $30 per year, or $90 for lifetime access. The paid versions unlock features like automatic backups and weather forecasts.

If you’re big on retro gaming, you’ve probably heard of Delta. The popular emulator just rolled out its biggest update yet, headlined by support for the folding iPhone Duo. Prop the Duo at a 90-degree angle, and custom skins transform the device into a classic clamshell like the Nintendo DS—complete with automatic sleep mode when you close it. Just remember: It’s strictly an emulator, so you’ll still need to source your own ROMs responsibly.

Bring your favorite PCs and Macs back to life with Tiny Computers. This $4 app houses a library of 10 computers, from the Commodore 64 to the iMac G3. Fold the Duo into tabletop mode to bring the museum of machines to life—a vintage screen up top and a functional, clickable keyboard below. The operating systems are interactive, so you can run retro paint apps, secret terminal commands, or kill time with classic desktop games like Solitaire.

#5229. 75% XRP Burn Rate Surge Prints Bullish Outlook for Price

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Although XRP has continued to show mixed price action, recent onchain data from XRPScan shows that its network activity is growing.

The wonderful counter to rising prices at the very top end of the watch world is that, frankly, things have never looked better for those seeking proper mechanical watches on a budget. Entry-level timepieces keep getting better and cheaper—and this isn't just from newly established microbrands hoping to make a name for themselves, but from the major players, too.

Recently, I was asked to pick a couple of wallet-friendly watches for WIRED's Gear newsletter, and it sent me down a glorious rabbit hole where I was genuinely surprised by superb timepieces well below what WIRED has previously set for affordable watches (the sub-$1,000 bracket). Far from dropping three figures on a decent automatic, you can now get almost anything you desire from a watch for far less than $500.

To check my math and make sure I wasn't going slightly crazy, I reached out to WIRED's wider circle of watch experts with a devilishly tricky, unenviable challenge: Pick their favorite mechanical watch for less than $400, the sort of price where quartz watches dominate. It was a hard ask, but they delivered. Here's the list—all are exceptional value, and watches we'd be delighted to sport on our wrists.

Caravelle has no right to make a mechanical dive watch this attractive and capable for a mere $300, but here it is. The secret to its timeless look is that Bulova's budget brand sensibly fashioned this modern Sea Hunter heavily on the diver's design from the late 1960s, while bumping the size up to a more modern 39 mm. Elegant and stylish, it's practically a GADA (go anywhere, do anything) watch. But with 200 meters of waterproofing, a 120-click unidirectional bezel, and a 42-hour power reserve, the Sea Hunter is a compelling sports watch. Throw on a rubber strap for diving, a nato for casual (our pick is Artem), or stick with the three-link steel bracelet for a more classic look.

Unquestionably one of the best-value automatic dress watches you can buy today, the $295 Orient Bambino’s timeless 38-mm design with white dial and leather strap has been carefully shrunken down from the original 40.5-mm casing, making this piece incredibly wearable (both in these smaller watch times and when case sizes inevitably get larger again.) You can view the automatic Japanese movement through the exhibition case back, and there's even 30 meters of water resistance—not that you should be swimming with such a watch. Want something less traditional? Go for the pink dial with a stainless steel bracelet.

This new smaller take on Brew's original Metric Retro is the choice of Mike Christensen, British GQ's lifestyle director and watch editor. Cashing in on the current trend for very small watches, Mike loves that this 30-mm piece is wearable for both men and women. “This li'l Brew number is so retro they decided to call it exactly that,” he says. At just $375, the stainless steel Metric Lite Retro, complete with a matching bracelet, uses an automatic movement visible through the open caseback. It has 50 meters of water resistance, and even sapphire crystal atop the striking dial. Proper retro charm that's forgiving on the bank balance.

Chris Hall, a watch expert who writes for WIRED, The New York Times, Financial Times, and others, as well as his excellent The Fourth Wheel Substack, recommends the Timex Marlin 1965 Reissue Hand-Wound at $259. “It was the model that marked Timex’s return to mechanical watches about a decade ago, and unlike so many watches at this level, it isn’t a rip-off of something else: It’s a tribute to one of Timex’s own designs,” Hall says. “It’s small, quirky, and very much its own thing. Looking a little further afield can often pay off in the watch world. A prime example is the Sea-Gull 1963, a hand-wound mechanical chronograph based on a vintage pilot's timepiece originally built for the Chinese Air Force back in 1961. Many call this watch the gateway drug to watch collecting, but there are a few different versions. Here we've gone for the “International Edition,” which seems to be a budget model of the 1963 Sea-Gull, as it sells alternative iterations for more on its own website.
