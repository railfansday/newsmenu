#75. Will elections in Israel change its Iran approach?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As Israelis head to the polls, the country's conflict with Iran casts a long shadow over the ballot box. But any leadership changes in Israel may not be enough to shake up its grand strategy on Tehran.

Israel's vote this Tuesday is the first general election for the country since the Hamas attack on October 7, 2023 — an assault which led to one of the deepest security crises in Israel's history.

As incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu fights to retain power, the question is not merely who will lead Israel's next government, but also how that government will navigate the region-wide security crisis that was triggered by the attack and the Israeli reaction, which included launching a war against Iran.

US President Donald Trump recently issued a full-throated endorsement of the incumbent Israeli leader as a "warrior prime minister" and warned that any other choice would affect Israel's alliance with Washington.

"If Israel has the wrong leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi (Benjamin Netanyahu), America will have to do this work ourselves, and I am not inclined to do so," Trump said in an online post, referring to the Iran conflict.

Behind the scenes, however, media reports indicated a rift between the Israeli government and the Trump administration. According to The Financial Times, the Israeli military intended to launch a new round of attacks on Iran's nuclear facilities, government centers, and energy infrastructure around October 10. However, Trump opposed the new escalation before the US midterm elections on November 3.

The Jerusalem Post later questioned the narrative, reporting that the army had no specific directive for such an operation.

The Israeli race has turned into a tight duel between Netanyahu and his main rival, Gadi Eisenkot — former IDF (Israel Defense Forces) chief of staff and leader of the front-running centrist party "Yashar."

But Raz Zimmt, the head of the Iran program at the Tel Aviv-based Institute for National Security Studies (INSS), warns political divisions between the two options are not deep enough to speak of a "fundamental change in Tel Aviv's approach toward Tehran" after the election.

"In the perception of Israel's left, right, and center factions regarding the Iranian threat, especially its nuclear threat, there are minor differences," Zimmt told DW.

Moreover, according to Zimmt, any military escalation against Iran depends "on the decision of the US president" far more than on the identity of Israel's prime minister.

"Overall, I do not expect Israel's policy toward Iran and its proxies to undergo dramatic changes, regardless of the future government's composition," he said.

INSS senior fellow Danny Citrinovitch agreed that one should not expect "fundamental changes" in Israel's Iran policy depending on the election outcome.

"Even if Netanyahu personally steps down from power, his influence and the policies shaped over recent years will not simply vanish," Citrinovitch told DW.

The current consensus on Iran can be traced to the Hamas terror attack three years ago. Before the assault, according to Navid Toobian from the US-based Middle East Media Research Institute, many believed Israel should deal with the Islamic Republic "in a limited and managed way" and confront only the arms of the so-called "Axis of Resistance." But October 7 completely changed the rules of the game.

While Israel had been aware of Iran's slogans calling for its destruction, it was believed that such calls had an "internal aspect" to rally the base of hardcore Islamists in Iran, Toobian said.

The terror strike by the Iran-allied Hamas, however, showed that Tehran "spares no effort to strike Israel."

Damon Golriz, a researcher at the Hague Institute for Geopolitics, also points to October 7 as the trigger of the "largest historical trauma of the Jewish nation."

"Preserving Iran's nuclear ambitions and continuous threats against Israel has intensified this fear that for a large portion of Israeli society, today's rulers in Iran have become a symbol of a historical threat," he told DW.

With that in mind, the current US and Israeli policy towards Iran follows three vectors, according to Golriz.

"First, the removal of the regime's leaders; second, industrial and infrastructural collapse," and third, orchestrating a civil war and what he called "geographic scissors" to dismember Iran with the help of armed Kurdish and Baluch separatists, he said.

The US and Israel, however, are not the only players in the conflict. In a recent report, the Reuters news agency cited senior Iranian security officials as saying that Tehran has prepared detailed operational plans for retaliatory strikes. Tehran warned that their retaliatory tools include destroying undersea fiber-optic communication cables in the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz, alongside targeting US-dependent data centers and AI infrastructure in the region.

Commenting on possible alternatives to military escalation, Golriz highlights a diplomatic opportunity that opened six months ago in Islamabad, when Tehran agreed to never initiate war or military operations against Israel.

"For the first time in the history of the Islamic Republic, the fundamental national security interests of Israel were signed by the presidents of Iran and the United States," according to Gorliz.

The first article of the Islamabad framework could have been "the beginning of the end for 'Death to America' slogans and anti-Jewish threats, including 'Death to Israel," Golriz said.

This diplomatic window was quickly closed, but "with political will, the provisions and spirit of the Islamabad Memorandum will be the first strategic step toward lasting peace," he said.

But INSS senior fellow Citrinovitch was less optimistic when assessing the willingness of Israeli elites to return to diplomacy.

"Currently, returning to the nuclear deal has no place in Israel's political arena," he told DW.

#64. Harry Kane: Ballon d’Or for Bayern Munich striker

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane has won football's most prestigious individual prize, beating Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal. Kane has developed even more in the Bundesliga despite an uneven league.

To most Bayern Munich fans under the age of 40, Karl-Heinz Rummenigge was the club CEO who regularly spoke his mind. Fans with longer memories, or a better grasp of Bavarian football history, recall Rummenigge as one of the club's all-time great players. He was also the last Bayern man to win the Ballon d'Or — in 1981. The last Bundesliga player to win was Borussia Dortmund's Matthias Sammer, in 1996.

That long drought ended on Monday, with Harry Kane, born 12 years after Rummenigge's win, picking up the 2026 trophy at a ceremony held, as a one-off, in his native London.

"I am almost speechless. Being here in London has made it extra special,” he said on stage.

Not many people would have thought it, and maybe even I wouldn't have thought I'd be this far," Kane later told British sports streaming plaftorm DAZN.

Lamine Yamal, the prodigious Barcelona and Spain forward who won the Euros at 16, finished third. At the end of the 2025-26 season, on which the award is judged, Yamal won the World Cup with Spain as well as the La Liga title. Kylian Mbappe came in second.

Kane's 73 goals for club and country last season made for a strong case, and the Bundesliga's most expensive signing of all time has already racked up 156 goals in 159 games for the serial German champions. But it's not just the numbers that tell the story, according to his club coach.

"People in England saw him as a goalscorer during his time playing there," Vincent Kompany said of Kane last season. "We've seen other qualities too, such as his footballing intelligence. We can appreciate that now — it was perhaps somewhat underestimated in England. He's like a fine wine that improves with age. I also enjoy that aspect of his game."

Kane continues to improve at 33, a point when many footballers start to regress. Though he is helped by being more reliant on guile and technique than pace, Kane's professionalism is key to staying at the top, according to his Bayern teammate Serge Gnabry.

"He always has the right attitude, always leads the way and sets the tone," Gnabry said ahead of Bayern's 2-2 draw in the Champions League against Arsenal last week. "He never strays from his path, helps the team with his performance and is a top-tier player."

Kane scored a penalty in that match and provided a sensational pass from which Alphonso Davies scored Bayern's opener, another reminder of his all-round game. "The pass was one of my best ones," Kane said after the match. "I knew Phonzy [Davies] would be able to get there with his speed. Whatever I can do to help the team, I'll do. At the moment, it seems to be with assists more than goals."

That attitude has been with Kane from the start. His first-ever coach, Dave Bricknell, from Ridgeway Rovers, an amateur club in northeast London, told DW after Kane joined Bayern from Spurs in 2023: "Harry's mentality is his greatest strength. He doesn't let anything worry him. If he misses a chance, he doesn't care — he will go and score from another chance."

His England coach, Thomas Tuchel, has also emphasized Kane's leadership. "He is the first one to push everyone, and that is his mindset, and he became a very, very hardworking forward on top of his finishing skills, Tuchel said in September as Kane extended his England goal-scoring record against the Czech Republic. "He is a complete package."

There are detractors who point to perceived "stat-padding," or scoring against inferior opponents in international qualifiers and in the Bundesliga, where there are fewer elite teams and a lot less money, than in England. Though Kane's ratio of 213 goals in 320 games in the Premier League is a little off his Bundesliga stats, it bears comparison with any other player in the history of the league.

Kane winning the award is also a boost in profile for the Bundesliga, which has been unable to keep pace, both commercially and on the pitch, with other top European leagues of late, particularly the Premier League and La Liga.

Winning it in a league outside of England or Spain perhaps makes Kane's feat even greater. Other than Ousmane Dembele, who won it last year after Paris Saint-Germain's Champions League win, and Lionel Messi, a serial Ballon d'Or winner, the last player to take the Ballon d'Or for a season played outside England or Spain was was Kaka at AC Milan in 2007, when Italy's Serie A was considered a stronger league.

Kane's career hasn't been straightforward, something he admitted in his speech. After signing his first senior contract with Spurs in July 2010, he was sent out on various loans to lower league sides between 2011-2013 without ever making a huge impression.

"My journey wasn't straightforward. I wasn't the kid who went straight to the top. I had to experience the downs, I had a lot of loan moves, I had to fight my way to be the very best right now, and I hope that can give you determination as well," Kane said in his speech.

For former teammate Jermain Defoe, who also coached Kane at Spurs, those experiences were formative.

"You have to show a resilience. It's not easy when you go out on loan, it can feel like you're being rejected by the club that you are at, which isn't the case," he told DW in 2023, before adding that Kane's ability was always clear.

"When he joined up with the [Tottenham] first team, you could see the potential," Defoe said. "He was always a good finisher, always hit the ball clean. … With Harry you already knew, he had that natural instinct and always felt comfortable in front of goal."

Those goals have, after a failure to win a trophy in England, led to two Bundesliga titles and a German Cup. But success with England remains elusive after a 2026 World Cup exit in the semifinals. Kane will be 35 by the time of the next Euros, in the UK and Ireland in 2028, and has a deal with Bayern until the following year. His form since joining Bayern suggests that he could get better still.

It's now 10 p.m. across Israel, which means that polling stations around the country either should have closed or should be making preparations to do so as the last people in line cast their ballots.

Exit polls should follow shortly on Israeli television. These have a fairly good reputation overall but of course are only projections.

The Central Elections Committee has rejected a request by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud party and some other groups to extend voting beyond 10 p.m. (2000 UTC/GMT).

"High nationwide turnout across all sectors of society reflects strong public engagement in the democratic process, and no need to extend voting hours was identified," a commission statement said.

The committee noted, however, that this decision was based on the circumstances "as things stand," leaving open the possibility for matters to change.

For instance, voting hours have been extended in some areas, including a polling location used by Israeli settlers in Hebron, in the occupied West Bank, where a power outage led to an extension of the closing time from 8 p.m. to 10 p.m. Polling stations with fewer than 350 eligible voters typically close two hours earlier than larger polling stations in urban areas.

The commission has also said several times that anybody who started queueing before closing time will be given a chance to vote.

Artificial intelligence is emerging as a major factor in Israel's election campaign, with political parties using AI-generated videos, automated calls and online content on a large scale.

DW's Josh Axelrod examined the growing role of AI in the campaign, as researchers and analysts raise concerns about misleading content, bots and deepfakes ahead of the vote.

The expected high turnout means the number of votes needed to cross the 3.25% threshold to enter the Knesset, the Israeli parliament, is going to be higher, meaning smaller parties are at risk of not making it.

According to opinion polls from last week, the two parties that are on the threshold limit are Amcha Israel, a far-right party led by former military commander Ofer Winter, and the Reservists Party, which has not declared support for either main bloc.

Winter repeatedly said he intends to join a Netanyahu coalition, and a scenario in which his party doesn't make it to parliament puts Likud's chances of a future coalition at risk.

Either way, Israel's Central Elections Committee will have to make a decision on the matter within the hour.

Voter turnout reached 66.7%, or roughly two in every three eligible voters, by 6 p.m. local time, according to an update from the Central Elections Committee.

This remains the highest partial turnout figure since such records were first taken in 1973.

The level is only slightly clear of the previous high of 65.2% in 1973 and 1988 — but it quite considerably surpasses the 57.7% who had voted by this time in 2022.

And that figure had already been the highest 6 p.m. turnout of the 21st century before being surpassed today.

Arab voters could play a decisive role in Israel's election. With neither Benjamin Netanyahu nor Gadi Eisenkot expected to win a Knesset majority, Arab parties could become key to forming the next government.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu continued issuing appeals to supporters to go out and vote online as the day progressed.

One common theme was apparent in almost all of the posts: warnings about high turnout in Arab and to a lesser extent left-leaning communities.

"We're at war for our home," one of Netanyahu's posts read, using a phrase that appeared repeatedly in his messaging. "Muslim Brotherhood supporters are raiding the polling stations and stealing the elections. Go out and vote, recover it!!"

In another post he showed an image claiming to show a long line of voters queueing in Rahat, with a crowd of women wearing niqab coverings prominently visible at the front of the queue, suggesting that they were Muslim Palestinian voters.

Sami Abou Shahadeh, the leader of the Balad party, which is part of the alliance known as the Joint List, in turn called on his supporters to vote before the deadline expired, also highlighting similar appeals to voters from the leader of the far-right Jewish Power party, Itamar Ben-Gvir.

"There is one response to the incitement by Ben-Gvir and Netanyahu against the Arab citizens that has been going on all day: go out and vote for the Joint List!" he wrote.

The president of the German-Israeli Society, Green party politician Volker Beck, said in a TV interview on election day that he believed a change in leadership might be in Israel's interests.

"It would be good for Israel, if it were to come to a change, because I believe that [Prime Minister Benjamin] Netanyahu has squandered a great deal of trust in recent years, both in foreign and domestic policy," Beck said in an interview broadcast on sister channels RTL and ntv.

Beck, a Bundestag lawmaker for more than two decades with the Greens who was perhaps best known as a politician as a campaigner for LGBT rights and advocate of an uncompromising Israeli foreign policy, is not typically associated with stern criticism of Netanyahu or his government.

"Although he has also done some things right and has done some necessary things, a change is really needed" to allow Israel to recoup support among the international community, Beck said, as Netanyahu becomes an increasingly polarizing figure on the world stage.

An opinion poll by Agam Labs at Jerusalem's Hebrew University predicts that supporters of Otzma Yehudit are younger than the average Israeli voter, with as many as a third voting for the first time.

#45. Myanmar: Is the US undermining the EU's diplomatic blockade?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With the US reopening talks with Myanmar's military-backed government, and several Southeast Asian countries restarting diplomatic contacts, the EU is facing uncomfortable questions about its own boycott of the junta.

The US government has begun a quiet reproachment with Myanmar's military-backed government, holding two rounds of talks since September with State Department representative Michael Vance. American diplomats reportedly meet Myanmar officials in Naypyitaw and then in Tokyo earlier this month.

Washington wants to tackle online scam centers, secure the release of imprisoned American businessman Adam Castillo, and potentially gain access to Myanmar's rare-earth resources. The market for the valuable minerals is currently controlled by the US rival China.

Meanwhile, Southeast Asian governments are also increasingly questioning their policy of isolating Myanmar's military leadership. Multiple countries have already renewed direct diplomatic contacts.

The EU, however, continues to reject the legitimacy of the military junta in the war-torn country. The European bloc has also remained unmoved by the 2025/2026 election, which ended with military chief Min Aung Hlaing becoming the president. The military retains control over the political system.

The EU responded to Myanmar's February 2021 military coup by suspending direct financial assistance to the government, imposing targeted sanctions and sharply reducing diplomatic engagement with the military authorities.

Its sanctions currently cover 105 individuals and 22 entities connected to the military. In April, the EU extended these measures for another 12 months.

At the same time, Brussels is providing humanitarian assistance to displaced communities and Rohingya refugees and is supporting civil society organizations.

The EU also stopped short of withdrawing Myanmar's preferential trade access because of concerns about the consequences for workers, particularly the mostly young women employed in the country's garment industry. Last year, bilateral goods trade was worth about €4 billion ($4.46 billion), up from €3.4 billion in 2019.

The civil war triggered by the 2021 coup has killed an estimated 100,000 people. Rebel groups still control or contest large swathes of Myanmar, and over 4 million people have been internally displaced, according to UN data.

The EU "continues to uphold its policy of non-recognition of the current military regime," in view of "the ongoing violence and the attack against its own population," an EU spokesman told DW.

At the same time, the bloc "is closely monitoring developments in the discussions and positions of the United States, Japan, ASEAN countries and other major actors with representatives of Myanmar's military regime."

"It also supports ASEAN efforts, in particular the Five-Point Consensus, which has yet to be fully implemented," the spokesperson added, referring to the peace deal proposed by the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) in 2021.

In the aftermath of the coup, ASEAN took the unprecedented decision to exclude Myanmar's military leaders from its highest-level meetings after the junta failed to implement the regional peace plan.

However, ASEAN's position is showing signs of change. Some member states, most notably Myanmar's neighbor Thailand, signal growing frustration with the lack of progress towards ending Myanmar's civil war.

Last week, Thai Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow welcomed Washington's renewed engagement and suggested that ASEAN members would hold substantive discussions about Myanmar's future at their summit in Manila next month.

Myanmar's authorities are also seeking to break their diplomatic isolation. In September, Myanmar's leader Hlaing gave an interview to Time magazine, his first with a major Western publication since the coup, as part of an apparent effort to rebuild relations with Western governments.

On October 22, Burma Campaign UK, a London-based human rights organization, accused the British government of making a "significant shift in approach" after the British Foreign Office hosted Win Zeyar Tun, the charge d'affaires of Myanmar's military-controlled embassy in London, at an ASEAN diplomatic gathering days earlier.

According to an article published by Intelligence Online this month, the regime's charge d'affaires in Brussels, Khin Thidar Ay, has also sought contacts with European politicians, including Czech MEP Ondrej Dostal, a member of the European Parliament's delegation for Southeast Asia.

Kristina Kironska, of Palacky University Olomouc in the Czech Republic and an expert on the EU's policy on Myanmar, told DW that the EU has little reason to follow Washington's apparent shift towards engagement with the Myanmar junta.

The US strategy "appears to be pursuing specific interests" such as rare earth mining and cybercrime, she said.

However, the most important rare earth mining operations are no longer controlled by the military, but by rebels such as the Kachin Independence Organization in Kachin State. "It remains to be seen what the US can achieve through negotiations with the junta," she said.

The EU has long positioned itself as a defender of democracy and human rights in Myanmar, "so abandoning this now because the US is exploring a new, perhaps more transactional approach would undermine the EU's credibility," Kironska added.

"At the same time, isolation has achieved relatively little. The question is therefore not whether the EU should engage, but rather with whom and under what conditions."

The EU is forced to consider risks to its own influence. If Washington, ASEAN governments and other regional powers increase their engagement with Myanmar's authorities, the EU risks becoming increasingly marginal in discussions about the country's political future.

Yet greater engagement with the military could undermine Brussels' relations with Myanmar's democratic opposition and ethnic resistance organizations, many of which control substantial territory.

"The key question for Brussels is whether it can remain a credible defender of democratic change while ensuring that it still has a meaningful voice in the negotiations that will determine Myanmar's future," Sabe Soe, director of the NGO Burma Centre Prague, told DW.

One possibility is for Brussels to maintain its sanctions against military leaders and their businesses while expanding diplomacy through ASEAN, the UN and other regional partners. The EU could also tie any easing of restrictions to humanitarian access, improvements in political freedoms and progress towards peace.

Soe also suggested that the EU could pursue economic engagement in areas administered by revolutionary and ethnic resistance forces, offering an alternative to engagement centred on the military authorities in Naypyidaw.

This approach would allow the EU to remain involved in Myanmar's future without necessarily recognizing its military-backed government, she said.

For now, Brussels seems to have little appetite for a fundamental policy rethink. But this could change as more governments reopen their doors to Myanmar's generals.

#34. Mbappe apologizes as Chinese sponsors drop him and TV broadcasts are halted over Mao dictator remark

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

French soccer star Kylian Mbappe has apologized after apparently referring to Mao Zedong as a dictator. Chinese sponsors have dropped him, while state television has suspended Spanish league broadcasts.

France striker Kylian Mbappe apologized after apparently referring to China's founding leader Mao Zedong as a dictator in a video that circulated online.

The controversy prompted three major Chinese companies to sever sponsorship ties with the Real Madrid forward, while Chinese state television suspended broadcasts of Spain's La Liga.

The controversy began when a video circulated showing Mbappe discussing his favorite internet meme featuring himself.

In the clip, apparently filmed ahead of an appearance on the French online talk show The Bridge, Mbappe referred to an image depicting his face superimposed on the body of "some guy ... I think he's a Chinese dictator."

The image appeared to depict Mao Zedong, who founded the People's Republic of China in 1949 and ruled until his death in 1976. Jokes about country's former leader and descriptions of him as a dictator remain taboo.

Mbappe has previously been depicted as authoritarian leaders in online memes mocking his perceived influence over teammates and suggestions that he has the power to influence club decisions.

Mbappe has been nicknamed "the dictator" in the European press.

The European Commission on Tuesdayunveiled a new proposal to beef up the powers of the EU border agency in order to empower it to support the return of irregular migrants.

The proposed legislation gives the agency, called Frontex, more resources to act beyond EU territory.

The plan would give the agency the power to support non-EU countries to return migrants to their countries of origin.

The proposal comes as EU member states increasingly focus on returns, saying too many people who are asked to leave the bloc remain.

The new role for Frontex comes after the humanitarian crisis in Ceuta this summer.

The tragedy in the Spanish exclave was seized upon by some EU member states as an example of where the EU could do more to block people from reaching the EU.

Italy, for example, placed some temporary border controls on travel from Spain.

Spain's prime minister at the time criticized several EU member states for their "selfish" response to the influx of migrants to Ceuta.

"Today we propose that Frontex becomes the central EU actor on returns," Magnus Brunner, commissioner for internal affairs ​and migration, told reporters.

"It will be able to act faster, with a quick reaction force that can ⁠deploy within ⁠24 hours and spot risks ⁠early, before an ​event becomes a crisis," Brunner added.

The ability to deport migrants from non-EU countries means that Frontex could, for instance, deport a Bangladeshi national from Libya, where many migrants start the final leg of their journey into the bloc.

The plan envisages gradually expanding Frontex's standing corps to 30,000 officers by creating a new "European border and return reserve" of 17,000 officers within the next 15 years.
