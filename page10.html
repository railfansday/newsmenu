When the most recent meeting of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), held in Addis Ababa, ended earlier this month, its conclusion drew negative media headlines. The United Nations body — the world’s forum for climate scientists — had failed, not for the first time, to agree a timetable for its next comprehensive review of the science of global climate change.

Many countries would like the review, known as the Seventh Assessment Report (AR7), to be published in 2028. This would be in time for a UN climate meeting called the Global Stocktake, at which countries assess their progress towards meeting climate pledges. A number of nations, including some European countries and small island states, want the report to be published ahead of the stocktake. This would allow decisions such as adjustments to pledges to be informed by the latest science.

A second, smaller group, including India, Iran, Kenya, Russia and Saudi Arabia, is arguing that 2028 is too soon. They say that some of the poorest countries lack the capacity to participate in the process of reviewing the report before it is published. They also object to the fact that the studies under review are dominated by science from institutions in high-income countries. However, for AR7, the IPCC says 51% of authors and review editors are from developing countries and nations with economies in transition.

Reviewing IPCC assessment reports is a big undertaking. When AR6 was published in 2023, it ran to more than 7,500 pages and received some 200,000 comments from reviewers around the world.

But the IPCC faces an even more serious problem: it is running out of money. For the first time in its history, it might not have the funds to finish an assessment report. The body relies on voluntary donations from governments and other UN agencies, and these have been falling since 2023 (see ‘Lack of trust’). The funds are also used to support scientists from the poorest countries, allowing them to attend meetings. Many of the countries that want to delay the next report have contributed nothing to the IPCC in years, if ever, although Russia is a notable exception. If contributions continue at current levels, by the end of 2028 the IPCC will be left with just 5 million Swiss francs (US$6 million) — the amount needed for an orderly wind-down of the body.

But there is a way forwards. The IPCC needs more than 20 million Swiss francs to stay operational until the end of 2029, and to complete AR7. Nations must consider increasing their contributions immediately. Other donors, including philanthropists and non-governmental organizations, should also approach the IPCC to see how they can help.

There are those who question whether the world still needs the IPCC. They say that there are faster ways to synthesize climate research, and that much is already known about climate change, its impacts and the measures needed to prevent dangerous consequences. Last week, a separate UN report confirmed that countries are not on track to limit global warming to within 1.5 °C of pre-industrial levels.

But these arguments overlook the true value of the IPCC, which includes its processes. IPCC reports are produced in a highly consultative way — scientists from the richest to the poorest countries collaborate, ensuring that voices from around the world are heard. This global, scientist-led process also ensures that the quality of the evidence under review takes precedence over political considerations. Once the scientists agree on the main text, governments are involved in writing the summary report, and can choose whether and how to act on the findings. But they cannot change the main text of the assessment report.

The IPCC has survived crises before. One of the most serious was in 1995, when AR2 concluded that “the balance of the evidence” suggested humans are responsible for global warming. This was challenged by representatives of some fossil-fuel-producing countries and oil and gas companies. But the organization’s scientists stood firm, often at great personal cost. Most IPCC-sponsoring governments backed the scientists, and the crisis eventually passed.

The current crisis is more serious because it concerns the IPCC’s very survival. Ideally, governments would come together again and ensure that the organization is properly funded. One way to resolve the matter would be to replace the current process of ad hoc voluntary contributions with multi-year budgetary commitments, similarly to those used to fund many other UN agencies. Support from non-governmental sources should also be considered.

The timing of the next assessment report must be resolved. But the dispute must not be allowed to distract from the most important matter of all: ensuring the continued survival of the IPCC itself.

#598. What Earth's sixth mass extinction event means for humans

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Human activity is driving plants and animals to extinction at an unprecedented rate. As countries meet in Armenia to discuss ways to reverse nature loss, DW reveals how declining biodiversity is already harming people.

From the tiniest microbes in the human gut to giant whales in the ocean, biodiversity is the sum of all life on the planet. It's another word for nature. And because nature is like a team, every player has a job.

When various species from trees to animals work together, they regulate weather, maintain soil health, purify water and support food production. These benefits are known as ecosystem services and they underpin human well-being.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF. "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as biodiversity declines, Shaw and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worse with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator that the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. But progress toward those targets remains insufficient, with funding and implementation among the biggest challenges.

Take wetlands as an example. Some 85% of these vital ecosystems disappeared between 1700 and 2000. One study from Canada's University of Waterloo found that habitats like marshes and swamps in southern Ontario provide C$4.2 billion (€2.63 billion) worth of water filtration services each year, removing nutrients like phosphorus and keeping drinking water sources clean.

The study found that as they disappear, water quality worsens, which massively increases the cost of treating it.

Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

At the same time, soil and ecosystem degradation coupled with storms and heat waves linked to climate change are already damaging harvests. A recent UK government report stated that every critical ecosystem is on a "pathway to collapse," warning that nature's decline posed a grave threat to global food supply and geopolitical stability.

"The UK, like many other countries, relies on imports for much of the food and so is reliant on ecosystems around the world," said Louise McRae, a research fellow at the Zoological Society of London, who also contributed to WWF's Living Planet report. "This is why we need to care about global biodiversity."

Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organization.

As biodiversity disappears, humans risk losing access to undiscovered treatments.

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute.

But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

"No one would ever think they did anything but sit in a shell and be slimy," said Shaw. "But you lose oysters, you can lose water filtration and clean water, you can lose the habitat for many other species that we interact with or sometimes consume."

And, said Shaw, the humble oyster protects coastal communities from storm surges. If you lose it "you lose the coastal protection."

The loss of a single species can also lead to more disease. One study suggests a decline in red foxes in North America is linked to the spread of Lyme disease, a tick-borne illness that also affects humans.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions, when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Both McRae and Shaw called the declines extremely worrying, but said they shouldn't provoke despair.

Though she acknowledges that the underlying global trend is moving in the wrong direction and cannot be ignored, McRae said "the statistics are there to just motivate us to action rather than to cause alarm."

And the Living Planet report also highlights conservation successes, showing that nature can recover.

The number of green sea turtles, for example, had fallen significantly, but has now bounced back. This is thanks to a massive worldwide effort to protect nesting beaches and changes to fishing practices that reduce accidental capture.

Sea otters are another example. Prized for their fur, in the 1800s they were hunted to near extinction on the US Pacific Coast. When they disappeared, it meant sea urchins had no predators, leaving them free to decimate kelp forests.

That matters because as well as providing habitat for other species, these giant underwater groves protect coastlines from storm surges and absorb carbon, making them important for climate protection.

Following a ban on commercial hunting, sea otters have also rebounded, helping kelp forests recover.

"You can see that ripple effect that really had an obvious and a measurable impact. But critically, it can be reversed," said McRae, adding that biodiversity loss and climate change are intertwined and exacerbate each other but have joint solutions.

"We're learning how to turn things around at very small scales," said Shaw. "Now the real challenge is to turn things around at very big scale so we can see it in the global indicators."

#586. The homeless man living in front of a London mega-mansion

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anders Fernstedt lives in London without any money. The Swedish native has built a most unusual home under the porch of a house worth over €200 million. How did that happen?

There's a lot of stopping and staring in Knightsbridge. This part of central London is within a stone's throw of Buckingham Palace, Hyde Park and Harrods, bringing inquisitive tourists in droves.

While it may not divert quite as many eyeballs as the seat of the British monarchy, a curious collection of plants, garden umbrellas, shopping bags and children's toys filling a building's entrance causes many a curious glance.

The peculiar arrangement on a grand portico is what Anders Fernstedt, a man from Sweden who fell on hard times, has been calling home for more than three years already.

That building hosting his makeshift setup was, just a few years ago, London's most expensive house.

The mega-mansion at 2-8A Rutland Gate sold in 2020 for a then-record £210 million (€244 million, $278 million) to Hui Ka Yan, the founder of China's Evergrande group, who was sentenced to life in prison last month.

Hui never moved in and the house was registered under his wife's name. The freezing of her assets means it can't be seized by liquidators and the house is in a state of limbo.

So too was Fernstedt when he first stumbled across it. "I just thought, it would be a shelter for the night. I'm an avid camper, like an amateur botanist. I'm not afraid of nature," he told DW.

Fernstedt has never been inside the property, which appears to be in a state of disrepair. Instead, he gradually built his home in the portico, where grand columns guard the 45 rooms, indoor swimming pool and 116 windows. Year round he sleeps under the portico roof and a large umbrella which provides a hidden nook in which he's placed mattresses and bedding.

"I haven't had a bad night's sleep for as long as I can remember. And that goes for four seasons. It's not cold in the winter, and because I don't have walls, I get the breeze in the heat," he said.

"My conceit when I started was that sort of fake dream. Oh, I'm just a boy, my parents are in the house, everything's normal, I just asked them for permission to camp on the balcony."

Fernstedt was raised by his mother in Sweden before embarking on a range of careers at home and in the US, building a name for himself in the tech world.

In 2016, he moved to England where he worked as a journalist and fact checker at publications including The Economist, before work dried up around the time of the COVID pandemic.

A share in a tech company from his days in the US gave Anders a nest egg he relied on. But it couldn't last forever and he found himself living in a tent and then temporary accommodation, where he experienced violence and brushed up against people with severe addictions.

"Perhaps in this contemporary world, the biggest curiosity about my lifestyle is I'm completely off money," he said.

Fernstedt has built relationships with bakeries, cafes and florists in which he gets unused food, flowers for his home and use of power. He also makes use of a homeless charity in nearby Victoria for showers, clothing donations and socializing. But he's also made himself a broadly accepted part of one of London's most affluent neighborhoods.

"This is a very sophisticated neighborhood. I'd stayed for a few weeks and I knew everybody knew that I was here. And I thought, how can I sort of convey my presence without being present? And I thought, my constituency has to be the neighbors' children and grandchildren. Because if they plead my case at the kitchen table, my prospects improve," he said, by way of explaining the toys that line his abode.

"It is, in a way, a squirrel's nut collection," he added, when asked if he's worried about leaving his possessions out in the open. "I have so many nuts that I won't notice when something disappears."

There are some neighbors who do object to his presence, but Anders does his best to keep his home neat and his life quiet. Indeed, many others feel his presence prevents the house being a target for thieves or vandals. Overall though, he's found the community welcoming.

"Sometimes they introduce themselves asking what might I want or need. And my stock answer being: 'well, what you just gave me, a little bit of normal time, right? And neighborly banter'," he said. "Sometimes I'll be given food and very elegant Italian dinner jackets and clothes have arrived on a few occasions."

Not all homeless people in London have found such a stable lifestyle. Homeless charity Crisis estimates that there are over 200,000 people classified as homeless in England's capital, where "4,392 people were recorded sleeping rough in London between April and June, up from 4% on the same time last year. This is up 58% compared to 10 years ago."

According to government figures, there were 105,138 empty homes in London in 2025, or 1 in every 37 homes. Andy Burnham, who became British Prime Minister in July, has since vowed to offer "every rough sleeper in England a route off the streets by Christmas."

With such an enormous price tag, even after six years of being abandoned, Fernstedt knows that the house he lives at could potentially become untenable at virtually any moment.

But over the last few years, it's given him something he craved when pitching up his tent or being moved from one scary temporary residence to the next.

"I'm no longer really worried that I'll cycle around the corner and everything will be gone. Because I've been here so long, I'm free of worrying now, and if we worry too much, we won't be happy."

#565. Could measuring immune health reshape medical care?

Nicolai J. Birkbak is a professor of cancer evolution and bioinformatics in the Department of Clinical Medicine, Aarhus University, Aarhus, Denmark, and in the Department of Molecular Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital.

John S. Tsang is the founding director of the Yale Center for Systems and Engineering Immunology, Yale School of Medicine, New Haven, Connecticut, USA, and a professor in the departments of Immunobiology and Biomedical Engineering, Yale University.

The immune system is central to human health. When it fails, people can become more susceptible to infection and cancer. When misdirected, it can attack the body’s own tissues. And as it ages, it can contribute to chronic disease.

People of the same age can have strikingly distinct immune systems, shaped by genetics and by environmental and lifestyle factors, including air pollution, smoking, diet and past infections1,2. Growing evidence suggests that variation in the immune system’s ‘biological age’ — the rate at which it has aged, which is distinct from chronological age — matters for health. This variation has been linked to neurodegeneration, cancer and mortality risk3.

Immune cells have a surprising role in exercise endurance

Yet, clinicians have no routine way of measuring how healthy someone’s immune system is. That’s because the immune system is a dynamic network that can change over time. It involves specialized organs that support immune-cell development and function, as well as immune cells that can circulate throughout the body and reside in tissues4. Signalling molecules, such as cytokines and chemokines, allow these immune cells and tissues to communicate.

Clinical tests provide only a fragmented picture of the immune system. A physician might examine an individual’s white blood cell count to establish whether circulating immune cells are at unusually high or low levels. They might also measure blood biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, which can indicate inflammation. But neither measurement is specific to a particular health condition, and they do not indicate whether the immune system as a whole is resilient or in decline, or if it is capable of mounting an effective response to infections and other challenges.

But advances in immunology, molecular profiling, medical imaging and artificial intelligence are opening up fresh possibilities for measuring the state of people’s immune systems. The stage is set for researchers to develop the immunome: a comprehensive characterization of immune health across cells, tissues, organs and molecular interactions (see ‘Defining the immunome’).

A raft of assays can provide information about the state of a person’s immune system.

Large-scale analysis of circulating proteins and antibodies

Patterns of immune signalling and antibody recognition beyond individual clinical markers

Types, abundance and molecular states of immune cells

Diversity and composition of immune-cell populations

Detection of quantifiable features from medical images of immune-related organs

Structural and compositional features of the thymus, spleen and bone marrow

Locations of immune cells and how they are organized relative to other cells

Measurements of immune-system activity before and after immune challenges

Strength, timing and persistence of responses to challenges such as vaccination

Long-term modelling of immune health using artificial intelligence

Several types of data can be combined to predict how ageing and disease change a person’s immune health

Research initiatives such as the Human Immunome Project, which one of us (J.S.T.) co-leads, are beginning to provide the data needed. What remains unclear is whether this information could be used to improve patient care, and how that could be achieved. Which measurements best capture clinically important aspects of a person’s immune health? How stable are these variables over time? And, most importantly, can assessing them improve clinical decision-making and, ultimately, disease outcomes?

Measuring every aspect of the immune system will not be feasible for every person. Instead, researchers should try to identify key features that can be readily assessed in the clinic to flag aspects of the immune system that are weakened, or declining quickly when they shouldn’t be. Such assessments could open avenues for disease prevention, treatment guidance and methods to monitor interventions.

For instance, these features might enable physicians to recognize cases of accelerated immune ageing and spot individuals who need close disease monitoring. For cancer treatment, measures of immune health could help to identify people who are most likely to benefit from therapies that rely on the immune system5,6, as well as those with a high risk of adverse reactions.

Such measurements could also help researchers to understand why people of similar ages and apparently similar health sometimes have substantially different responses to the same infection — a phenomenon that was apparent during the COVID-19 pandemic. Antibody responses to the same vaccine likewise vary widely between individuals2,7. If this variability could be measured, clinicians could use it to tailor preventive measures, treatments and monitoring to an individual’s immune system.

Researchers, health-care systems and funders must work to find the smallest, most scalable set of measurements that can capture meaningful differences in immune function and guide clinical decisions. Here, we outline a road map for moving the immunome from the laboratory to the clinic.

First, scientists should generate longitudinal data sets using large research populations and biobanks. Gaining a full understanding of immune health will require measuring several components of the immune system alongside broader indicators of health and disease in the same people over time.

Analysis of genes and gene expression in single cells from large research populations and biobanks could help researchers to understand which immune-cell groups are present in distinct populations. This could reveal which genes and signalling pathways are active in particular populations of cells, and what combination of markers best distinguishes between healthy, declining and dysfunctional immune states2,3,8. Analysing blood proteins can indicate whether a person is experiencing inflammation and whether their immune system has been activated. And studying the receptors on immune cells called lymphocytes can reveal the diversity of cells available to recognize threats. These receptors determine which pathogenic antigens the cells will identify; greater diversity generally allows the immune system to detect a broader range of threats.

Researchers must also study how the immune system changes over time to respond to challenges. Studying people before and after they are vaccinated is one standardized way to do this. It’s already clear that a person’s immune state before vaccination can predict the strength and characteristics of the immune response that follows2,9. The task now is to find out whether a person’s response to routine vaccination could provide a practical read-out of their immune competence10, and whether it would be possible to modify the immune health of an individual before interventions such as vaccination or cancer immunotherapy to improve their response2,5.

Data sets used to probe the immunome must represent the diversity of human immunity, because genetics and environmental factors alter immune states1. For example, malaria vaccine efficacy can differ markedly between populations across continents7,11. Yet, many existing immune-profiling data sets are concentrated in populations from high-income nations. When studying diverse populations, researchers around the world should use the same protocols so that results from various regions can be compared.

A big challenge will be making sense of complex data sets. AI tools can identify patterns across thousands of immune measurements, but those patterns might reflect differences between populations, laboratories or technologies rather than underlying immune health. Models will therefore need to be tested across independent populations and over time to establish which signals are robust and clinically meaningful.

Scientists working on the Human Immunome Project are developing and applying AI approaches to immunome data collected from various populations. An important goal for the project is to create reference maps and foundation models that scientists can use for tasks such as identifying small, manageable sets of measurements that would be useful in clinical settings.

With large-scale data in hand, researchers should work towards simplification. The success of the field should be judged not by how many immune variables can be measured, but by whether it is possible to improve clinical decision-making with just a small, manageable set of them. Tests used in the clinic would ideally be cheap and should not put extra demands on patients. Before any AI tools can be used routinely, they will need to be tested across a range of hospitals and populations. Researchers must establish whether a tool’s outputs truly improve treatment decisions.

One example of a tool that might be useful in the clinic is an AI model capable of extracting information about immune health from routine blood tests and clinical information such as age, sex, body weight and medical history. In a study published last year6, such an approach was able to predict health outcomes after immunotherapy treatment for people with cancer.

#550. Bulgaria seeks suspects in Germany after Starlink sabotage

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Bulgarian court has issued arrest warrants for two young German nationals suspected of setting fire to Starlink antennas. The case is reminiscent of another in Poland, which Warsaw says was orchestrated by Russia.

Bulgaria on Monday issued European arrest warrants against two 19-year-old German nationals, saying it suspected their involvement in the sabotage of a series of Starlink antennas near the capital Sofia.

"The evidence gathered makes it possible to conclude that there are reasonable grounds to suspect the involvement of the two defendants," the court in Sofia rules, granting a request from prosecutors.

Ukraine's military uses Starlink's military-grade satellite internet service Starshield for battlefield communications amid Russia's invasion.

Bulgaria does not directly border Ukraine, but it is on the Black Sea, one of the flashpoints of the conflict. A similar case in Poland, which does directly border Ukraine, led to a total of four arrests last week, also in Germany.

#538. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

#492. Rising emissions and temperatures: The cost of Trump's climate retreat is now clear

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The world is set to breach the critical 1.5-degree temperature limit, with Donald Trump's US withdrawal from climate action worsening the outlook, according to a UN report. But there is a small silver lining.

For over a decade, scientists have warned the world must limit global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 Fahrenheit) to avoid the most severe consequences of climate change.

Decades of "insufficient" action on cutting the fossil fuel emissions heating the planet means overshooting 1.5-degree temperature threshold is now "imminent and unavoidable," according to the United Nations Environment Program's (UNEP) annual Emissions Gap Report.

If current climate policies continue, temperatures will rise by an average of 2.8 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels within this century. But even if countries meet their new climate pledges, global warming is still projected to reach 2.6 degrees Celsius. Both scenarios would tip the world into a period of catastrophic weather extremes.

The projections reflect a gap between what governments promise to do and the action they are taking to curb emissions. Some 167 countries have submitted climate action plans to the UN, covering 83% of global greenhouse gas emissions. But those pledges to cut emissions fall far short of what is needed to limit warming.

Among the world's largest emitters, Indonesia, China and the United States saw emissions rise in 2025, according to preliminary estimates. The increase in the US "far exceeded that of all other countries," driven by a resurgence of coal — the dirtiest fossil fuel source — and rising electricity demand, partly linked to massive AI data center expansion.

The outlook has worsened in part because of US President Donald Trump's withdrawal of the United States from the historic Paris climate accord in which the world came together in 2015 to agree to halt climate change. The US is the second-largest annual emitter of climate-warping greenhouse gases.

Since Trump entered the Oval Office for a second time, his administration has taken a sledgehammer to climate policy, gutting environmental regulations and expanding oil and gas production.

"The main takeaway is simple — governments need to get on with enacting policies that cut fossil fuel use as soon as possible," said Bill Hare, CEO of the global science and policy institute Climate Analytics, of the report.

Exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius even temporarily sets humanity on a dangerous course, as it increases the risk of triggering potentially irreversible or difficult-to-undo "tipping points," like the loss of coral reefs, long-term retreat of ice sheets, and the Amazon Rainforest turning into a grassland.

As ecosystems disappear and sea levels rise, these changes could trigger a deadly chain reaction, releasing more greenhouse gases and accelerating warming, dramatically increasing the likelihood of deadly extreme weather.

This summer alone, Europe recorded more than 32,000 excess deaths related to heat and nearly 1,500 people died in a flash floods in Nepal and Tibet, after climate change laid the groundwork for a glacier to collapse.

But scientists maintain 1.5 degrees Celsius is not a magic number, and that every fraction of a degree of warming brings escalating risks. To reduce the risk of future damage, countries should still focus on rapidly transitioning away from fossil fuels, the largest contributor to global climate change, and toward renewable energy like solar and wind.

Now the best-case scenario is overshooting 1.5 degrees Celsius for a short time before bringing temperatures back down quickly.

"Overshoot is not surrender," UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said in a press statement. "We can still keep it as small and as short as possible — and bring temperatures back below 1.5 degrees before the end of the century."

UNEP lays out a scenario in which the world sees warming peak at 1.7 to 1.8 degrees Celsius around mid-century, before falling back to 1.5 Celsius by 2100. Getting there would require steep emissions cuts of 69% by 2040, relative to 2025 levels.

It would also require removing 330 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and storing it in the ground. But recent studies have cast doubt on the feasibility and costs of the technology, as well as the nascent carbon removal industry's ability to scale as quickly as required to meet climate goals.

The UN warns any delays will make the challenge harder, saying that every five years that emissions remain at current levels adds about 0.1 Celsius to projected peak warming.

"We must pull out all the stops to minimize peak warming and return to 1.5 Celsius by the end of the century," Inger Andersen, Executive Director of UNEP, said in a press release. "This is possible."

Still, climate policies have proven effective in reducing emissions. The UNEP report draws on more than 200 studies that analyze how measures like carbon taxes, government climate targets, building codes and subsidies for low-carbon technologies are working.

Carbon-pricing schemes have been the most widely studied so far. China's pilot schemes reduced emissions by about 11%, while the European Union's emissions trading system saw an 8% cut, compared with projected levels without these policies.

"There are more opportunities than ever to accelerate action," according to the report, pointing to the boom in renewable energy since the adoption of the Paris Agreement a decade ago.

Global renewable electricity capacity has grown three times faster than anticipated, and solar build-out grew seven times faster. In 2024, more than 90% of renewable projects were cheaper than fossil fuel alternatives.

While total energy supply from coal, oil and gas has continued to rise, that's happened at a slower rate than was projected in 2015. And coal use is likely to peak and decline by the end of the decade.

"We have a wealth of success stories showing that emissions can be cut quickly," Andersen said. "It is now up to nations to urgently put in place policies that spark fast, deep and sustained emissions reductions, and put the world on track for a more stable climate and more stable economies and societies."

Fabian Hürzeler is younger than his star player, a fellow Premier League German. But, contrary to his reputation in his homeland, Hürzeler has become a popular figure both on and off the pitch in England.

"We are absolutely in love with him. He's been an incredible manager. The results are extraordinary and we're flying."

Those words from Ben Parker will be familiar to fans of any club who have been on the kind of winning run Brighton and Hove Albion are currently on.

But all the signs on and off the pitch suggest that the man in question — Brighton's German coach Fabian Hürzeler — is more than just a flash in the Premier League pan.

Hürzeler has never coached in Germany's Bundesliga. After winning promotion to Germany's top flight with St. Pauli in 2024, he left to become the youngest man ever to take charge in England's Premier League at 31. His team currently sit third, having won six games in a row including a 5-0 thrashing of bitter rivals Crystal Palace last weekend.

It was the repeat of a fixture last season that marked Hürzeler's lowest Brighton ebb, with a 1-0 home loss making it one win in 12. "There are only two options," the German coach said at the time. "Give up or keep pushing harder, and that's always the option I choose in my life."

Parker, the co-editor of Brighton and Hove Albion fanzine, Dogma, admitted to DW that there were plenty who weren't sure if persisting was the answer. "There was some disquiet at the end of that game, there were people shouting: 'You're getting sacked in the morning.'"

While such sentiment may be difficult to swallow for many coaches, Parker said that Hürzeler told him in an interview soon after joining the club that he welcomed it when needed.

"He talked about wanting a fan relationship that had a two-way aspect. The club and him are really ambitious, so if the performances aren't up to scratch, we should be telling him that. And that's what happened on that day. I see the whole thing as being quite healthy."

Hürzeler never played at the top level. He represented Germany's youth teams and turned out for Bayern Munich's reserves but soon grew frustrated with second-team football. He then accepted a player-coach role at German fifth-tier side Pipinsried at the age of 23. From 2018, he was also an assistant coach for Germany's U20s.

He performed this alongside a job at an art dealership in the German state of Bavaria, where he moved with his family at the age of two having been born in the United States. After starting as an assistant coach at St. Pauli in 2020, he took over after head coach Timo Schultz was fired in late 2022. Since then, he hasn't looked back.

That unusual path to top-flight management and some innovative tactics at St. Pauli led to a perception in Germany that Hürzeler was a little aloof and something of a 'laptop manager' in the vein of former Bayern Munich and Germany boss, Julian Nagelsmann who had a similarly nondescript playing career. This, so the charge goes, meant Hürzeler wasn't able to forge the relationships and togetherness needed to make a team more than the sum of its parts.

That was rarely what was reported by his players and staff at St. Pauli, nor is it Hürzeler's belief. "I want to be myself, I don't want to play a role, and my age definitely helps regarding understanding the players," he said.

"I always say I speak their language and I understand their thoughts, because it wasn't that long ago when I played."

His antics on the sideline also earned him a reputation as a bit of a hothead. Hürzeler was booked seven times during St. Pauli's promotion push and since joining Brighton, the German has been booked nine times and been sent off once. When asked about this, he said: "Emotions are part of the game."

Parker said this authenticity has also been reflected in his relationship with Brighton and Hove, on the south coast of England.

"For most Brighton fans he comes across as quite a quirky, interesting, left-field figure. The mantra he set out when he first arrived was quite counter-culturalist, to challenge the establishment, and that fits well here," Parker said.

"You will see him walking along on the seafront, going up for coffees around town, and he travels back and forth on an ebike, not in a chauffer-driven car as a lot of managers might. He's also hosted fan events in pubs, which I believe he paid for. So I'd say he's very approachable."

"I was here for seven years, got married here and both my kids were born in England. The most important milestones in my life happened here. I missed my friends and the feeling of being deeply connected to the club."

"I think he's said he's a supporter of Bayern Munich, and he uses them as an example of the standards he wants," said Parker of Hürzeler. "I can see that he could be a good fit in a couple of years time. He won't go to a Liverpool or a Chelsea, I think there's a strong pull to Bayern in particular."

Bayern's current boss, Vincent Kompany, is in little danger at the moment, though Bayern coaches are only ever a few losses from crisis. For the meantime, Hürzeler is also happy where he is, having extended his contract to 2029. Again, the feeling is mutual.

"If he can be the person that wins us that first trophy, then he will go down as the greatest."
