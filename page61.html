#72. ‘Will Lula’s win end Latin America’s far right turn?’: Our correspondent in Brazil answered your questions

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Having lost the first round of Brazil’s presidential election to far right rival Flávio Bolsonaro, incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva spectacularly triumphed in the second. The Guardian’s Latin America corrrespondent Tom Phillips answered your questions on what’s next for Brazil.

Thanks so much everyone. That’s all from me. Or, as the potty-mouthed Jair Bolsonaro would say: “Acabou, porra!” (“That’s the bleeping end of it!”).

I really appreciate everyone following and supporting our coverage of Brazil. Certainly, a rocky road ahead for Lula. But for now, tens of millions of Brazilians, and the world, breathe a sign of relief.

Dougie McGilvray asks: To what extent was Lula’s victory a case of choosing the “lesser of two evils”, given his previous corruption convictions (annulled on procedural grounds) and net-negative approval ratings? Is this part of a broader Latin American trend of increasing polarisation and persistent corruption concerns? Argentina seems a particularly stark example, with no credible candidates emerging from either the left or right, let alone the political centre. What should the left learn from the region’s shift to the right, and to what extent has it contributed to this polarisation?

double quotation markTom: I think that while Lula has huge support in some parts of the country, for a lot of voters, that was indeed the case. Many people seem to have been casting an “anti-Bolsonaro” vote, because of their revulsion at the idea of such a radical political project returning to power. They remember the Covid chaos under Jair Bolsonaro, when 700,000 Brazilians died and he mocked patients and undermined vaccination efforts. They remember the violence of January 2023 when Bolsonaro’s supporters trashed congress, the supreme court and presidential palace. They are aware of the allegations of Flávio Bolsonaro’s alleged ties to organised crime and the police investigations into suspicions of money laundering. And on Sunday they said: no.

I think that in Lula’s next government he will need to build a broad church, including moderate, democratic sectors of the right in his administration. And he will also need to show real results on one of the most pressing concerns Brazilians have - crime and violence - to stop the right weaponizing this issue against the left, as they have done successfully in places such as Brazil, Colombia and El Salvador.

PersonN0nGrata asks: US interference in this election has been rife from the evidence, from the administration and the US based corporates. This follows a pattern throughout Latin America where US interference has been continuous and escalated, up to and including actual military attacks. Why is this not given the prominence it should be in reporting?

double quotation markTom: Thanks for the question. We’ve been reporting extensively on Trump era US interference in Brazilian politics since last year when the campaign ramped up in an attempt to derail Jair Bolsonaro’s coup trial.

We reported on how one of Lula’s ministers saw their US visa revoked, how a supreme court judge was sanctioned over Bolsonaro’s trial, which Trump claimed was a witch-hunt, and in the lead-up to this campaign we published a big story about how the US campaign had echoes of Washington’s cold war-era attempt to manipulate Brazilian politics in the lead-up to the 1964 coup, which ushered in two decades of authoritarian rule in which hundreds of people were killed or disappeared. We’ve also reported on how Meta has been accused of trying to meddle in Brazil’s election, claims it has denied.

With our team around the region we have also been reporting a great deal on US military strikes in the Caribbean and off the coast of Ecuador, including this great recent piece by my colleague Tiago Rogero which is definitely worth a read if you missed it. I’ve been back to Venezuela twice this year myself to report on the aftermath of Trump’s invasion to abduct Nicolás Maduro in January. This piece is from a housing estate I visited that came under attack as US attack helicopters swept towards Caracas.

Kirsten Schulz asks: There’s been much reporting about how Gen Z brought in the vote, not least via orchestrated WhatsApp. Lula thanked the women of Brazil for his re-election, or at least the “carinho” they have shown him. What role did women have in the election?

double quotation markTom: Brazil’s nearly 84 million female voters appear to have played a decisive role in the election. One poll just before the second round found that 55% of women planned to vote for Lula, and 45% for Bolsonaro. The number was reversed when it came to male voters: 56% to Bolsonaro and 44% to Lula. Political scientists say women are often repelled by Jair Bolsonaro’s history of radical policies and misogyny.

Before this election, it was predicted that women would have the final say – and that seems to have been in large part what happened. In the final presidential debate, Lula pointed to how Bolsonaro had once voted against extending maternity leave and asked him: “What do you have against women?” He didn’t have a convincing answer for female voters – who make up nearly 53% of the electorate – and seems to have been punished at the polls.

Heather Clarke asks: Thanks so much for your well informed reports from Brazil, you must be exhausted and exhilarated. Lula is 81. Who should we be watching to step into his shoes? Who could be the next leader of the PT (“Partido dos Trabalhadores”)? Also he said he wanted a fresh start but this is his fourth term and he doesn’t have many allies in the Senate, how can he take a progressive agenda forward? And what lessons can progressives take about campaigning to overturn seemingly impossible odds?

double quotation markTom: Thanks for reading, Heather. I am exhausted, indeed. And, like Lula, I need a holiday! Despite constant doom-mongering about Lula’s supposed lack of an heir, I think there are a few interesting names to watch. There’s Flávio Dino, 58, the supreme court minister and former Maranhão governor, who some suspect may step back into politics next year. There’s Rafael Fonteles, the Workers’ party (PT) governor elect of Piauí state, who has played an important role in the 2026 election and is tipped as a possible finance minister in Lula 4.0. He’s 41. Another name to watch is Guilherme Boulos, 44, a charismatic activist and politician who became Lula’s Minister of the General Secretariat last year. His beard and combative style are reminiscent of Lula.

Interestingly, some people have also been talking about the vice-president, Geraldo Alckmin, too, although he’s from the centre right. Lula shared the microphone with Alckmin on election night, leading some to suspect he will play a much bigger role in the next administration and possibly the 2030 election. Finally, we have Fernando Haddad and Camilo Santana, both former education ministers who are close to Lula.

As for what lessons can be learned, I think the role of social media mobilisation is crucial. The ‘Lula’s Army’ WhatsApp group and its 10m members clearly played a huge role in turning the election around, despite clear signs of meddling from US tech giants. The role of young people was also absolutely central to Lula’s fightback. The student-led protests that began in Niterói, near Rio, immediately after Lula’s first round loss, and then swept the country, clearly played a major role in helping Lula regain the initiative, up and down the country.

Even in pro-Bolsonaro parts of the Amazon, I recently saw large numbers of young people out on the streets trying to change voters’ minds and vote Lula, in large part as a result of this mobilisation. Lula hinted at that in his first speech after the result was announced on Sunday night, thanking young people for their “generosity” in fighting for their country’s democracy and future.

Danidown asks: Has Andy Burnham reached out to congratulate Lula yet and what was the main takeaway? Are the PM and Lula actually planning to do any business together on green energy, or is it mostly talk?

double quotation markTom: Yes, he has – although it took Andy Burnham more than 24 hours to release a 33-word tweet, which has caused some perplexity and frustration among Brazilian officials and observers who had hoped for a quick recognition of the result. (In the message Burnham celebrated Lula’s win as “a true victory for democracy and progressive values”.)

That said, Ed Miliband has been in touch with top members of Lula’s team. I was just on the phone with Celso Amorim, Lula’s foreign policy chief and a former ambassador to London, and he has been in touch with Miliband since the election. They know each other well because Amorim once studied with the foreign secretary’s father, the left-wing intellectual Ralph Miliband. Amorim called Burnham’s message “very warm and positive”.

It would be nice to see a British prime minister make an official visit to Brazil – the last such visit was, amazingly, back in 2012 I think, when David Cameron was in power. But the 24-hour delay in Burnham’s tweet suggests, alas, that his government may not see Brazil as a priority. Let’s hope it’s not just talk. The British envoy to Lula’s last inauguration, in January 2023, was the then environment secretary Thérèse Coffey. Hopefully, the prime minister will come this time, although perhaps don’t hold your breath.

(PS: Many years ago I travelled to the Amazon with Ed Miliband for this story so he knows and is interested in Brazil, which is a good sign)

JamesRyan asks: What factors underpin the growth in Christian fundamentalism (specifically the Pentecostal movement) in Brazil? What are its consequences for Brazilian politics, and is this a pattern reflected across South America?

double quotation markTom: Hi James, Pentecostal missionaries first came to Brazil in the early 20th century but there has been a massive expansion of their activities since the 1970s and 80s. Evangelical churches embedded themselves in deprived and neglected communities such as Rio’s favelas, which had grown massively because of the urbanisation process that started in the 50s. Those churches sought to offer immediate solutions to day-to-day problems residents were facing: alcoholism, addiction and urban violence. In recent years the “prosperity theology” preached by these churches has struck a chord in communities and regions where people are desperate to get ahead in this life, rather than the next one.

The reach of such churches spread so quickly – and massively outpaced the Catholic activists who once offered social and spiritual support in such places – because it was relatively easy to recruit pastors, who didn’t need any of the training required to enter the Catholic priesthood. That means that evangelical churches can now be found absolutely everywhere you go in Brazil, from poor communities in the south-east to extremely remote parts of the Amazon, and even illegal goldmines.

The consequences for politics and society have been dramatic – evangelicals now make up nearly 30% of the population – although it’s important not to see the evangelical church as an entirely monolithic movement. Before this year’s election, one well known Rio pastor/politician, a former Bolsonarista called Otoni De Paula, backed Lula.

But many important leaders of the mega churches have thrown their weight behind the Bolsonaro family, believing they will push their conservative agenda when it comes to issues such as abortion, drug legalisation and same-sex marriage. Such leaders have huge sway over their flocks. One poll on the eve of the second round showed that 73% of evangelical voters planned to vote Bolsonaro and 27% Lula.

It is a trend you see across Latin America, to a greater or less extent, from Nicaragua and El Salvador in Central America, to Brazil, Colombia, Peru and Venezuela in South America. In Argentina and Mexico you see it less, so far at least, because of the historic role of the Catholic church.

Jeremy Hurley asks: Why did the Brazil stock market tank after the election result, and will it recover?

double quotation markTom: Basically, because the markets had been convinced that Flávio Bolsonaro was going to win and that after taking power he would implement a “chainsaw” style austerity programme inspired by Javier Milei’s government in Argentina, deal with Brazil’s budget deficit, and bring down interest rates. Lula’s victory came as a shock to investors who believe his fourth presidency will see increased and unsustainable public spending.

Yes, I’m sure things will stabilise at some point soon after the initial panic – during Lula’s previous administrations investors made a lot of money, particularly during his first two terms. There is talk of Lula naming his vice-president, the centre-right politician Geraldo Alckmin, as finance minister in order to calm markets down. Another possible name is Rafael Fonteles, a mathematician who is the governor of Piauí and used to run that state’s finances.

Adam Law asks: Two major issues in Brazil’s election were corruption and violence. There is plenty of discussion about corruption, but it is violence that motivates many to turn politically right politically. Is there a way that the left can deliver on this issue without becoming authoritarian?

double quotation markTom: That’s a really good question and, as someone who lives in Rio de Janeiro, it is one that has been on my mind a lot. The far right has been very good at blaming “soft” left wing policies and federal government incompetence for the failure to control armed violence. It was a huge part of the campaign against Lula in the run up to this election.

But public security is a state matter and Rio state has largely been governed by the right for years now. Their hard-line, shoot first ask questions later approach has not worked - in fact many believe it has made the violence even worse and led to massacres such as the one I covered in the Complexo da Penha almost exactly one year ago. It must be said that similar tactics have been used in Bahia state, which has a huge problem with violence and is governed by the Worker’s party (PT). My colleague Tiago Rogero has written about this.

double quotation markClearly, though, Lula needs to do something different in his fourth term – and it won’t be joining Donald Trump’s “Shield of the Americas” crackdown on “narco-terrorists”, as Bolsonaro had planned to do. Lula has promised to create a public security ministry and “offer protection to workers, and put crooks in jail”. Controversially, during the campaign he said he now backed lowering the age of criminal responsibility to 16 – a major U-turn. That’s a sign that there will be a tougher stance towards crime in Lula 4.0 – but fortunately one that will fall short of the all-guns blazing doctrine supported by Bolsonaro-style politicians. Hopefully, he will continue to crack down on arms trafficking given the huge number of guns that went into circulation during Jair Bolsonaro’s presidency – many of which ended up in the hands of drug dealers Bolsonarista politicians claim they want to fight.

Tragically, I do think we are likely to see even more bloodshed in Rio state, where the recent election was won by Douglas Ruas, a little known Bolsonarista politician and former police officer who has vowed to declare war on crime. Three of the police officers involved in last year’s Complexo da Penha operation have also been elected (one to Congress, two to Rio’s state parliament), showing there is real public support for such policies and actions. Next year is going to be a tough one for residents of the city’s favelas, I fear.

Patric Mauz says: How can Brazil ‘shore up’ the democratic process against outside influences? The natural resources that Brazil stewards is a massive amount and it is so very obvious that wealthy outside parties would very much like to have a say in that. Datacentres and natural resources are more important than the Amazon rainforest or local native tribes to those who have the wealth and power. It will only get worse and that is terrible for future generations.

double quotation markTom: I think the key answer to this question is in the first speech Lula gave after the result was announced on Sunday night. “This was an election in which we had to overcome outside interference, through digital platforms, which we are going to have to regulate,” Lula told us at the São Paulo hotel where he was following the vote count. “It is unacceptable that we don’t recognise that crimes committed in the digital world must be treated the same as real-life crime. We cannot have monstrously huge corporations making millions and billions, paying zero taxes in this country, and trying to steer the outcome of its elections,” Lula went on.

Clearly – not just in the lead-up to this election, but stretching back several years – social networks in Brazil and elsewhere – have been skewing their output in favour of far-right candidates and movements.

On the eve of Sunday’s vote, Meta was accused of actively trying to interfere in Brazil’s election in Bolsonaro’s favour – although it denied those claims.

One of Lula’s biggest – and most complex – tasks during his fourth term will be trying to tackle this problem.

Haranf asks: Has there been a significant decline in logging and deforestation since Lula has been president?

double quotation markTom: Yes, there has been a historic reduction of deforestation, not just in the Amazon but also in the Cerrado savannah at the heart of Brazil. This has been the result of a major push by Lula’s government, under the leadership of former environment minister Marina Silva, who may now return to government having failed to win election to the senate. Lula’s efforts came after four years of destruction under Jair Bolsonaro, who governed from 2019 to 2022, and set about dismantling Brazil’s environmental protection agency, Ibama, and other efforts to protect the environment. Back then, environmental officials were effectively banned from talking to journalists like me. They were grim times for the environment.

When I spoke to Marina Silva on Sunday night she was overjoyed that Lula’s efforts to protect the environment could continue now he had secured a second term. “My heart is happy and filled with gratitude to God and the Brazilian people,” she told me. “The thing I most feared was that our Indigenous territories would be invaded again [under another Bolsonaro government]. Now, I am certain that they will be preserved and that there is no risk of illegal miners returning to destroy these populations.”

That is one of the most uplifting elements of Sunday’s result, I think, although things will not be easy going forward given the major expansion of the far right presence in Congress, which will make stripping away environmental protections easier. ​After the recent first round election, the Climate Observatory umbrella group warned that 64 of Brazil’s 81 senators and 345 of the 513 lower house lawmakers were now in some way hostile to environmental protection issues. So it’s going to be tough for Lula.

DapperDon asks: It seems to me that national football team colours have been hijacked in recent South American elections with conservative and rightwing candidates adopting team colours to claim patriotism. Arguably Bolsonaro Snr was one of the first to politicise national pride back in 2018, but it seemed not to have been as much of a factor in this election with Jnr. Are folk growing accustomed/cynical to this ugly, naked attempts at association, or did the left combat this effectively?

double quotation markTom: I think the left has been quite clever about reclaiming Brazil’s national shirt from the far-right in recent months. For years leftwing Brazilians shunned the jersey because of its association with Bolsonarism. The problem has been compounded by the association of some of the country’s top footballers, such as Neymar, with the Bolsonaro movement and family. But during this year’s World Cup and election I’ve seen lots of progressive people and politicians wearing it.

There has been a concerted effort to make sure all Brazilians can feel pride in the shirt. At Lula rallies you now see lots of vendors selling yellow shirts with Lula’s name and his election number 13 printed on the back. On social media, Lula supporters have even been using the yellow shirt to get around what they see as the bias in terms of promoting left and rightwing content. By wearing the shirt, they believe they can trick the algorithm into turbocharging leftwing content, because it identifies it as being from the right. Let’s hope the trend continues.

On Sunday night, Lula took to the stage to celebrate his victory alongside his social media whizz ally, André Janones, who was wearing the yellow “Canarinho” shirt. That image is on the front page of the Guardian today.

ThirdHandLuke asks: What are your views on Flávio Bolsonaro being banned from leaving Brazil so soon after losing the election? As an interested outsider, to me the optics don’t look particularly great, given Lula’s statement “Democracy always”.

double quotation markTom: The optics do indeed look odd. But Bolsonaro is the focus of several serious criminal investigations and his relatives and allies have a long history of skipping the country to avoid justice. His brother, Eduardo Bolsonaro, left Brazil for the US in early 2025 and has not returned. He would have to serve a four-year sentence for coercion if he returned. Jair Bolsonaro’s former spy chief, Alexandre Ramagem, fled the country through the Amazon last September to avoid having to serve a 16-year sentence for his role in the coup attempt that happened after Lula won the 2022 election. Carla Zambelli, another prominent Bolsonarista, is facing extradition from Italy after fleeing there. Jair Bolsonaro briefly sought shelter in the Hungarian embassy in Brasília, in 2024.

There are unconfirmed reports that Flávio Bolsonaro may have already left the country. Federal police are investigating claims that he fled across the border into Argentina and plans to take a private jet to the US. We are trying to find out what is going on and will keep readers posted.

Yesterday, on Today in Focus, I predicted that Bolsonaro would probably leave the country in the days or weeks ahead because he feared arrest. And it looks like I may have been right. Reports in the Brazilian media today say that allies of Bolsonaro have been trying to get in contact with him this morning but have not been able to.

Brazil has land borders with nine countries and the territory of French Guiana, stretching nearly 10,500 miles. So if someone wants to escape, it is not hard. Yesterday Bolsonaro was given 24 hours to surrender his passport. But he has reportedly yet to do so.

LiamCurran asks: I am delighted with Lula’s victory and the progressive policies and changes that should come with it, but have been severely appalled at his support for Putin and his atrocities committed in the Ukraine war and the general abuse of human rights in Russia. Do you think Lula would ever change this policy and stand up for democracy and justice in Ukraine and Russia?

double quotation markTom: I think Lula’s position on the Ukraine war and its longstanding strategic partnership with Moscow is unlikely to change. Brazil’s agribusiness sector is hugely reliant on fertilisers bought from Moscow. In fact, Brazil is now Russia’s biggest customer of fertilizers such as phosphate. After the start of the war, Brazil also became one of the biggest importers of Russian diesel. The Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy congratulated Lula “on his impressive election result”. He also said that he hoped “Brazil continues to play an active role in international affairs and contributes to the effective protection of human rights, the rights of peoples, and the rights of states”.

Speaking at the UN recently Lula called for diplomatic efforts to end the war. “We all know that there will be no winners on the battlefield. Diplomacy is the only option,” he said. But I would not expect any more forceful condemnation of Vladimir Putin from Brasília, no.

Clara Panozzo asks: How do you think Lula’s win will affect Javier Milei in neighbouring Argentina? And will Lula’s fourth and last term bring about the beginning of the end of the far right turn in the region?

double quotation markTom: I’ve heard it said that, deep down, Javier Milei wanted Flávio Bolsonaro to lose. Had Bolsonaro become Brazilian president, Brazil – as the region’s biggest economy – would surely have become ground zero for the Trumpian far right in South America. Eduardo Bolsonaro – who is close to the Maga movement – would possibly have become foreign minister or ambassador to the US. And Milei – who has tried to position Buenos Aires as a Maga mecca – would have lost his position as Trump’s go-to man in the region.

That said, relations between Brazil and Argentina aren’t going to be easy. Lula clearly despises Milei (I’ve heard he doesn’t even like to utter his name) – and the feelings seem to be mutual. While Lula apparently has a decent relationship with Chile’s hard right president, José Antonio Kast – who has already said he’ll attend Lula’s inauguration in January – Milei is unlikely to be on the guest list.

I think it would be premature to declare the demise of the far right in Brazil, or the region because of Sunday’s result, although it is certainly a major repudiation of the Bolsonaros by a majority of Brazilians. The Bolsonaro movement has put down deep roots in Brazilian society and, while Flávio may now have to leave the country or face jail, there will still be three members of the family in Congress from next year. Key state governments, such as Rio and São Paulo, are run by Bolsonaristas.

double quotation markPerhaps, however, Lula’s election will erode the Bolsonaro clan’s supremacy over the Brazilian right. I suspect one lesson conservative politicians might take from the result is that the Bolsonaro name has, for many voters, become a toxic brand. For them, it might, therefore, be wise to shift away from the Bolsonaros and seek a leader without that baggage and that surname. With Flavio Bolsonaro defeated and Jair Bolsonaro, the movement’s leader, now unlikely to emerge from house arrest – in the short term at least – there might be a window of opportunity for a less extremist right wing leader to emerge.

That said, the smart money is probably on the pro-Bolsonaro governor of São Paulo, Tarcísio de Freitas, becoming the key leader of Brazil’s right. Some even believe that, like Milei, he was secretly rooting against Flávio Bolsonaro because he harbours presidential ambitions of his own and hopes to challenge whoever Lula’s successor is in the 2030 presidential election. Time will tell.

BMcG0305 asks: A couple of questions. Is there any legal way Bolsonaro could use to contest the result? Also is there any potential way to stop certification of the result?

double quotation markTom: In a nutshell, no. His party acknowledged the election results yesterday and although Flávio Bolsonaro has said he will take legal action, legal experts agree he hasn’t a hope in hell of success. Donald Trump’s rather half-hearted claim of a “rigged” election, suggests the US president isn’t going to go to war with Brazil over this either – even if Steve Bannon is calling for all diplomatic and trade relations to be cut. It just isn’t going to happen.

Apart from Milei, rightwing leaders in Latin America have all recognised Lula’s win. In Brazil, meanwhile, prominent rightwing leaders have urged supporters not to take to the streets to protest against the election win, as they did before the 2022/23 coup attempt.

So, as I said in our Today in Focus podcast, I think Bolsonaro’s presidential aspirations – and hopes of challenging the result – are toast.

After losing the first round of Brazil’s presidential election to far right rival Flávio Bolsonaro, incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, better known simply as Lula, pulled off the most stunning political fightback in modern Brazilian history by winning the second, in so doing securing a fourth presidential term. It will see him become the country’s longest-serving leader.

The result – 51.79% to Lula, 48.21% to Bolsonaro – was delivered after a supreme court judge had extended polling time by two hours, and after Lula’s camp accused their opponents of sabotaging public transport in an attempt to keep their voters from the polls. It was the first time in Brazilian electoral history that a first-round verdict had been overturned.

But Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro who is serving a 27-year jail term for his part in an attempted coup after the last presidential election, refused to accept the result – just as his father had done, four years before. He called Lula’s victory “sacanagem”, a Portuguese word that roughly translates as “bullshit”.

Brazil remains a deeply divided country and Lula will have to navigate a hostile Congress, packed full of Bolsonaro allies, if he is to make significant legislative changes in his fourth term. His options are also constrained by a gloomy economic outlook, with Brazil experiencing soaring debt and entrenched structural problems. As he turns 81, will the re-elected president have the energy to face down the far right opposition for another four years and to lay out the ground for a credible successor?

And what does Lula’s latest victory mean for the wider region, and for the country’s relationship with Donald Trump’s administration? In the Guardian’s Opinion pages columnist Jordana Timerman wrote that “Lula’s victory shows that the rightward shift is neither inevitable nor irreversible. But the movement retains substantial electoral support, influence over the political agenda and a powerful ally in Washington.”

McClean, now 37, moved to the Stadium of Light in the summer of 2011 after starting his career with League of Ireland club Derry City. He was handed his debut by then-Sunderland manager O’Neill in a 2-1 win over Blackburn Rovers that December, and quickly endeared himself to the home support with a series of glittering displays on the left wing.

However, his time on Wearside turned sour the following season when he was criticised by a section for his decision not to wear a poppy on his jersey in a home game against Fulham in the the lead up to Remembrance Day. Ahead of the game against the Cottagers, the Black Cats released a short 22-word statement which read: “As a club, SAFC wholeheartedly supports the Remembrance commemorations. It was James’ personal choice not to wear a shirt on this occasion.”

He later stated he could not wear the poppy as he viewed it to be a symbol that commemorates all British military personnel, including those involved in the troubles in his hometown of Derry in Northern Ireland. Now, 14 years on, he has spoken about the aftermath of the statement, and how current Celtic boss O’Neill was the first to inform him of threats made to his life.

"We played Fulham on TV,” he told talkSPORT. “I was called to reception by Martin and there were two police officers. It was extreme, but there was a threat on my life. At the time, nobody knew how to handle it, it was all new. I didn't know how to handle it, it was the first time I had a threat on my life. You have to take it serious.

“They put someone outside my hotel room that night. Did I expect the backlash? No, because it's my personal choice. What impact does my personal choice have on so many lives? It turned out it had a massive impact apparently on people's lives and day-to-day business. The statement didn't say at any point 'we respect James' decision'. It completely distances itself. I had just turned 23, so my belief that Sunderland had a duty of care to look after me, and that they would look after me. Because I had no reason not to think that.

“I didn't know they were putting that statement out. My belief to this day is that the statement made it a bigger story. Straight away it put all eyes on me. We wore red and white, realistically, if that statement doesn't go out who is going to be looking? People wouldn’t have noticed. Why couldn't it have been an in-house thing?

"Martin [O’Neill] protected me. Martin has always been brilliant. It was all new to him as well. Looking back now would I have pushed back more on Martin and the club for me to say something? 100 per cent. But at the time, you're 23 and you've just got off the back of a brilliant season and you think the club had your best interests at heart. They didn't. #3408. Why does the FCC hate Minions routers?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The viral Minions-shaped routers are caught by the same ban as TP-Link.

Seen a Wi-Fi router in the form of a Minion in your feed lately? It isn't AI; it's a real product from the South Korean networking company Davolink. Kevin, the taller of its two Minions routers, went viral after an X post last month called it one of the best Wi-Fi 6E routers money can buy. The post drew millions of impressions and Amazon's stock, priced at $62.99, was gone by the next day.

Unfortunately for Minions fans, the FCC treats the router version of Kevin and Bob like every other overseas consumer router. Since March, the agency has banned all new foreign-made network routers from getting the authorization they need to go on sale in the US, unless the manufacturer has an exemption. ASUS, Eero and Netgear all have exemptions, but Davolink isn't. So, while the two existing Minions routers stay legal to sell, we're not going to be seeing a Wi-Fi 7 Minion stateside anytime soon, and firmware updates for the duo are only guaranteed through the end of 2028.

Davolink first showed the routers at CES back in January 2023, under an exclusive design agreement with Universal and put them on sale in South Korea that summer. Kevin, AKA DVW-632, is a tri-band Wi-Fi 6E router that stands at 9.6 inches tall, with a 2.5 Gbps WAN port, one gigabit LAN port, WPA3 security and mesh support. Its AXE5400 rating breaks down as 573 Mbps on the 2.4 GHz band and 2.4 Gbps each on 5 GHz and 6 GHz. Bob, the DVW-642, is the smaller one, a dual-band Wi-Fi 6 router with a gigabit WAN port and a top rating of 1.77 Gbps.

Davolink's core business is supplying gateways and access points to the country's three big carriers, SK Broadband, KT and LG Uplus. The US government's vulnerability database has only one entry for a Davolink product: a weak password flaw in a DVW-3200N router from 2018. It was rated 9.8 out of 10 for severity and fixed with new firmware.

The taller Kevin's AXE5400 rating puts it in the same class as TP-Link's Archer AXE75. RTINGS, which has tested both, says the Minion comes out on top in terms of speed and range. But while the more traditional-looking Archer has four gigabit LAN ports, Kevin has only one, so anything beyond one wired device will need a network switch. Wi-Fi 7, the generation after Kevin's, adds 320 MHz channels and lets a device use several bands at once.

The FCC added "routers produced in a foreign country" to its Covered List on March 23. That addition was triggered by a national security determination it received three days earlier from an interagency body convened by the White House. Equipment that makes it onto the Covered List can't get the FCC authorization a wireless device needs before it can be sold in the States. Law firm Wiley's interpretation is that "produced" covers any major stage of making a router, including design and development, regardless of the nationality of the company.

Routers authorized before the March listing can still be imported, sold and used, and the FCC authorized Kevin and Bob between November 2023 and February 2024. Listing also bars changes to authorized devices, software updates included. The FCC has waived that for things like security patches and compatibility fixes until at least January 1, 2029, which is why banned drones and routers in the US will still get critical updates until 2029, and its engineering office has said it will recommend writing the waiver into the rules. If that doesn't happen, and the date doesn't change, Davolink will have to stop patching Kevin and Bob after 2028.

Exemptions, which the FCC calls Conditional Approvals, are granted by the Department of Defense or the Department of Homeland Security and last for up to 18 months. Applicants must disclose where every component and the firmware come from, justify building abroad and commit to a dated plan for US manufacturing, including spending figures and quarterly progress reports. Netgear was approved on April 14, Eero on April 22 and ASUS on September 9. Davolink, which has reported operating losses since 2022, hasn't publicly said whether it has applied for a Conditional Approval.

When attorneys general from across the US announced a $17 billion settlement with Meta this summer, the sum was unprecedented. But a ruling last week by a New Mexico judge blew that number out of the water. The judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion to resolve claims that Meta misled users around the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and related incidents. Similar claims were resolved by the settlement with most other states. The outcome raised an obvious question: Should these states have asked for more?

The answer depends on a mix of factors, including whether the $25 billion order stands up to Meta’s legal challenges. Meta’s chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney said in a statement that the company is “confident we will prevail on appeal” and called New Mexico’s lawsuit an attempt to impose “a tax on speech.” If the fine sticks, however, it could create a new appetite for Big Tech’s opponents to stick it out in court — or ask for bigger payouts to settle.

“For the settling AGs, they’re going to look at this and maybe experience some regret for all the claims that they waived, and start looking around and trying to figure out what’s left that they didn’t waive, if they can try to get some money like this too,” says Daphne Keller, director of platform regulation at the Stanford Program in Law, Science & Technology. For the many individuals, school districts, and tribes with outstanding claims, “this is very encouraging and will make them hopeful that continuing to litigate aggressively will lead to a big payout,” Keller says.

The $25 billion court order covered millions of violations of New Mexico’s unfair practices law, after a jury found Meta had misled users about how it protected their data and policed misinformation and hate speech – claims stemming from the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal. In most states, such claims were covered by a section of the kids online safety settlement, resolved with a $459 million payment.

While that number looks comparatively puny, there were upsides to taking it. A settlement provided more certainty and an expedited payout for states even though Meta will be making the payments over 10 years. It also secured product commitments from Meta, like restrictions on teen usage, that might have been more difficult to get from a court ruling. And it allowed those to be implemented soon after the settlement was approved, which advocates say is important to prevent further harm to kids. Other state attorneys general also may have been working with different laws or evidence that could result in outcomes different from those obtained in New Mexico.

“Assuming that even a fraction of this award gets upheld, then they clearly took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico”

But New Mexico’s win, combined with other recent legal victories for AGs and private plaintiffs — including a separate judgment of nearly $1 billion against Meta in New Mexico — may embolden anyone still litigating privacy and kids online safety cases. “Assuming that even a fraction of this award gets upheld, then they clearly took pennies on the dollar compared to New Mexico,” Keller says of the settling states. Imagining she’s an AG with similar claims — like public false statements about privacy or content moderation — “I become less willing to settle in those cases,” Keller adds. “Or if I do settle, I ask for more.”

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier, whose state avoided joining the Meta settlement, seemed to take the latest New Mexico ruling as a validation. “As I said at the time, the multi-state settlement with Meta was a slap on the wrist,” Utheimer wrote on X. “Florida isn’t going to sell out to Meta. We’re going to hold them accountable.” The District of Columbia could be the next place to test whether New Mexico’s win can be replicated since it did not settle its Cambridge Analytica claims and has continued pursuing its lawsuit in court.

“Any one, or 10, or 50 of them could hit the jackpot”

While the AGs who joined the settlement can’t pursue covered claims against Meta anymore, they could still push for more in cases against other social media defendants, including TikTok, YouTube, and Snap. And those who weren’t part of the Meta settlement, including school districts and individuals, may be encouraged to continue pushing ahead. “Realistically, there’s so many cases, it’s not feasible that every single case can have an outcome like this,” says Keller. “Meta actually would go broke because the multipliers are so large. But any one, or 10, or 50 of them could hit the jackpot.”

States have stepped in to pursue tech policy and competition cases where the federal government has declined to, but the wide range of potential outcomes for each state presents its own challenges. As Congress has stalled on major tech policy bills and the Trump administration has often favored lighter regulation on tech, Colorado Law associate professor Blake Reid sees recent cases as a “move toward state-level tech policy.”

“The trick of that is, now you have 50 states that all have different sets of resources, and different sets of politics, and different sets of incentives, and frankly just different skill levels in terms of the sophistication that it takes to do litigation at this scale,” Reid says. “And it’s the same Meta, but now it’s up to 50 small opponents that are going to break in different ways.”

As for whether the $25 billion ruling makes other states rethink their strategy, Reid says there could be a range of approaches, depending partly on whether the figure is upheld on appeal and whether Meta loses yet more cases. “Is that a game that every state attorney general wants to play?” he asks. “And I think the settlement in the other case suggests maybe not.” Still, the new figure may change what AGs think they can achieve in court. And the more losses Meta and other tech companies rack up, the more ambitious they might get.
