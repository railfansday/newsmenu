#3119. Kovac speaks out on the demands of coaching following Kompany’s collapse

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Vincent Kompany is recovering after being hospitalised following a sudden collapse at Bayern Munich’s training ground last week, forcing the Belgian to take a temporary break from his coaching duties.

While Bayern have yet to announce an exact diagnosis, reports have suggested that Kompany’s demanding work schedule and lack of sleep may have contributed to the incident. The Bayern boss is known for his meticulous approach, often working late and prioritising his job over rest.

Speaking at a press conference on Monday, Borussia Dortmund head coach Niko Kovac addressed his colleague’s absence and reflected on the immense pressure that comes with coaching a top-level football club.

“You take pretty much everything home with you. None of us can say that we close our office door, get in the car and leave everything behind. You think about things and consider how you can do better. You can’t switch that off,” Kovac said.

“There is enormous stress when you are fighting relegation. When you are at the top, you are expected to win practically everything, which is almost impossible. It is a very, very demanding profession, and one that is certainly intensified by outside pressures,” he added.

Kovac had already sent his best wishes to Kompany over the weekend, telling Sky:

“Like all football fans, I wish him nothing but the best. I hope he recovers quickly and returns to the touchline soon. What matters most at this stage is giving him peace and quiet, because he needs time.

“We all know how demanding a coaching job can be, so I genuinely wish him all the very best,” he added.

#4585. Read censored texts from around the world at this Minecraft library

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 2:15 PM EDT

High-end graphics, cinematic plotlines, and challenging boss battles aren’t what made Minecraft one of the most popular video games of all time. After nearly 15 years, millions of people still play it every day because it doesn’t offer any of those features. Instead, Minecraft hinges exploring, building, and experimenting inside a virtually infinite digital universe. For many players, logging onto a server provides a sense of freedom that the real world sadly denies them.

It’s not symbolic, either. Minecraft’s digital freedom produces tangible benefits that extend far beyond the game itself. In 2020, the international press advocacy organization Reporters Without Borders (also known as Reporters Sans Frontières, or RSF) announced the Uncensored Library, an online repository filled with books, essays, and articles by individuals routinely silenced by their authoritarian governments. But instead of stashing the collection in an unassuming corner of the game, RSF and the Minecraft design collective BlockWorks built a massive, gorgeous neoclassical library located on a picturesque, pixelated island.

“Freedom of expression is a fundamental human right, and yet, there are countries that seek to censor their citizens or prevent them from accessing certain information,” RSF explais. “This is why The Uncensored Library exists; a grand digital library meant to combat censorship – and it’s all accessible through Minecraft.”

Even six years after its debut, the project regularly goes viral on social media, and receives routine updates from RSF and BlockWorks. There is even a map to guide visitors through the digital institution’s multiple wings that are organized by country. Until recently, the library hosted materials from writers contending with particularly repressive governments across eight nations, including Iran, Saudi Arabia, Russia, and Egypt. Earlier this year, RSF added a section for the United States following the Trump administration’s pushbacks on the press.

“Often, suppressing stories takes a less overt form than direct censorship,” RSF North America executive director Clayton Weimers said at the time, adding that the decision to include the U.S. was intended to highlight that “fully authoritarian regimes do not have a monopoly on free press violations.”

The Uncensored Library is accessible either by downloading the files to your personal computer, or accessing the visit.uncensoredlibrary.com server while playing Minecraft.

#317. The US Government Is Hiding a Secret Court Ruling About How It Spies on Americans

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Two Democratic senators who have long overseen US intelligence say the Trump administration is illegally withholding a secret court’s findings from Congress until lawmakers renew a program that lets federal agents read Americans’ communications without a warrant.

Seven months ago, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court objected to search filters that let intelligence analysts pull Americans’ messages from intercepted data without the approvals, written justifications, or records the law requires. The court approved another year of surveillance anyway. The ruling has never been made public, and Senators Ron Wyden of Oregon and Adam Schiff of California say the administration’s own deadline for releasing it passed in September.

The program, Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), lapsed in June after Congress failed to renew it, though collection continues under the court’s March approval. The administration’s own public documentation says rulings like this one are reviewed for public release “within 180 days of issuance.” That window closed September 13, even as intelligence officials press lawmakers to reauthorize the program by the end of the year.

Wyden, the longest-serving member of the Senate Intelligence Committee, and Schiff, the former chair of its House counterpart, say the administration owes Congress an explanation for the delay, and the names of the officials responsible, by November 6.

“If this administration won’t follow this simple statutory directive,” the senators told Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and Attorney General Todd Blanche in a demand letter obtained exclusively by WIRED, “it is unclear why Congress or the American people should trust this administration to follow any of the other legal limits to Section 702.”

Outside Congress, Demand Progress and 22 other civil liberties and government accountability groups on Monday urged congressional leaders to refuse to reauthorize Section 702 “while the administration actively withholds legally mandated information about how the surveillance program operates.” Signatories range from the American Civil Liberties Union to the conservative-backed Project for Privacy and Surveillance Accountability.

The Trump administration has already been pressed, and has promised, to release the ruling. In April, senators Tom Cotton and Mark Warner, the Intelligence Committee’s Republican chair and Democratic vice chair, pressed the administration to release it within 15 days so it could inform the Senate’s debate. In July, asked in writing whether he would commit to declassifying it by the 180-day deadline, Blanche replied, “Yes.”

Warner “has been clear that he supports transparency with regard to Section 702,” his communications director, Rachel Cohen, tells WIRED. Neither former director of national intelligence Tulsi Gabbard nor Bill Pulte, who briefly replaced her as acting director, “were responsive,” Cohen says, “but Senator Warner expects that DNI Clayton will declassify the opinion, consistent with the bipartisan letter and as required by law.”

Cotton’s office did not respond to a request for comment. The White House, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, and the US Justice Department did not answer questions WIRED sent Friday about the delay.

Clayton, who is legally responsible as director of national intelligence for declassifying the ruling, has made no such promise. Asked before his July confirmation whether he would ensure the opinion was public before Congress voted on reauthorization, he wrote only that the government was “working arduously to expeditiously declassify” it and had made the full opinion available to lawmakers.

Some of what the court found is already known. A FISA court opinion declassified last year described a similar FBI tool, a “participants filter” that let agents select people in contact with surveillance targets and read their messages. In February 2025, the Justice Department’s National Security Division told the court the tool had been running searches no one counted. The FBI shut it off the next month and switched it back on later that year, after the department approved a rebuilt version.

This year’s ruling went beyond that. The court found that tools agencies use to narrow intercepted data to an American’s messages amount to searches of Americans, subject to the same limits, and ordered them rebuilt, The New York Times reported in April, citing unclassified administration talking points sent to lawmakers. Senator Alex Padilla said in a written question to Blanche that the Justice Department “is appealing portions” of the ruling “limiting the government’s use of certain filtering tools.”

While the ruling stayed secret, seven intelligence agency fact sheets from March 2026, one dated the day of the ruling, cited a 98.9 percent compliance rate for FBI searches of Americans and credited Section 702 with stopping a planned terrorist attack on a Taylor Swift concert in Vienna. None mentions the court’s findings. The figure appears nowhere in the Justice Department inspector general’s October 2025 review of those searches, which put the compliance rate at 98.6 percent and could not conclude that “the FBI’s querying compliance issues are entirely in the past.”

According to the fact sheets, FBI auditors review every such search. FBI director Kash Patel closed the bureau’s Office of Internal Auditing last May and moved its auditors into another division. The bureau’s search counts before 2025 also omit queries run through the participants filter, searches the government’s own privacy watchdog says are “not possible to track.”

Lawmakers who have read the ruling hint that it says more. Senator Dick Durbin, the top Democrat on the Judiciary Committee, urged colleagues on the Senate floor in June to read it in a secure room. “I have seen it,” he said. Once they did, he told them, “your opinion of the role of FISA and the rights of Americans will be impacted for sure.”

Wyden has also warned about a classified interpretation of Section 702 that he says multiple administrations have relied on and refused to declassify. “When it is eventually declassified,” he said in March, “the American people are going to be stunned that it took so long and that Congress had been debating this authority with insufficient information.”

The Trump administration has until November 6 to answer the senators. The court’s current approval of the program expires in March.

Surveillance reform advocates say the administration may try to avoid that fight by attaching a renewal to the annual defense authorization bill, which Congress usually finalizes in December. In 2023, lawmakers extended Section 702 for four months through that year’s defense bill after a Senate effort to strip the provision failed. This year’s House and Senate versions of the bill do not yet include it.

#3926. After a year of testing, one of Steam's best features is finally out of beta, making it easier to find games you might actually like

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Steam's Personal Calendar, a feature Valve has been testing for nearly a year, has finally left beta and is now available for everyone.

Personal Calendar, which initially launched as a Steam Labs experiment last October, works by highlighting upcoming games and recent releases - tailored to your interests - and arranging them by release date. The feature has its own section on the store homepage, and also has a dedicated page showing the games Steam's algorithm believes you're going to like, meaning what's included is different for everyone.

While Steam has always had several ways of recommending games to players based on their tastes, Personal Calendar takes it a step further by having a stricter recommendation criteria and arranging the results in an intuitive, month-based way that's less likely to overwhelm.

Artificial intelligence giant Anthropic is pushing deeper into drug discovery, building its own molecular biology lab and investing in research that could eventually produce drug candidates, according to the company's blockbuster IPO filing.

The maker of the Claude AI platform laid out its growing ambitions in life sciences in a much-anticipated S-1 filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission on Monday, while acknowledging that its expansion into drug development brings scientific, regulatory and operational challenges that extend well beyond its traditional software business.

In the run-up to its initial public offering, Anthropic outlined its vision for what it calls “Powerful AI,” a highly capable form of artificial intelligence that the company believes could become widely available within a few years. Anthropic envisions AI systems that could match or exceed the capabilities of top human experts across scientific disciplines, perform complex tasks autonomously and operate laboratory equipment and robotic systems.

“If Powerful AI is directed at solving humanity’s hardest problems and the risks are managed responsibly, the potential is extraordinary,” Anthropic said in the filing, pointing to the possibility of “accelerating drug discovery, disease detection and the delivery of personalized therapy,” as one of the technology's potential benefits.

Anthropic is preparing to go public under the Nasdaq ticker symbol “ANT,” with an offering that could raise more than $100 billion and push its valuation toward $2 trillion, according to an August report from The New York Times. The S-1 does not yet specify an offering price or the number of shares to be sold.

Meanwhile, Anthropic has already made inroads into the drug development industry through partnerships with pharma giants such as Novo Nordisk and Bristol Myers Squibb. It also brought Novartis CEO Vas Narasimhan onto its board of directors in April.

Anthropic's push into the sector also includes its Claude for Life Sciences platform, designed to support scientists and researchers across drug development, from early discovery and preclinical research to clinical trial operations and regulatory submissions.

According to the filing, the platform integrates with electronic laboratory notebooks, scientific databases and other research tools while providing specialized capabilities in organic chemistry, structural biology, laboratory protocols and bioinformatics.

Beyond its partnerships with drugmakers, Anthropic is building its own life sciences research capabilities, hiring industry specialists, establishing a molecular biology laboratory and deploying Claude in drug discovery and development through its own researchers.

Anthropic is also looking to bring AI into physical laboratory operations. The company said its Model Hardware Standard enables AI agents to operate laboratory and manufacturing equipment, including microscopes, liquid handlers and commercially available robotic arms.

The company has further expanded its internal research capabilities through its April acquisition of Coefficient Bio, a computational biology startup, in a deal previously reported to be worth $400 million. The S-1 disclosed that the transaction involved 15.4 million Anthropic shares, with a portion of the consideration tied to continued employee service.

Together, those efforts suggest Anthropic is positioning itself to participate more directly in scientific discovery rather than simply supplying technology to researchers.

Eric Kauderer-Abrams, Anthropic's head of biology and life sciences, told Fierce Healthcare in January that healthcare and life sciences represent one of the company's biggest strategic bets.

That opportunity comes with a new set of risks, however, and Anthropic's IPO filing offers a particularly candid assessment of the challenges it could encounter as its research ambitions expand.

The company acknowledged that its life sciences research program requires activities outside its historical experience as a software and AI research organization, including handling biological and chemical materials, operating laboratory equipment and complying with biosafety and environmental requirements.

Those activities could expose Anthropic to new liabilities, including workplace injuries and environmental contamination, while potentially subjecting its operations to preclinical and clinical research regulations.

Anthropic specifically cautioned that any drug candidates or discoveries emerging from its research would face the “lengthy, costly, and uncertain development and regulatory pathways characteristic of the life sciences industry” and might never generate revenue.

The company also highlighted the potential consequences of using AI in sensitive scientific and healthcare settings, including errors, inaccurate results and hallucinations that could contribute to patient harm or compromised research.

“We may be unable to fully understand or explain why our models produce particular outputs or behave in particular ways, which could limit our ability to predict or prevent failures, provide explanations to customers or regulators, or satisfy emerging regulatory, transparency and explainability requirements,” the company said in the S-1.

As Anthropic expands its healthcare and life sciences offerings, it could also face oversight under laws governing drugs, biologics, medical devices and protected health information, including regulations administered by the FDA and comparable international agencies.

Regulators could require the company to obtain additional authorizations, modify its products or restrict certain applications, the filing warned.

Anthropic has already faced regulatory and political headwinds. In March, the Department of Health and Human Services barred employees from using Claude as President Donald Trump sought to blacklist the company's technology across the federal government. Competing products from OpenAI and Google remained available to federal employees.

The dispute also raised questions about AI adoption at the FDA, which initially relied on Claude for its Elsa platform, designed to help accelerate aspects of the drug review process. The agency has since transitioned to Google's Gemini as its primary AI model.

Despite those challenges, Anthropic is betting on life sciences as a significant growth opportunity. The company cited expectations that global pharmaceutical spending would exceed $2 trillion in 2026 as it outlined plans to expand into industries where AI could accelerate new discoveries.

Its IPO filing makes clear that the company sees a role for itself on both sides of that equation, supplying AI technology to drugmakers while building the research infrastructure to pursue scientific discoveries of its own.
