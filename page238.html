#4694. HRC Parts Are Officially Coming for the Honda Civic Type R, Acura Integra Type S, and More

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Owners of the Honda Passport and Pilot TrailSport models can get in on the fun, too.

Honda has been teasing us with an HRC (Honda Racing Corporation) performance parts division for a couple of years—and as of Thursday, we have word that it’s finally here.

HRC is using the 2026 SEMA Show in Las Vegas as a launching pad for the initiative, as it’s planning on showing off a few ready-to-buy parts in addition to prototypes of future parts. Just as you might suspect, the current Honda Civic Type R and Acura Integra Type S are the stars of the show, and will be the two cars where most of these parts can be installed.

Initially, HRC will be offering a new forged aluminum wheel made in partnership with 2elle-engineering for the Type R and Type S. (This Italian wheel-maker supplies the wheels for the Acura ARX-06 race car, so it only makes sense to extend the partnership to the road.) A 19-inch wheel in either gray or bronze will be offered: it’s 0.5 inch wider than the factory wheel, and drops 5.5 pounds per corner; the offset is 55mm versus 60mm from factory. Honda says it tested the wheel on the Nürburgring, noting its reduction in unsprung weight, wider width, and increased negative offset all help improve handling. Not to mention, the offset should naturally improve the car’s stance. Unfortunately, we don’t have a price yet.

In addition to the wheels, Honda says it intends to show off prototypes of adjustable coilovers with camber plates, a six-piston Brembo brake kit (the stock Type R has a four-piston caliper), and a short throw shifter. We don’t know the details on these parts yet, but there could be more to learn at SEMA this year.

Beyond the Type R and Type S, HRC is also offering parts for the Passport and Pilot. For folks who intend to do more than the typical off-roading in their TrailSport models, you can buy a new 18-inch off-road-focused wheel developed with Titan 7. This forged aluminum wheel allows for an additional 220-pound load capacity beyond the factory wheel. Both black and bronze finishes will be available, and “Honda Racing” will be machined into the wheel face.

And in case you wanted a louder Passport or Pilot, HRC has you covered there with a cat-back exhaust system developed with Borla. This stainless steel exhaust increases flow rate by 6 percent, and the system’s design increases ground clearance by up to 1.9 inches to ensure it isn’t damaged when off-roading. Honda says it uses “Anti-Drone” technology from Borla to keep things quiet on the highway, but the idea is to accentuate the “natural sound” of the 3.5-liter V-6. Tips with the HRC logo finish the job out back.

HRC says all of these parts will be available through Honda and Acura dealerships and the Honda DreamShop. That said, you’ll need to wait until the official on-sale date in March 2027 to bring them home.

Despite being constantly minimized to just "small-market" teams, you shouldn't be surprised to see a Tampa Bay Rays vs. Milwaukee Brewers World Series. It is a heavyweight bout that we don't always get these days. Remember, the World Series was simply between the teams with the best record in each league until 1969, when the leagues implemented the League Championship Series. Still, when only four teams made the playoffs, matchups between the teams with the best record in each league were common. Once the wild card was introduced in 1995, the extra playoff teams invited more upsets. This season now marks just the sixth World Series in the Wild Card Era with two No. 1 seeds facing off.

The year after MLB canceled its season due to a labor fight, the best team in baseball resided in Cleveland at 100-44, a full 10 games ahead of the second-best team. That would be the Braves, who went on to win the World Series in six games. Five of the six games were one-run affairs, with the lone outlier being a 5-2 Braves win in Game 4. Tom Glavine had a 1.29 ERA in his two starts to win MVP, while Greg Maddux had a 2.25 ERA. The Braves homered eight times, including three from Ryan Klesko.

The dynasty Yankees won 98 games that regular season, but the Braves, with 103 wins, had the best MLB record. No matter, the Yankees dispatched Atlanta with a four-game sweep, outscoring the Braves 21-9. Mariano Rivera had a win and two saves to win MVP, Derek Jeter hit .353, and Roger Clemens, David Cone and Orlando Hernandez were dominant on the mound.

More than a decade passed before two top seeds squared off again. The Red Sox took Game 1, but then lost the next two and were down heading to the fifth inning of Game 4 when a fired-up David Ortiz gathered his teammates for a pep talk. A three-run Jonny Gomes home run in the sixth inning put the Red Sox on top for good, and they never trailed again, winning the title in six. Big Papi himself was the MVP after going an absurd 11 for 16 (.688) with two doubles, two homers and six RBI. He walked eight times against only one strikeout. Add it all up and he had a 1.948 OPS. Jon Lester went 2-0 with a 0.59 ERA, too.

The 60-game season with no fans was forgettable. MLB decided to flood the playoffs with 16 teams and yet the two best teams still emerged, as the 43-17 Dodgers faced the 40-20 Rays. The Dodgers hit 12 home runs in the series, getting two apiece from Mookie Betts, Corey Seager and Justin Turner. The Rays took a wild Game 4 with a walk-off thanks to an error at home plate to even the series at two games apiece. The Dodgers won Game 5 and then trailed 1-0 in Game 6 with Blake Snell absolutely dealing for the Rays through five. With one out in the sixth, he gave up a single and was removed. The Dodgers scored two runs after the pitching change, bringing the series home. Seager won MVP.

The Yankees only needed 94 wins to achieve the best AL record, while the Dodgers paced the NL with 98. Heading into the series, Dodgers first baseman Freddie Freeman could barely walk on an injured ankle. It was a big storyline heading into Game 1. Then he hit a walk-off grand slam. He homered again in Game 2. And Game 3. He won MVP after compiling four home runs and 12 RBI in the five games.

The Dodgers had a 3-0 lead before the Yankees took Game 4 and took a 5-0 lead in Game 5, only to blow that in the fifth inning, which included multiple defensive gaffes. The series was a lot closer than the 4-1 series tally suggests, but the Dodgers were the team that kept coming through when it mattered.

#4835. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, the Gravity-Defying Staircase Was Designed to Look Like a Hand Fan

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Along the banks of the Huangpu River in Shanghai, a sweeping spiral staircase winds along the side of a white building, flowing upward toward the roof like a puff of smoke hanging in the air. But journey below the gyratory geometry, and a new image appears: The steps and railing become the canvas and sticks of a hand fan unfurled in the wind. This is the recently unveiled Shanghai Grand Opera House, where the site’s sinuous pièce de résistance can be enjoyed well before the curtain rises.

“Our initial thought wasn’t about what the building should look like, but rather: How can an important cultural institution be integrated into everyday public life in one of the world’s most vibrant cities?” says Kjetil Trædal Thorsen, founding partner of Snøhetta, the firm behind the project. “We wanted to explore how to transform it into a public landscape—a place where people can stop, experience, and enjoy themselves, whether they are watching a performance or not.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House is located on the banks of the Huangpu River, with a total construction area of ​​approximately 1.6 million square feet. It officially opened to the public on October 17, 2026.

The Grand Opera House’s overall architecture responds to the rhythm of the Huangpu River’s tides with a fluid and dynamic form. The continuously rising spiral roof integrates diverse functions such as dining spaces, exhibition spaces, education centers, and the theater into a cohesive whole.

Overlooking the Shanghai Grand Opera House, this double-track grand staircase, where one can sit and stroll, has become the stage and theater for the entire city of Shanghai.

A decade in the making, the completed project is one of the most significant in contemporary architecture. Spanning roughly 1.6 million square feet, the building encompasses three auditoriums that are equipped for diverse musical performances, including traditional opera, classical music, and even experimental productions. In addition to the venues, the building includes rehearsal, production, education, exhibition, and hospitality spaces, creating an all-encompassing cultural experience.

The double helix staircase is the core and main body of the entire building, with independent two-way circulation routes that cleverly separate the flow of people. From below, it resembles a hand fan.

When conceiving the project, Snøhetta sought inspiration from the building’s site. Located in the Expo Back Beach area, the adjacent Huangpu River with its flowing stream, passing ships, and winding bends prompted the building’s most important design concept: movement. “While the Shanghai Grand Opera House certainly has distinctive visual characteristics, its form stems from considerations of circulation, participation, and public accessibility. The building’s geometry is closely linked to how people enter, ascend, explore, and experience the space,” Kjetil says.

Take the striking steps. While they’re the most visually captivating aspect of the design, they’re also rooted in function, allowing patrons and passersby alike to ascend them and experience the structure in new ways. Once you reach the roof, the views of the Puxi skyline and the river further connect visitors to the environment.

This idea continues throughout the rest of the complex, but as Kjetil explains, “the experience unfolds gradually.” Inside, large glass-curtain walls bring natural light into the sweeping lobby; as patrons move closer to the theaters, the design becomes more intimate, while the colors and textures become increasingly rich. “People move between open public landscapes and more intimate indoor environments; natural light changes with the alternation of day and night and the changing seasons,” he adds.

The indoor plaza of the Shanghai Grand Opera House comprises three theaters with a total seating capacity of 4,200.

Each of the three theaters also possesses a distinct spatial character. The Red Hall, with 2,000 seats, is the heart of the building. A deep burgundy color envelops the audience seating, and its compact five-tiered layout minimizes the distance between the audience and the stage. The meticulously designed balconies, boxes, and walls not only create a visual rhythm but also serve to reflect and propagate sound, turning the theater into its own expertly tuned instrument.

The main theater, the Red Hall, has 2,000 seats and is the core performance space of the entire building. The seating is divided into five levels, including floor seating, orchestra seating, and the three-tier balcony seating.

Shen Hall, with 1,200 seats, draws inspiration from the spatial form of classical opera houses and can accommodate various performance types, including Baroque opera, classical opera, local operas, and dramas. The Hall of Mirrors, with 1,000 seats, offers greater flexibility; the stage and seating can be adjusted to meet different production needs, creating more possibilities for experimental performances. The Hall of Mirrors also connects to the external landscape through a large glass-curtain wall, allowing light and the scenery beyond to become part of the performance.

Shen Hall has 1,200 seats, providing a more immersive viewing environment for smaller productions.

But great design isn’t just about what’s on the surface. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, a network of interconnected tunnels and paths support behind-the-scenes movement necessary for a building program such as this. Audiences, actors, orchestras, stage crew, and backstage staff each have their own distinct movement paths, and multiple theaters must be able to operate simultaneously without interfering with one another. For example, the symphony orchestra’s rehearsal space and orchestra pit are located on corresponding underground floors, allowing for convenient horizontal transport of large instruments.

The Hall of Mirrors uses the outdoor landscape as a backdrop for the stage. It features 1,000 seats and a flexible stage and seating layout. More like an open art laboratory, it allows for diverse performance possibilities.

As noted, the Shanghai Grand Opera House is more than just a performance venue; it’s also meant to be a touchpoint for anyone in the city, audience member or not. “For us, ‘publicness’ is not just an ideological vision; it must be translated into a spatial reality,” Kjetil says. “People can connect with this space in many ways: watch performances, stroll on the rooftop, meet friends, visit exhibitions, admire the Huangpu River view, and even relive past memories here.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House uses prefabricated components and partial cast-in-place technology to create the striking curved stairs.

In an era where architectural images spread rapidly through social media, this building, resembling a white folding fan, undoubtedly has the potential to become a popular landmark. But Kjetil is more concerned with whether people will want to return. “Photographs can spark curiosity, but truly meaningful architecture evokes emotional attachment,” he says. “If I return here in 50 years, I hope to see this building still deeply integrated into the daily life of Shanghai; in people’s eyes, it will no longer be just a ‘new landmark,’ but a familiar part of the city.”

#5527. SEIA analysis: Solar is curbing consumer energy costs

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As energy affordability emerges as a defining issue heading into the midterm elections, new analysis from the Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA) shows how solar and energy storage are lowering electricity costs in both red and blue states. Growth in solar and energy storage helped drive down average wholesale electricity prices by 42% in Texas and 51% in California from summer 2021 to summer 2026, according to the analysis.

A West Virginia residential solar installation by Solar Holler.

Prices fell even as electricity demand grew in both states, according to the new analysis. Over the same five-year period, solar generation increased 421% in Texas and 90% in California, while battery storage capacity grew more than 26-fold in Texas and nearly sevenfold in California.

From June through August 2026, solar and storage saved Texans approximately $3.55 billion and Californians approximately $2.5 billion compared with the same period in 2021.

“As the midterm elections approach, delivering affordable, reliable power is a priority that transcends party lines and Texas and California are proof that the growth of solar and storage is driving down prices,” said SEIA CEO Tim Pawlenty.

In California, electricity use grew 2.5%, while the average wholesale price fell from $77.54/MWh to $38.32/MWh after accounting for inflation. In Texas, electricity demand surged 30%, yet the average wholesale price fell from $54.97/MWh to $31.77/MWh.

Lower wholesale prices have helped reduce generation costs for residential customers, even as other expenses have pushed bills higher. From summer 2021 to summer 2026, residential generation costs fell 4% in Texas and 10% in California. Solar and storage growth has helped put downward pressure on energy prices despite transmission and distribution costs driving up overall residential electricity bills by 3% in Texas and 22% in California over the same time period.

The analysis compared weighted average hourly wholesale electricity prices from June to August in 2021 with the same period in 2026 across the CAISO and ERCOT markets, using inflation-adjusted 2026 dollars.

#5636. Tesla (TSLA) quietly cuts 2026 capex guidance to $20B

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla has lowered its 2026 capital spending forecast. In its Q3 10-Q, the company now says it expects capital expenditures “to be in excess of $20 billion in 2026,” down from the “more than $25 billion” it had guided since April.

The change was in the 10-Q Tesla filed Wednesday, not in the shareholder letter it released the same day. And it lands in a quarter where Tesla reported $1.53 billion of positive free cash flow, a number that depends a lot on timing.

Tesla started 2026 guiding to about $20 billion in capex. In April, it raised that to more than $25 billion, and in July, CFO Vaibhav Taneja reiterated the figure while Elon Musk called 2026 a “massive capex year.”

Now it’s back to “in excess of $20 billion.”

Tesla has spent $13.01 billion so far this year: $2.49 billion in Q1, $5.79 billion in Q2 and $4.73 billion in Q3. Hitting $25 billion would have required about $12 billion in Q4 alone, more than double its biggest quarter. The new floor only requires about $7 billion.

The 10-Q says 2026 spending is “driven by our SI initiatives, including investments in compute infrastructure and data centers,” along with factory ramps and Tesla’s fleet of “company-operated SI-enabled assets.”

There’s a catch in the cash numbers, though. Tesla had $3.67 billion of equipment and construction spending sitting in liabilities at the end of September, meaning it was booked but not yet paid. That’s up from $2.63 billion at the end of June and $1.63 billion a year ago. So Tesla’s actual spending is running ahead of the cash capex it reports.

Tesla reported $6.26 billion in operating cash flow and $1.53 billion in free cash flow after capex. Here’s how it got there:

Depreciation and stock compensation are normal add-backs. They reduce profit without using cash.

The working capital line is the one to watch. Without that $2.59 billion, Tesla’s free cash flow would have been about negative $1.06 billion.

Most of it came from bills Tesla hasn’t paid yet. Accounts payable rose $1.92 billion in the quarter and accrued liabilities rose $1.95 billion, including $722 million more in “accrued purchases,” goods Tesla received but hadn’t been invoiced for.

Another $1.26 billion came from deferred revenue, mostly energy storage. Tesla’s energy deferred revenue jumped to $3.30 billion from $2.31 billion. But not all of that is cash. Tesla disclosed that it billed SpaceX $588 million for Megapacks “that have not been delivered,” which SpaceX hadn’t paid at quarter-end. That amount sits in both deferred revenue and accounts receivable, which rose $565 million.

Those gains were partly offset by a $1.82 billion increase in prepaid expenses and other assets, and a small inventory build.

If you also count the $1.03 billion increase in unpaid capex, Tesla’s free cash flow on an “as spent” basis was closer to $500 million.

The capex cut comes a month after Tesla signed $30 billion of new credit facilities. The 10-Q details them: a $20 billion delayed-draw term loan maturing in 2029, and $10 billion of revolving credit replacing a $5 billion facility. Tesla hadn’t drawn on either at quarter-end, and it had $45.4 billion in cash and investments.

Rearranging the deck chairs ahead of the merger?

None of this is improper in today’s accounting standards, which we can argue are pretty awful. Paying suppliers later, collecting deposits, and booking capex when it’s incurred are all somewhat normal accounting, and Tesla disclosed them. But they explain why the “$1.5 billion of free cash flow” headline from Wednesday’s results says less about the business than it seems.

#3766. What to Wear When Exercising Outdoors, Based on How Cold It Is

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I fell in love with cold weather running while I was training for a spring marathon. It wasn’t a choice at first: Some weeks, the weather was bad for days on end, and if I stayed home, I would fall behind in my training. Everybody else in my training group was braving the elements, so I did too—with no regrets.

I got to run on quiet mornings while snow was softly falling. I signed up for a winter race that herded finishers into a heated barn for hot chocolate and soup. I set a personal record at a spring race, during which I pushed myself hard without breaking a sweat. I now find it sad when somebody tells me they’re afraid of running in the cold, or that they would run in the winter but don’t know what to wear.

No, cold weather doesn’t have to stop you from running, but getting dressed can be a puzzle: How do you keep from freezing your butt off at first and avoid overheating once you start to sweat? Fortunately, it isn’t as tough as it looks. With the right gear, your fingers and ears can stay toasty while you jog through a winter wonderland. Below, I’ll give you a temperature-by-temperature running cheat sheet, and then dive into the specifics on how to buy the best socks, shirts, and more.

When I get dressed to run in winter, I take a look at the “feels like” temperature, not the actual high or low. I also make sure to check the weather for the time I’ll go running, not for the whole day. It doesn’t matter how warm it will be at 2 p.m. if my run will be happening at 6 a.m., before the sun is up. I use the Carrot Weather app, but any app that gives you hourly feels-like temps will work just fine.

Next, use the 20-degree rule. Imagine that the temperature is 20 degrees (Fahrenheit) warmer than it really is. What would you wear then? Here's an example: if it’s 50 degrees out, you should dress as if it were 70: in a T-shirt and shorts, most likely. If it’s 30 degrees, you’d dress as if it were 50: long pants and maybe a light jacket. Get the idea?

Everybody has their own preferences. Some people wear shorts even when it’s snowing, while others will pop a beanie on as soon as there’s a detectable chill in the air. I’m a pretty middle-of-the-road runner when it comes to keeping warm, so here’s what I wear at each “feels like” temperature, starting with warmer weather and working our way colder. I'll give a guide to each type of garment or accessory at the end.

At 50+ degrees Fahrenheit, I’m wearing basically the same things I would for a summer run.

Head and hands: Nothing special. No gloves and no hat, unless it’s a baseball cap to shade my eyes from the sun.

We’re starting to feel the first bit of a chill. This is where I may start bringing gloves, although they’ll probably get stuffed into a pocket by the end of the run. Remember, with the 20-degree rule, it will feel like 60 degrees by the time you're warmed up.

Bottoms: Leggings, lightweight long pants, or shorts.

Top: Something with long sleeves, but ideally made of thin material (think long-sleeved T-shirt, not a full-on sweatshirt or jacket). If you wore a sun shirt in the summer, that's a good layering option for these cool temps. I like my REI Sahara Shade hoodie here, but any long sleeve will do. If you run fall and winter races, you'll probably be handed a free long-sleeved version at some point.

Hands: I usually just pull my shirt's sleeves over my hands, but you could wear knit gloves if you want. Consider something like Flipmits for an intermediate option (they can be folded down into a wrist cuff when your hands warm up).

Head: A headband if you need protection for your ears.

You may see a few snowflakes drifting down, but don’t bundle up in a snowsuit. Once you’re warmed up, it will only feel like 50 degrees—so go for something in “light jacket” territory.

Bottoms: Leggings or running tights. The fleece-lined kind can be nice. (I have these.)

Top: You’ll want some kind of jacket or sweatshirt. A lightweight, reflective rain jacket is great here, or just throw on a regular hoodie over your T-shirt. (I go with cotton sweats in dry weather or light snow, but I'd go for synthetics or wool if there's rain or sleet.)

Hands: Definitely wear gloves. The cheap knit ones are usually good enough, but consider treating yourself to a nice pair of running gloves.

Head: Your choice of hat or headband; I’d stick with the headband.

Feet: This is around the temperature you may start to want warmer socks. That goes double if snow is in play: snow that falls onto your shoes will work its way through the mesh, melt, and make your feet wet. Wool is a good material here, so consider something like these from Smartwool.

This is where I’ll officially say it’s “cold.” Whatever warm-weather gear you were getting away with at warmer temps, you’ll probably have to swap it out now. Shorts people will break out their long pants. Headband people will wear a real hat.

Bottoms: Leggings and shorts. I don’t care if you think it looks goofy to wear both; this is an A+ hack. Or try a puffer skirt.

Top: It’s definitely jacket weather. Rather than getting a warmer jacket, I like to wear the same light jacket as I did at 30 degrees, but this time with a long-sleeved fleece or wool base layer underneath.

Hands: Real gloves, please. You can keep wearing your little knit gloves if you’re able to tuck your hands inside your jacket sleeves, or if you have a pair of mittens to wear over top; otherwise, get windproof gloves.

Head: A hat, and maybe also a buff for your nose and mouth. (If you have any cloth masks still hanging around from the early COVID days, they’re perfect for cold weather runs.)

Feet: Warm socks for sure. If you’ll be doing a lot of running in these temperatures, you may want shoes that are specialized for the elements. Look for ones with Gore-Tex uppers, like the Brooks Ghost GTX.

This is where I tap out. If a run is just barely in the teens, I’ll make do with the 20-degree items I mentioned above, adding an extra layer wherever needed. But if that isn't enough, I opt for the treadmill. You certainly can run in this weather if you are determined. You’ll want windproof everything, some kind of face covering, and you may need eye protection such as goggles.

Whenever the weather gets to your own personal “too cold” temperature, remember that you still have options. The early afternoon is often the warmest part of the day, so a run may be tolerable then. There’s also no shame in using a treadmill (even if you have to join a cheap gym for this reason alone).

Everybody has different preferences, and with practice you’ll figure out if you’re the kind of person who wears three shirts before it hits freezing or the type to run through the snow in shorts. But this should give you a starting point when you head out for your first (or next) cold weather run.

As you continue on your cold-weather running journey, you may need to buy some new gear or repurpose things from elsewhere in your closet. And yes, the first few minutes of your run will feel chilly. But if you’re wearing the right shoes and clothes (and planning routes with a few extra factors in mind, which I’ll discuss below), winter running is not just feasible; it’s actually fun. So here are the items you may want to consider.

Your feet need to be warm and, if possible, dry. Warm socks go without saying, but there are some extra considerations when you’re picking socks to run in. Wool socks are ideal because they keep your feet warm even when they get wet. You may not think of winter as a wet time, but any snow that meets up with your body heat will turn into water. If you don’t have wool socks, synthetic wicking fabrics are a good second choice. Cotton is a no-no

First, the socks have to be able to fit into your shoes. If you’ve been running in super thin cotton or synthetic socks all summer, thick socks may not fit. Smartwool run socks are available in a thin version that can fit no problem—but in colder weather you’ll want some thickness for extra warmth. That thickness, in turn, may mean that your winter running shoes need to be roomier than the shoes you wear in summer. If you’re buying a new pair, make sure to try them on with the type of socks you intend to run in.

If your warm socks fit, you can make do with the same shoes you wore in the summer. Your feet will stay dry if the weather is dry and you stay out of puddles. If you encounter snow and slush, though, your feet will have to endure warm-but-wet conditions for the rest of your run. That can be okay for short runs, but for serious winter running, consider upgrading your shoes.

Some running shoes are available with water-resistant uppers. This level of water resistance will keep stray snowflakes out of your socks, but it isn’t reliable if you’ll be stepping in slush puddles. In that case, you’ll want something more waterproof, like Gore-Tex shoes, many of which are made for running trails.

If you expect to run on a lot of ice or packed snow, especially on trails, spikes can give you a little extra grip.

Yaktrax makes a rubber harness that stretches to fit your shoe, with steel coils and small spikes, for about $40.

Microspikes are a notch up from there, costing around $90 for what look like a miniature version of the chains you’d put on car tires.

And if you’re really dedicated—and are seeking a purpose-built pair of snow shoes—you can attach individual spikes: either these traction spikes from IceSpike, or via a DIY approach using sheet metal screws. (There’s a tutorial here.)

The versions that include rubber, like Yaktrax, can wear out quickly on pavement, so it’s best to save these for runs on trails or on unplowed paths. You’ll also want to be careful about the fit. I tried a pair that were similar to Yaktrax, but they were a tad too big. Shortly into my run, I found that the rubber stretched enough to pull the front set of spikes up over the top of my shoe. I've since gotten better-fitting ones, and they work great.

Spikes and specialized shoes help you go more places, in worse weather—but remember that they’re not required to just head out on a chilly day. Regular shoes are fine if the weather is cold but dry, or if you’re willing to put up with a little moisture.

I actually love when the weather gets cold enough that I need a jacket, because then I get to wear something that gives me extra pockets! Stick with thin jackets that have some room inside. By the time temps dip into the 20s or below, you’ll be doing some serious layering: for example, an undershirt, a long-sleeved warm layer, and a jacket. A great option for that warm layer is a wool base layer like this one. (Don’t worry, it’s not itchy.) If you can’t wear wool, a synthetic fleece is a good alternative.

Before you run out to buy all new clothes, experiment with what’s in your closet. Try repurposing a thin sweater, for example. Some people would never wear cotton for running (it can be cold when wet), but I wear it as my undershirt layer and have lived to tell the tale. Just make sure to shower or change after your run, so you aren't sitting around in cold, wet clothes.

The jacket is the most important layer. You can grab a sweatshirt in a pinch, but to be really well-equipped for nasty weather, you’ll want a jacket that is windproof and water-resistant. Jackets made for running are lightweight, for layering. Consider ones that are visible at night, with bright colors and reflective areas, and that come with zippered pockets.

Once it’s too cold for shorts, you’ll want to start layering your bottoms, too. Loose pants let cold air in, which is why leggings or “running tights” like these are standard winter wear for all genders. Running tights come in different thicknesses; go for something fleece-lined for your coldest runs, or layer two pairs.

You need to keep your ears warm, but don’t reach for a winter hat right away: an ear warmer or headband can cover your ears while still letting heat escape from the top of your head. And if anything is falling from the sky (say, snow), a baseball cap will keep it out of your eyes.

Fleece or knit hats are great for cold enough days, of course. The dividing line between earwarmer weather and hat weather is a personal one, and depends on whether you’re overheating. If you’re wearing a hat but sweating into your base layers, the hat is probably overkill.

Gloves, however, are essential. I keep a pair of cheap knit gloves in my jacket pockets, but windy or very cold days call for something stronger. You can layer gloves; I find gloves under mittens work well. You can also get windproof insulated gloves, which are warmer than any number of layers of holey knit gloves.

Your face will freeze on windy or very cold days, so this is where your old stash of pandemic face masks really shines. Consider a balaclava or even a fleece-lined bandana. A neck gaiter stays on better than a scarf if your neck is cold. At this point, every inch of you is covered but your eyes. Sunglasses can take care of that.

#4766. Rattlesnake bite venom is based on their personality

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:42 PM EDT

Rattlesnake venom is a complex blend of necrotic toxins designed to quickly disrupt blood coagulation and destroy tissue cells. This effective biological weapon comes at a major premium, however. Snakes must regularly replenish their venom reserves, which means they need to dole out just the right amount with each bite. Injecting too little venom into prey may allow their next meal to escape, while delivering too much can leave a rattlesnake defenseless against its own predators.

But that doesn’t mean rattlesnakes adhere to a one-bite-fits-all policy. According to researchers at California’s Loma Linda University, the amount of venom routinely varies by individual reptile. Their recent findings published in Frontiers in Ethology indicate these dosage ranges aren’t randomly distributed, and that snakes and their venom are much more complicated than many people realize.

“This study gives us a better understanding that reptiles, such as rattlesnakes, are behaviorally complex and have personalities like humans do,” explained Loma Linda University biologist and study co-author Chelsea Martin.

Martin and her colleague, William Hayes, focused on the preferences of the Pacific rattlesnake (Crotalus oreganus helleri). On three occasions over a four-week window, the team offered 48 snakes the chance to sink their fangs into a membrane-covered beaker. Previous experiments mainly focused on the frequency of bites, but Martin and Hayes also measured the amount of injected venom in each instance. They then compared the data across snakes.

“The results were striking in their consistency,” the study’s authors wrote in an accompanying Phys.org rundown.

The team also reported measured behaviors like aggressiveness, bite willingness, and the amount of venom amount were statistically significant and repeatable across many of the snakes. Additionally, their study further debunks the lingering myth that larger snakes actually produce less venom.

“Larger snakes expended more venom than smaller snakes, as expected given their greater capacity for venom storage,” explained the biologists, while noting that actual delivered levels still occasionally varied regardless of size.

The study’s authors emphasize that their findings shouldn’t be held against the rattlesnakes themselves. While often feared, the snakes generally do everything they can to avoid confrontations with larger animals like humans. Bites are almost always the result of an unintentional encounter in the wild rather than an overly aggressive serpent. If anything, it’s people who aren’t paying attention to their surroundings, and not the other way around.

“One practical lesson remains unchanged: No rattlesnake should be approached or handled on the assumption that it will behave predictably,” the authors said. “Consistent individual differences do not make any particular snake safe.”

#4310. "It just seemed arbitrary, and not very smart": EA was never a responsible owner for Bioware, but the developer never seemed to learn from their mistakes

19 years after buying Bioware, Electronic Arts have closed the veteran RPG developer. But as saddening as this is, bringing an end to series such as Dragon Age and Mass Effect, EA have never had an easy relationship with the studio.

Looking through old interviews with developers who worked across Baldur's Gate, Neverwinter Nights, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Dragon Age, Mass Effect, and Anthem, the corporation immediately changed the atmosphere of the studio and never seemed to understand what exactly they had bought, or even why.

Sometimes they could pull an exceptional game out of this mess, but other times it led to costly missteps like Anthem.

Back when I was running TechRadar Gaming, features editor Jeremy Peel worked with Jon Bailes to produce the oral history of Mass Effect. "What I noticed once EA came in was how the language changed at work, and started adopting more corporate-isms, like the language of corporate America," audio design lead Steve Sim recalled. "At one time if you needed help on a project, you'd say, 'I need more guys,' or 'I need more people to help me out with this.' And then it turned into, 'I need more resources.' Doing so, they say, "allows us to focus our teams and resources so we give our biggest opportunities everything they need to succeed."

Also speaking in the Mass Effect oral history, director of technology and Bioware co-founder Trent Oster describes the immediate costcutting EA enacted on taking over the studio. "When we were acquired we were all looking forward to it like, 'Yeah, we're owned by EA, they're going to give us a budget, we finally don't have to keep constraining ourselves and crushing things down and cutting corners.' And one of the first things they did was like, 'OK, based on your budgets, we need to cut across the company 10%.' And we were like, 'Oh, so we're not getting more, we're getting less.' That was a bit of the EA feeling. We call it the EA butter knife. It just seemed arbitrary, and not very smart."

The corporate cost cutting Oster describes took place nearly 20 years ago, but it continued throughout EA's ownership. Most recently, in 2023 when 50 staff were laid off to “shift [Bioware] towards a more agile and more focused studio”. Then in 2025, more staff were laid off, this time it was described as "taking [the] opportunity between full development cycles to reimagine how we work at BioWare".

It becomes particularly clear with Anthem, Bioware's launched-but-quickly-shuttered live-service game, how EA's influence manifested at the studio. Interviewed in the Summer Eternal Anthology, long-time Bioware writer David Gaider explains the design concept for the game seemed to be formed from executive demands. "The nearest I could tell, working in a corporate environment, was that it was a sifted-down list of bullet-point requirements, that no single person ever took responsibility for," Gaider explains. "It just kind of happened like that. There needs to be this project, so it was willed into existence, but nobody would say that before this project is actually kicked off, someone needs to take responsibility for the vision."

Mark Darrah echoes this in his own interview in the anthology. "Casey pitched the exact thing [EA execs] wanted to hear," he says. "Always online, live service, infinite revenue. Multiplayer." Darrah describes it as "a near-perfect pitch deck." But, he also questions whether "Bioware is the company to make a live service looter shooter."

However, while those ideas may have filtered down from EA and then been pitched back to them by Bioware leadership, it meant that work would begin on a project long before it had crystalised into a clear premise. "The idea was that they could kick the project off, and that the designer, the design department, or the creative director, particularly, would then create the vision," Gaider says. "But at that point, the project's already happening. So each time it felt like trying to catch a bronco bull who's already out and running and bucking, and you're just trying to keep up."

For that reason, Gaider claims "Anthem was doomed from the start."

Development on Anthem began in 2012, but when Gaider joined the project 18 months later, it was struggling. "The team on it was just going around in circles," Gaider says. They didn't know what they wanted." Hudson then left Bioware in 2014 and Darrah says "they basically ran with no leadership, for two, three years." In 2017 Hudson returned and Darrah was brought over from the team working on the next Dragon Age project. Over the next two years Anthem took shape before it eventually released in 2019, but, in doing so, it meant the Dragon Age team was also then left without leadership for the next two years.

"My biggest regret," Darrah says, "is I kind of feel like I should have told EA 'no' when they asked me to take over Anthem. I should have just let whatever was going to happen, happen at that point." He adds that leaving "Dragon Age running without an executive producer from 2017 to 2019 was bad. That was a huge mistake."

Darrah says he doesn't feel like he had a choice, however, because "EA was very good at making Bioware feel like things were very dire." Though, he points to the fact that Hudson's replacement as Bioware's general manager, Gary McKay, was able to delay Dragon Age: Veilguard through multiple financial years. "That tells me either he's magic, which is possible, or it was never quite as 'gun to our head' as we thought it was," Darrah says.

(Bear in mind Darrah was being interviwed ahead of Bioware's closure and he's referring to events in the past five years.)

However, while pressure from EA may have significantly impacted the development and direction of Bioware's games, it appears that the studio repeated the same mistake for decades. Gaider, Darrah, and the developers that contributed to the Mass Effect oral history, all say Bioware consistently got deep into projects without having a vision of what they were trying to build.

"When we started, Dragon Age's big premise was 'It's D&D, but it's not'," Gaider says in his Summer Eternal interview. "We'll just file the ID numbers enough to make it our own. We went through a long period of going around in circles on Dragon Age." Edwin's covered this as a push and pull between teams that thought they were making Conan, others thinking it was Lord of the Rings, and still others thinking they were making a new Diablo.

Gaider believes the only reason EA allowed Bioware to complete the original Dragon Age was because the team "were already halfway through development, and [EA] considered it sunk cost. They said, 'Well, you've got to finish this.' But it was clear that Electronic Arts considered Dragon Age, our initial vision, to be too old fashioned, too slow, and too ponderous."

Gaider says Mass Effect had the benefit of Casey Hudson, who was able to pull together a single vision for the project much sooner. Though this may be something of a grass is greener situation, because the developers on Mass Effect describe being on the game for three years before it started coming together and even then it was only because they had to prepare something for E3.

"It was the first time we pulled together playable moments in the engine that really proved to us what the game was capable of," Hudson says in the oral history. "Until you pull things together, it's all sort of theoretical, and you think you know what game you're making. But once you have that moment in time, where everything's pulled together, you know exactly what the game needs to be."

It was only after the vertical slice was completed, lead designer Preston Watamaniuk says, "that the team understood what we were building and was able to then work autonomously, without as much hands-on direction."

This approach continued through the years. In 2012 Bioware began work on Mass Effect: Andromeda. Three years into development they scrapped the plans and tech they were using to procedurally generate worlds switching to a more traditional style of RPG, making the bulk of the game in its final 18 months. In 2015 they started work on a their next Dragon Age project, which was shelved in 2017 in favour of the project that would become Veilguard, but that would then take another seven years to complete, with a live service component first adopted from Anthem and then dropped after Anthem's failure.

It looks to have still been a problem for the studio with its current Mass Effect project. First announced back in 2020, initially as part of the "N7 Day" and then followed up with a teaser trailer at The Game Awards, the new Mass Effect was still described in EA's open letter as in "early-development". Developers who worked on the cancelled game are saying they believe they were "making something special", and aimed build on what they saw coming out of Larian and CD Projekt Red, but six years is a long time to still be in early development and sounds a lot like the project could have fallen into the "going around in circles" Darrah described of Dragon Age and Anthem.

I am saddened by Bioware's closure. The team have put out incredible games over the decades, and it sounds like they were never a natural match for EA's executives or the corporation's goals. However, it also sounds like the studio have continued to face the same challenges on projects year after year without really learning how to avoid them.

Still, it would have been much better that they be allowed to spin off and become independent or be sold to another publisher who knows what the talented team can create, than to be closed down after years of staff layoffs and project cancellations.
