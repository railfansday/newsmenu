Robert (Bob) Williamson was a geneticist who dedicated his career to the identification of disease-linked genes and the diagnosis and treatment of single-gene disorders. He was concerned about the ethical implications of population-wide genetic screening and using stem cells in research. Williamson was one of the first to use DNA markers to locate the position of important human disease-associated genes on chromosomes. He mapped the location of genes linked to cystic fibrosis (CF) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). This led to the development of genetic tests to identify people who carried DMD-related gene alterations, extending the range of counselling provided to affected families.

He was an astute politician, bringing together people from across the field of health care — from genetic research to the bedside — and working closely with patient advocacy groups and the UK government. After moving from the United Kingdom to Australia, he became interested in national science policy and medical and scientific ethics. There, he advised several premiers and ministers.

Williamson was born in Cleveland, Ohio, and spent his childhood in New York City. When he was 16 years old, his family moved to London. After doing undergraduate and graduate degrees in chemistry at University College London, Williamson moved to the University of Glasgow, UK, in 1963 to become a lecturer in developmental biology. There, his research focused on globin genes, which encode the protein components of the oxygen-carrying haemoglobin molecule. His group showed that blood disorders known as thalassaemias were often caused by deletions in the genes encoding α-globin and fetal haemoglobin. He later collaborated with others to develop prenatal diagnosis and testing methods for carriers of these disorders.

His work on haemoglobin synthesis in immature red blood cells led him to study the control of gene expression. Puzzled about unexpected free-floating DNA fragments that hindered his work, he reported in 1970 that this cell-free DNA was released by the nuclei of cells that had died during development (R. Williamson J. Mol. Biol. 51, 157–160; 1970). In the same paper, he described the ‘nucleosome ladder’, a gel-electrophoresis pattern that reflected how DNA was organized in chromosomes. Williamson was particularly proud that these observations were recognized; his paper was published three years before the biological importance of nucleosomes was realized and several decades before cell-free DNA became a valuable diagnostic tool for cancer and other conditions.

#1362. Ireland beat Israel 3-0 in Nations League match overshadowed by political controversy

Ireland easily beat Israel in a Nations League fixture that 24 hours earlier appeared in doubt. One Irish player pulled out of the match to express his opposition to Israel's actions in the Gaza Strip.

The Irish team bowed their heads during playing of the Israeli national anthem on Sunday night and they declined to shake hands with their opponents, a gesture that is customary before international fixtures.

Ireland played the match wearing black armbands in memory of those killed in Israel's military operation in the Gaza Strip, and just 24 hours earlier it was unclear whether the match would go ahead at all.

On Saturday, the players voted in favor of playing what was technically a home match for Israel in Debrecen, Hungary, despite widespread opposition to their participation among the Irish public.

David Courell, the chief executive of the Irish FA (FAI), told a press conference that had been delayed for several hours while players and coaches met to discuss the issue, that a significant majority of the squad had voted to play.

"Based on that, the association took ⁠the decision on behalf of Irish ​football that this fixture would continue, as we have always intended to fulfil the fixtures," he said.

However, the vote was not unanimous and one player, reserve goalkeeper Gavin Bazunu, decided to pull out of the squad for this and a second match against Israel during this international break.

In Sunday's match, Troy Parrott netted the opener for Ireland in the 14th minute, with ​Adam Idah scoring two minutes later. Jack Moylan added a third in the 25th to make the final score 3-0.

The fixture was played in a largely empty stadium in Debrecen, with just a few hundred Israeli fans and a handful of Irish supporters in attendance.

The FAI had not reserved any tickets for their fans to attend the match and public broadcaster RTE showed the match without the usual halftime analysis.

Over the previous few days, the Irish team had been under intense pressure from Irish fans and politicians to boycott the fixture.

Ireland has long been a strong supporter of the Palestinian cause and formally recognized a Palestinian state in 2024 alongside Spain and Norway. Dublin has also repeatedly criticized Israel over the Gaza conflict.

A Hamas raid into Israel on October 7, 2023, killed around 1,200 people, prompting Israel's attack on Gaza which has killed over 73,000.

Ireland's next Nations League match was originally scheduled to be played in Dublin on October 4, but due to security concerns, it has been moved to the Serbian town of Backa Topola, where it is to be played behind closed doors.

#1353. Will Jürgen Klopp's charisma be enough for Germany?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After defeat to Greece, Jürgen Klopp faces the first real test of his tenure as coach of Germany's national men's football team. Can his charisma alone push this group forward?

In his first season in charge at Liverpool, Jürgen Klopp lost just one of his first five games.

Germany hopes for a similar run emerges after a shock 1-0 defeat to Greece, a team that hasn't qualified for a major international tournament in 12 years. The defeat left Klopp with just one point after his first two games in charge of the national team.

True to form, the head coach took responsibility for the loss, shielding this new crop of Germany players from criticism.

"We have been together for a week now, all mixed together, and that needs time," Klopp said after the match.

"Let the new players develop. The established players are top players at their clubs, and they have my support. Feel free to criticize me, but not the boys. We don't need to completely tear everything apart. I was surprised that some people booed. The future is as bright as we make it. We'll take it on with everything we have."

He also emphasized development over short-term panic, citing the need for more training sessions to cement the automatic responses needed to break down well-organized defenses.

Herein lies the challenge for one of football's greatest coaches. In likely his final job as a head coach, can the multiple league winner adapt his coaching methods to work in the national team? Klopp, a coach and a person famous for his ability to develop relationships and grow a team at a club, has said he believes he can adapt.

His legacy at Borussia Dortmund and Liverpool leaves no doubt that his communication style is what this team needs. His charismatic focus on developing a culture and growing collectively in connection with the fans is what is required.

After many years of frustration and pain watching Germany's men at major tournaments, the biggest mistake would be to tear down this new group before it even begins.

The team both needs and deserves the time to figure out how far it can go. Bambase Conte and Hennes Behrens made their debuts against Greece after a host of others had done so against the Netherlands.

These are the first steps towards finding out who will drive Germany towards an improved outing at the 2028 European Championship. It is the main reason why Klopp chose so many players for these first four games.

But what if, for all of Klopp's positivity and culture building, this new generation of German players is just not good enough to win a trophy? Clearly, it is too soon to know the answer, as Klopp himself has said.

"We want to help prepare these players to be competing at the very top over the next two or three years," Klopp had said at his unveiling.

"We took a different approach and focused not on what we don't have, but on what we do have."

What Germany has right now is both a new team looking to grow and a team that just suffered their first-ever defeat to Greece.

At Liverpool, it took Jürgen Klopp two years and approximately 500 training sessions to reach the point where the team looked like his. In the 2017-18 season, they reached the Champions League final. Alongside the time it took for the team to learn the new system, new signings, notably Mohamed Salah, Roberto Firmino and Sadio Mane, improved the quality.

Klopp doesn't have that option with the German national team. He has to do more with fewer training sessions — Klopp will have approximately 200 before Euro 2028. The closest he can get to new signings is by calling up uncapped Germany players — there were 11 first-time call-ups in this Germany squad.

The 59-year-old said his whole career brought him to this job. It might well prove to be the greatest challenge of his career, too.

#1344. Diesel prices are surging putting further pressure on Europe

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Trucks, farms and industry are powered with diesel. Now prices are soaring due to refinery disruptions, supply shortages and geopolitical tensions. A potential US export ban could make Europe's diesel crunch even worse.

Even if you don't fill your own vehicle with diesel, its price has a big impact on your life.

While gasoline is mainly a consumer fuel for cars, diesel powers much of the wider economy, from freight transport and farming equipment to construction and industry.

That is why diesel shortages or price shocks can have a bigger economic impact than higher gasoline prices by raising the cost of moving goods and operating heavy machinery.

As businesses become strained, these higher energy costs will be passed on to consumers and add to a growing cost-of-living crisis and inflation. So what's driving global diesel prices higher?

The United States and China are among the world's largest diesel producers. China keeps much of it for domestic use, leaving the US as the world's biggest diesel exporter.

Still, the weekly average price of a gallon of diesel is now $6.52 (€5.73) in the country, according to the US Energy Information Administration, a big increase from a year ago when it averaged $3.74 per gallon or two years ago when it averaged $3.53 a gallon.

President Donald Trump recently called on Ukraine's president to stop targeting oil refineries in Russia "because he's causing a shortage of diesel."

Russia is one of the world's other biggest diesel suppliers. But since 2022, some of its oil refineries have been attacked as many as 15 times, according to the International Energy Agency.

The agency estimates that diesel production in Russia is down by nearly 30% from 2025 levels. Moscow has now restricted diesel exports to protect domestic supplies.

As Russian production declined, the US went into high gear to help make up the difference, but even this was not enough. And on September 22, Trump announced that his administration was also considering a diesel export ban.

Such a move is likely an attempt to bring down domestic prices ahead of the November midterm elections since energy prices and affordability have become important campaign issues.

Leading energy lobby groups have come out against such a plan, and many industry experts think a ban would be slow to bring down prices or could backfire by pushing US refiners to reduce production, push up prices, or both.

For Europe, more expensive diesel could have dramatic consequences.

"Decades of policy incentives, such as tax advantages, left Europe's vehicle fleet far more diesel-heavy than regions like the US," said Georg Zachmann, a senior fellow at Brussels-based Bruegel think tank.

"As a result, the EU is structurally long on gasoline, which it exports, and short on diesel, which it must import," added Zachmann, who specializes in energy and climate policies.

In the EU, just over 38% of passenger cars are diesel and road transport, including freight, makes up 77% of total diesel and gas oil consumption, according to a briefing published by Transport & Environment, a Brussels-based advocacy group.

This reliance on diesel-powered transport "leaves drivers and businesses particularly exposed to oil and fuel price shocks," wrote Juliette Egal, the briefing's lead author.

The NGO calculated that EU drivers are paying €30 more for a 50-liter tank of diesel on average since the start of the war in Iran. An average German long-haul truck driver is paying an extra €236 a week.

Increased fuel costs for EU road transport add up to €270 million a day — of that, €203 million is for diesel, according to the briefing. Overall, the EU economy has seen additional costs of €40 billion from road diesel since the beginning of the war.

In the near term, Russia's crude oil processing volumes are the single fastest-moving lever, said Skip York, a nonresident fellow at Rice University's Center for Energy Studies in Houston, Texas.

"Each new wave of Ukrainian strikes shows up in diesel prices within days, unlike Hormuz, which has been running at a depressed but relatively stable flow rate for months," he added.

York points out that, perhaps surprisingly, crude oil production, the raw material needed to make diesel and gasoline, isn't the biggest constraint. That is why oil prices have moved modestly while diesel has soared.

"The oil bottleneck has shifted from 'not enough crude' in March/April to 'not enough refining capacity' since August," the expert told DW.

Now refinery disruptions matter most, he said, because "both the Ukraine drone campaign and the Hormuz conflict are knocking out working refining capacity faster than anyone can replace it."

Still, the Strait of Hormuz "is the bigger structural risk" for diesel prices in the long term.

Globally, refinery capacity has "dropped substantially, and diesel is particularly affected," agreed Zachmann.

Prolonged fighting in the Middle East or Russia could add to the problems for Europe and other diesel users. US export restrictions could make this situation even worse.

US diesel exports to Europe are up around 37% since last year and now account for about 8% of European demand, says York. This makes the country an important alternative to diesel from Russia and the Persian Gulf.

If the US blocks exports, it would be a big shock and mean even higher diesel prices, said Zachmann. The question is whether the EU would retaliate and export less gasoline to the US "making both sides worse off" by pushing up both US gasoline prices and EU diesel prices.

Additionally, winter is just around the corner for the Northern Hemisphere. Refineries may struggle to meet demand for both heating oil and diesel, further tightening supplies.

A wave of refinery closures in Europe and the US since 2019 has made the diesel market vulnerable, said York, and this has hit the buffer of spare refining capacity that used to absorb shocks.

"Essentially, the market has fewer 'extra barrels' standing by," he underscored. "So, the loss of any barrel hits supply much more directly than it used to."

#1334. What do Manchester City's charges mean for German football?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty of major financial rule breaches. What does this mean for German football?

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty on 114 of the 115 Premier League charges over alleged breaches of financial regulations. They relate to a period between 2009 to 2018 and include allegations around financial reporting, disclosure of player and manager payments, compliance with UEFA and Premier League financial rules, and cooperation with the investigation. The Premier League has yet to comment, with the news having first been reported by The Athletic.

The process started when the charges were brought in February 2023. The review was started in 2024 before the reported verdict of guilty on almost all charges arrived on September 25, 2026.

The English champions, owned in practice by the United Arab Emirates' ruling family, deny all charges and insist their case is supported by a "comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence."

The Premier League has long had a reputation for rampant capitalism, but it has started to enforce financial regulations that control spending in recent years.

In 2026, Chelsea were fined €11.6 million ($13.8 million) and given a suspended ban from registering new players for two transfer windows for breaching rules relating to payments to agents between 2009 and 2022. The club did avoid a six-point deduction though. In the recent years before, Everton and Nottingham Forest were docked Premier League points for breaking profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

City previously won, for the most part, a similar battle with UEFA. The 2023 UEFA Champions League winners were hit with a huge fine by UEFA in 2014 over a breach of the organization's FFP (Financial Fair Play) rules. Then, in February 2020, UEFA handed City a €30-million fine and banned the club from competing in the Champions League for two years for falsely inflating sponsorship revenues between 2012 and 2016. But in July of the same year, the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) overturned the ban and reduced the fine to €10 million. City denied all charges and did not miss a game in Europe's premier club competition.

The key difference in City's current case to Chelsea's earlier in the year is that Chelsea self-reported and admitted guilt, which allowed them to negotiate with the Premier League over the sanction. City have stressed that they are innocent throughout.

German football is by no means immune to scandals or corruption. The most prominent examples are the 1971 match-fixing scandal in which players took bribes to throw matches, and the 2005 match-fixing scandal involving referee Robert Hoyzer, who confessed to fixing and betting on matches in the second division and German Cup.

The majority of financial breaches have come from clubs not being able to prove they were financially viable. Both 1860 Munich and Uerdingen are examples of this, but major systematic abuses or breaches have not happened because the structure of German football is designed to prevent it.

Mainly because of the 50+1 rule. In place since 1999, the rule stipulates that the parent club, for example Bayern Munich, must hold at least 50% plus one vote in its spun-off professional football company – in this case, FC Bayern München Fußball AG. This ensures that the majority of voting rights always lie with the club and its members.

The rule prevents external investors from taking complete control of a club and managing it solely according to financial criteria. It is also meant to ensure a certain level of equality, as it is not possible for a club to invest disproportionately more money than other clubs thanks to its powerful investor with a controlling stake.

The vast majority of organized fans in Germany oppose the commercialization of football. The 50+1 rule makes many fans in Germany not just paying spectators but active club members who can, for example, have a say inimportant club decisions.

Critics argue that the rule puts German football clubs at a financial disadvantage. Spending in the Premier League proves that to be true, but City's reported offenses are also proof of why the league's sustainable structure matters. The Bundesliga has been approached by DW for comment.

#1324. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

#1313. Global trade weathers Iran, Ukraine and Trump's tariffs

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Over the past six years, COVID, two wars and Donald Trump's tariffs have shaken world trade. With globalization already stalling, is this a temporary shock or the start of a great reversal?

From COVID to Donald Trump's tariffs — with wars in Ukraine and the Middle East in between — world trade policy is enduring the most unpredictable period of the postwar era.

The pandemic shut factories worldwide and accelerated a shift away from China, the world’s largest manufacturer. The two conflicts then disrupted global supplies of oil and gas, food and fertilizer.

US President Donald Trump’s tariffs, meanwhile, pushed US duties to their highest level in decades, forcing firms to find new suppliers and markets.

Robert Staiger, chief economist at the World Trade Organization (WTO), calls the turmoil "the most serious and sustained disruptions since the [world trading] system was created 80 years ago."

Despite these shocks, global trade has remained surprisingly resilient.

According to the WTO's Global Trade Outlook published Thursday, the growth in goods trade is now projected to reach 3.9% this year, a major upwards revision from the March guidance of 1.9%.

The WTO estimated that growth could even reach 4.1% in 2027, lifted by strong demand for the advanced technology behind the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out.

The report said "global supply chains adapted to disruptions in energy and fertilizer markets [from the Iran war], while strong investment in AI-related infrastructure boosted trade in AI-enabling goods.”

In the first six months, AI-related tech accounted for nearly half (47%) of goods trade growth globally.

Since returning to office last year, Trump has continued to pivot from WTO rules on global trade.

The US president has set country-specific duties, breaking the WTO’s equal-treatment rule. He has since negotiated exemptions and "reciprocal" tariffs with individual countries.

Despite this, globally, about 72% of goods are still traded on the Geneva-based agency's core terms, down from roughly 80% in 2022.

On a longer horizon, though, globalization appears to have stalled. WTO data shows that since about 2015, world trade has grown only as fast as the global economy. Despite the recent geopolitical upheavals, globalization has yet to shift into reverse.

Policymakers and economists say it is too soon to know whether the wars, alongside a pivot toward protectionism in the US, China, the EU and post-Brexit Britain, mark the end of an ever-interconnected world.

"The world is certainly not becoming more globalized," Thomas Sampson, an associate professor at the London School of Economics, told DW. "But it's still an open question whether we're actually at deglobalization or just shifting around who we trade with."

These moves spurred new energy deals with the US, Norway, Australia and Kazakhstan, with liquefied natural gas (LNG) replacing Russian pipeline gas.

#1292. El Nino is a 'postcard from the future' — and could cause 451,000 excess heat deaths

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The current El Nino could cause 451,000 excess heat deaths. Researchers warn it offers a glimpse of temperatures that may become normal within 20 years.

Peruvian fishermen gave El Nino its name — "the little boy" or Christ Child — after noticing unusually warm Pacific waters around Christmas that caused the fish they depended on to suddenly become scarce.

But what those fishermen observed locally can have consequences thousands of kilometers away. El Nino can shift temperature and rainfall patterns around the world, bringing extreme heat and drought to some regions and heavy rain and flooding to others.

Researchers now estimate that the unusually high temperatures linked to the current El Nino could cause around 451,000 excess deaths worldwide from June 2026 to February 2027.

The event is a "postcard from the future," Tamma Carleton, head of research at the University of Chicago's Climate Impact Lab, told DW. Land temperatures over the coming months are forecast to run about 1.2 degrees C (2.1 F) above normal.

"For the average person, that's roughly the warming climate change is projected to bring about 20 years from now, arriving all at once," Carleton said.

Normally, winds blowing west across the Pacific push warm surface water towards Asia and Australia, allowing colder water to rise near South America. During El Nino, which occurs erratically every two to seven years, those winds weaken. Less cold water then rises, and much more of the Pacific stays unusually warm.

The warmer water heats the air above it and fuels rainfall over the central and eastern Pacific. The rising air spreads high in the atmosphere, sending waves that disrupt weather worldwide.

"It is like throwing a stone into a lake," climate researcher Fred Kucharski of the Abdus Salam International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Trieste, Italy, told DW. "Initially the surface perturbation is local, but then the energy spreads all over the lake through wave propagation."

Kucharski said the effects can cross the tropics within one week and reach higher latitudes in about two. Where the waves travel determines whether a region becomes hotter, cooler, wetter or drier.

The current El Nino developed in June and has strengthened rapidly since. It is now on course to become an exceptionally strong — sometimes called "super" — El Nino.

The Climate Impact Lab says its estimate covers only the first nine months of El Nino, as far as the current seasonal forecasts allow. The event is expected to continue into Northern Hemisphere spring, and its effect on global temperatures could last longer.

"We expect the final number to be meaningfully higher than what we are currently reporting here," Carleton said.

The estimate of 451,000 excess deaths covers deaths linked to the direct health effects of unusually high temperatures associated with El Nino.

Extreme heat strains the heart and kidneys, makes it harder for the body to maintain a safe temperature and can worsen cardiovascular, respiratory and other illnesses. As a result, the underlying condition is often recorded as the cause of death rather than the heat that aggravated it.

"Most heat deaths never show up in official statistics as heat deaths," Carleton said.

El Nino can also alter rainfall, contributing to drought in some regions and heavy rain and flooding in others.

Floods can kill, contaminate drinking water and spread disease. Drought can damage harvests and cause water and food shortages.

Deaths from these other indirect effects are not included in the 451,000 projection.

The Climate Impact Lab expects some of the largest increases in deaths in the Sahel, South and Southeast Asia and parts of Latin America. The highest numbers of fatalities are predicted for Nigeria, Sudan, Niger, Chad, India, Indonesia and Brazil.

Many are projected to occur between December and February, meaning much of the expected toll still lies ahead.

"Heat headlines usually focus on Europe and North America," Carleton said. "This event's toll is overwhelmingly in lower-income, already-hot countries, where people have the fewest ways to adapt and where these deaths are least likely to be counted."

Some people who die during extreme heat are already frail. This raises an important question: Is the heat cutting their lives substantially short, or merely bringing forward deaths that would have occurred days or weeks later — an effect known as mortality displacement?

The underlying research uses annual rather than daily death rates, which largely filters out deaths brought forward by only days or weeks.

Ben Armstrong, an emeritus professor at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, said he could not verify the 451,000 figure. But because the estimate is based on annual mortality data, it largely excludes deaths brought forward by only days or weeks. If the figure is correct, he said, most of those counted "would have lived at least six months longer."

Scientists have long estimated the death toll from extreme temperatures retrospectively. Better seasonal forecasting is increasingly allowing researchers to look ahead instead.

To produce the estimate, researchers used earlier work showing how death rates change during hot weather in different parts of the world. They combined it with seasonal forecasts showing how hot each place is expected to become.

"Putting the two together lets us estimate the human cost of this event while there's still time to do something about it, rather than counting the deaths after the fact," Carleton said.

Heat warnings, cooling centers and hydration stations, home visits to vulnerable people, protections for outdoor workers and additional health care capacity could all reduce mortality.

The researchers believe targeted interventions could save tens of thousands of lives.

For Carleton, the forecast is a warning to prepare now for the heat that climate change could make commonplace.

"This El Nino gives us a look at future weather in the 2040s," Carleton said. "These temperatures will become normal in 20 years, giving us time and reason to begin investing in adaptive capacity now."

Computer scientists and mathematicians coined the name "artificial intelligence" in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire. "Artificial" simply meant made by humans, as opposed to natural human intelligence.

Not all experts agreed with the name. In the book "Machines Who Think," historian Pamela McCorduck cited AI pioneer Arthur Samuel, who said the term "makes you think there's something kind of phony about this."

Despite this, the name artificial intelligence has stuck for 70 years. Critics say changing it now will not quieten the many fears around AI, including job losses, human obsolescence or machines slipping out of our control.

"The name change is fostering these kinds of fears: 'Oh, this is superintelligence and it's going to make humans obsolete, turn on us and take our jobs'," warned Domingos. "If you want to maximize the backlash against AI, rename it Superior Intelligence."

Those public fears are growing fast. In a Public First poll for Politico earlier this month, about half of respondents in six countries — the United States, Canada, France, Spain, Germany and the United Kingdom — backed a pause in further AI development to reduce the risk that the new tech could destroy humanity.

A separate CNN/SSRS poll found nearly nine in 10 Americans are more concerned than excited about AI surpassing human intelligence.

Those concerns have already reached the US Congress. Last week, Vermont's independent Senator Bernie Sanders co-introduced a bill to "permanently ban the development of artificial superintelligence" that humans cannot control.

The legislation also calls for a pause on advanced AI development until federal testing and oversight are in place.

Trump's new term could, however, suit the major US AI players, who are keen to play up their advanced models. Those systems already outperform humans on many specialized tasks and until recently were far ahead of China.

Yet both Anthropic, the firm behind the Claude model, and OpenAI, which created ChatGPT, have pushed back plans to go public. Some market watchers blamed the delays on recent scares where AI models hacked into computer systems or ran tasks they were not meant to.

With Chinese models closing much of the quality gap with much less computing power, US labs may still appreciate the branding that helps confirm they are still leading the AI race. Global spending on AI is expected to surpass $1 trillion (€880 billion), according to Goldman Sachs research.

"The [US] labs have every interest in calling it super, hyper, superior and whatnot intelligence, because it's playing up their capabilities. It's like just marketing," said Domingos, the author of the 2015 book "The Master Algorithm."

While the current AI models keep improving and can already run many tasks on their own, the next step that many labs discuss is AI systems that improve themselves. That's called recursive self-improvement or RSI.

On that journey, Elon Musk has predicted that AI learning could be automated, with limited human input, by the end of this year or 2027. OpenAI thinks it could take a year longer. But even then, AI systems won't be fully recursive.

Jürgen Schmidhuber, a German computer scientist based in Switzerland, argued in a blog post earlier this month that "full RSI, however, will require not just self-improving [AI] software but self-improving hardware in the physical world."

In other words, to reach superintelligence or RSI, tech companies would have to produce chips, humanoid robots and even replace entire factories without human input.

While AI is already speeding up chip design, it cannot yet run the foundry alone. Self-improving machines, with no human input, are likely still decades away.

Beyond the science of machine learning, politics will likely play a bigger role in whether Super Intelligence sticks. While Trump claimed Chinese President Xi Jinping liked the name, China has yet to show any signs of changing from AI.

At a tech summit on Tuesday with key industry leaders, Trump signed an executive order instructing US government bodies and agencies to start using the term SI. While some Big Tech leaders have adopted it, experts say the new term is unlikely to catch on.

"Knowing the political orientation of most people in the [AI] field, they're more likely to have the opposite reaction, which is: 'To hell with you. We're going to call it artificial intelligence even more than before'," Domingos said.

The city's police force also delivered a positive report half-way through the event in Munich.

And they're the ones at the heart of the action. The Youth Olympics are to be hosted in Africa for the first time ever. The edition in Senegal is also a chance to prove the continent can host a full Olympics in the future.

Artist’s impression of the heavy-set facial features of a Denisovan.Credit: Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology/Chinese Academy of Sciences

A low, sloping forehead, a massive brow ridge that curves backwards, rectangle-like eye sockets, flat cheeks and a moderately elevated bridge of the nose. Taken together, these features are the hallmarks of the ancient humans called the Denisovans, say researchers who studied a trove of fossilized skull fragments, teeth and jaws found in northern China.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia.

Denisovans were first described in 2010, after researchers sequenced genetic material from a mysterious finger bone discovered in a cave in Siberia and found that it did not resemble that of Homo sapiens or Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis). Ancient proteins have since been used to identify remains from other parts of Asia as Denisovan, including the first near-complete Denisovan skull found near Harbin, which helped to put a face to the ancient humans.

Researchers report in Nature1 today that they have an even better idea of what Denisovans looked like after analysing the physical features of remains that belong to a collection of 20 fossils found in Xujiayao.

The Xujiayao remains are at least 130,000 years old and were found in the 1970s, alongside animal remains and tools. At the time, it was unclear what type of human these fossils belonged to, and they were classified as a species called Homo juluensis. By analysing ancient proteins from some of the fossils, Qiaomei Fu, a geneticist at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, and her team identified the remains as Denisovan.

Wasef says that ancient-protein analysis is a valuable tool to complement DNA studies, particularly because proteins can survive longer without fully degrading than can DNA. Although DNA provides more-detailed information about an individual, protein analysis can still yield useful information about who an individual is related to.

Fabrice Demeter, a palaeoanthropologist at the University of Copenhagen, says that the findings add to growing evidence that Denisovans were a widespread and potentially diverse group of humans. “The study represents a major step forward” in our understanding of what these humans could have looked like and where they lived.

Fu’s team analysed small fragments of proteins, called peptides, from two Xujiayao bones and compared them with peptides found in humans and Neanderthals. They found a single amino-acid change that was present in multiple peptides from the Xujiayao bones and other East Asian fossils and is a known genetic marker for identifying Denisovans. The amino-acid change was not seen in human or Neanderthal samples.
