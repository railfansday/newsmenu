#5174. Want a More Affordable Home? Don’t Overlook Condos.

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When you picture buying your first home, chances are you’re imagining a traditional single-family house with its own yard, maybe a garage, and little extra room to grow. But with affordability still tight, getting everything on that list right away may come with a higher price tag than you’re comfortable with right now.

So, if you’re running the numbers and buying a house like that feels like a stretch, there may be another way in. A condo.

Because in a lot of markets, condos come with a significantly lower price tag. And that could make them a smart way to get your foot in the door.

“Condos are the most commonly affordable home type in virtually every metro. At the starter level, the savings can be pretty significant. Nationally, starter condos average $197,468 — that’s about 6% less than the typical starter home.”

And depending on where you live, the difference can be much bigger.

Zillow identified metro areas where typical starter condos are priced significantly below the estimated home price a median-income household could afford. Here are the top 10 according to that research (see chart below).

In Houston, the gap is at its widest. There, a typical starter condo was valued at about $85,600 – 72% below the price considered affordable based on the area’s median household income.

Of course, every market is different. But the bigger point is this: if you’ve only been searching for traditional single-family houses, you may be overlooking some of the more affordable options on the market.

And even if your city didn’t make this list, that’s okay. The trend holds true in many places across the country. Here’s why.

Part of the reason condos can cost less is pretty straightforward. They’re typically smaller. And less square footage can mean a lower purchase price.

So, if you can make do with a bit less space or you’re coming from a smaller apartment now, this can be a great way in. Plus, on the upside, you won’t have to buy a ton of furniture to fill out a bigger house. You can get a potentially lower price up front and spend less once you move in because you’ll need less new stuff to fill that empty space.

And more buyers are making that trade off on space to offset the current affordability challenges. Ali Wolf, Chief Economist for New Home Source, explains:

“In addition to favoring smaller floor plans, more consumers are showing a willingness to live in an attached home. This shift is not driven by a preference for shared walls, but by a pursuit of value.”

That’s really the heart of it. Looking at condos isn’t necessarily about wanting less. It’s about finding another way to make your move happen with the budget you have.

If you’ve been struggling to find a house you like at a price you can comfortably afford, talk to your agent about adding condos to your search.

You may find something in a neighborhood you thought was outside your budget. You could get access to amenities you wouldn’t otherwise have. And you may be able to become a homeowner sooner rather than waiting for the numbers on a single-family house to work.

While there are trade-offs like shared walls, less outdoor space, and an HOA fee to factor into your monthly budget, maybe that’s worth it if you finally get your own set of keys.

Plus, a lot of the times, a condo community comes with great amenities like pools, gyms, and the condo association handles at least some of the maintenance. Which is closer to what you’re used to from your apartment – it could make the transition to homeowner not just more affordable, but that much easier too.

The first home you buy doesn't have to be a single-family house. If affordability has been holding you back, talk to a local agent about expanding your search and see whether a condo could fit the price point you're looking for.

#5307. "Mind-blowing" Alzheimer’s breakthrough: Two hours of sleep restored without clearing brain plaques

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered an unexpected reason why Alzheimer's disease disrupts sleep, and their findings suggest that the problem may be reversible. In experiments involving mice with Alzheimer's-like brain changes, researchers restored more than two hours of sleep per day without removing the amyloid plaques associated with the disease.

The surprising discovery points to an overlooked culprit: the brain's own immune cells. Rather than simply protecting the brain from damage, these cells can trigger inflammation that interferes with healthy sleep.

Researchers at the University of Kentucky found that temporarily eliminating most of these immune cells allowed the mice to sleep longer and spend more time in the deep, restorative stages of sleep. The results suggest a potential new approach to treating one of the most disruptive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease.

The findings, published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, were reported by a team led by Shannon L. Macauley, Ph.D., an associate professor of physiology at the University of Kentucky College of Medicine, and first author Nicholas J. Constantino, Ph.D., a recent doctoral graduate.

Macauley described the discovery as "paradigm-shifting" because it challenges the prevailing understanding of why people with Alzheimer's often experience severe sleep problems.

How the Brain's Immune System Can Disrupt Sleep

To understand what the researchers uncovered, imagine a small fire breaking out in a kitchen. Instead of containing the flames, the building's sprinkler system activates everywhere, flooding the entire house and causing widespread damage.

Something similar may happen in the brain during Alzheimer's disease.

Amyloid plaques are deposits of sticky protein fragments that accumulate between nerve cells. They are one of the characteristic features of Alzheimer's and have long been investigated for their role in the disease.

The brain also contains specialized immune cells known as microglia. These cells normally protect the nervous system by removing debris, responding to injuries, and helping maintain a healthy environment for neurons.

However, when microglia detect amyloid plaques, their defensive response can become excessive. Instead of simply addressing the threat, they release inflammatory signals that may interfere with normal brain activity.

Scientists have traditionally linked sleep problems in Alzheimer's primarily to damaged neurons or the accumulation of amyloid plaques. The new findings suggest that the immune system's reaction to those plaques plays a major role.

"Basically, we showed that it is not the plaques themselves, or solely dysfunctional neurons, that cause sleep loss but actually microglia," said Macauley. "Microglia are immune cells that, when they respond to plaques, kick off this elaborate cascade of inflammation, as if the microglia are partying all night, and keeping the brain awake."

In other words, the brain's response to Alzheimer's pathology may create additional problems beyond those caused by the plaques themselves. The researchers compared this widespread reaction to a "whole house response," in which a defense mechanism ends up causing more disruption than expected.

Tracking Alzheimer's Sleep Changes Inside the Brain

To investigate the connection between microglia and sleep, the scientists studied two groups of mice. One group was genetically predisposed to develop amyloid plaques, while the other consisted of normal "wild-type" mice that experienced typical aging.

The researchers examined the animals at two ages: six months, when plaques first appeared in the genetically modified mice, and 18 months, when the disease model showed more advanced pathology.

Comparing these groups helped the scientists distinguish changes associated with Alzheimer's pathology from those that naturally occur as the brain ages.

The team used several advanced technologies to measure sleep and examine what was happening inside the brain.

Each mouse was fitted with a small device that recorded electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG).

EEG measures the brain's electrical activity, including the patterns and rhythms produced when networks of neurons communicate. These electrical signals change depending on whether the brain is awake, dreaming or experiencing deep sleep.

EMG measures electrical activity in muscles. Combining the two techniques allowed researchers to identify different sleep stages and determine how long the animals remained in each one.

The scientists also wanted to visualize the relationship between amyloid plaques and immune cells throughout the brain.

For this, they used light-sheet microscopy, an imaging technique that makes biological tissue transparent and illuminates it with a thin layer of laser light. The resulting images can be assembled into detailed three-dimensional maps, allowing scientists to examine structures that would otherwise be difficult to see.

This approach revealed the distribution of plaques and microglia across the brain, helping researchers understand where immune cells were responding to Alzheimer's pathology.

Scientists Temporarily Remove 87% of Brain Immune Cells

To determine whether microglia were actually responsible for disrupted sleep, the team conducted an experiment designed to temporarily eliminate most of these cells.

The researchers administered a drug called Pexidartinib (PLX3397), which was originally developed for cancer research. It blocks a cellular signal that microglia depend on for survival.

After the mice received the medication in their food for 14 days, approximately 87% of their microglia had been depleted.

This allowed the scientists to test whether sleep would improve when the cells driving the inflammatory response were largely absent.

The researchers also performed a detailed analysis of the animals' brain activity using a mathematical method called Fitting Oscillations and One Over Frequency (FOOOF).

This technique separates electrical signals into periodic activity (the rhythmic waves we usually think of as brain waves) and aperiodic activity (the background electrical noise).

By examining these different components, the team could identify changes in how the brain was functioning, including whether it remained unusually active during periods when it should have been resting.

The process is somewhat like monitoring a car engine to determine whether it continues running at high speed even when the vehicle is supposed to be idle.

A Surprising Discovery About Alzheimer's Brain Plaques

Macauley called the results "mind-blowing and unexpected."

One of the most surprising findings was that sleep problems did not continue worsening as amyloid plaques accumulated.

The researchers initially expected that increasing plaque buildup would lead to progressively more severe sleep disturbances. Instead, the mice developed substantial sleep problems relatively early, and those problems remained largely unchanged even as plaque levels increased dramatically.

"I expected that as plaque burden became more severe, sleep disruption would also worsen," said Constantino. "The disruptions in sleep and cortical EEG activity that occur at 6 months, when plaques first emerge, did not worsen by 18 months, despite more than double the amount of plaque burden."

The team described this pattern as a ceiling effect. Essentially, sleep disruption appeared to reach a certain level early in the disease process rather than increasing in direct proportion to the amount of amyloid in the brain.

This suggests that the initial inflammatory response triggered by plaques may be particularly important. Once microglia become activated and disrupt sleep, additional plaque accumulation may not necessarily produce an equivalent increase in sleep loss.

The discovery offers an important clue as to why sleep disturbances can emerge relatively early in Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Disrupts a Different Type of Sleep Than Normal Aging

The study also revealed a notable difference between sleep changes caused by normal aging and those associated with Alzheimer's pathology.

Sleep is not a single, uniform state. Throughout the night, the brain moves through several stages that perform different functions.

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is strongly associated with vivid dreaming and plays an important role in processing memories. The researchers found that normal aging primarily reduced this type of sleep.

Alzheimer's pathology, however, had a different effect. It selectively disrupted non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which includes the deepest and most physically restorative stages of sleep.

During NREM sleep, the brain performs essential maintenance functions that support learning, memory, and recovery. Deep sleep is also associated with processes that help clear metabolic waste products from the brain.

"That restorative sleep is super important for physical repair, learning and memory and washing out the toxins of the day," Macauley said.

Losing this important stage of sleep could have consequences beyond daytime fatigue.

Because healthy sleep helps maintain the brain's internal environment, disruptions may interfere with processes that normally remove potentially harmful waste. Researchers suspect this could contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle in which Alzheimer's pathology disturbs sleep, and inadequate sleep may further aggravate the disease.

Breaking that cycle could therefore become an important treatment strategy.

More Than Two Hours of Sleep Restored Without Removing Plaques

The most striking result emerged after the researchers temporarily removed most of the microglia from the brains of mice with Alzheimer's-like pathology.

The animals gained more than two hours of sleep per day.

They also experienced longer periods of restorative NREM sleep, giving them more opportunities to transition naturally into REM sleep, which is important for memory formation.

Even more surprisingly, these improvements occurred without any reduction in amyloid plaque levels.

The plaques were still present, but the mice were sleeping substantially better.

This finding provides evidence that the inflammatory response driven by microglia contributes directly to sleep disruption and that suppressing this response can reverse at least some of the associated sleep problems.

It also suggests that researchers may not need to eliminate amyloid plaques to improve sleep in Alzheimer's disease.

Although these findings are promising, the experiments were conducted in mice, and the same benefits have not yet been demonstrated in people. Temporarily removing most of the brain's immune cells is also not an established or practical treatment for Alzheimer's patients.

The researchers are now interested in whether the beneficial effects can be reproduced by adjusting microglial activity rather than eliminating the cells themselves.

If such an approach eventually proves successful in humans, it could provide a way to improve sleep and potentially reduce some of the additional stress that poor sleep places on the brain.

The work was conducted in Macauley's laboratory in the Department of Physiology at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging.

Macauley credits the achievement to a "beautiful partnership" with students and researchers who were willing to challenge established ideas about Alzheimer's disease.

"I love people who take initiative, find their passion, are curious, and keep pushing to find an answer," Macauley said.

She encourages members of her laboratory to be "calculated risk-takers," emphasizing that meaningful discoveries often require scientists to investigate possibilities that may initially seem unlikely.

A quote from hockey legend Wayne Gretzky hangs in her office: "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."

Constantino, who recently completed his doctorate at the university, said the laboratory's collaborative environment encouraged him to pursue questions that crossed traditional scientific disciplines.

"Dr. Macauley has also taught me to embrace uncertainty and failure as part of the scientific process," Constantino said. "Some of the most interesting studies I have been a part of emerged because our original hypothesis was wrong."

Macauley reinforces this mindset with a simple instruction to her team: "Follow the data, ask better questions, and figure out what is actually happening."

That approach helped the researchers move beyond the traditional emphasis on neurons and amyloid plaques to examine the broader role of the brain's immune system.

Could Existing Medications Help Restore Sleep in Alzheimer's?

The research team is now exploring ways to translate these findings into treatments and diagnostic tools that could eventually benefit people with Alzheimer's disease.

One promising direction involves calming overactive microglia without removing them.

Because microglia perform essential protective functions, eliminating them is not an ideal long-term strategy. Instead, the researchers hope to modify how these immune cells respond to amyloid plaques and regulate inflammation.

The laboratory is investigating existing medications, including the diabetes drug Metformin and the anti-seizure medication Stiripentol.

These drugs are being studied for their potential to alter how microglia produce and use energy, a process known as cellular metabolism. Changing that energy use could potentially reduce excessive immune activation and its harmful effects on sleep.

The hope is to prevent the brain's inflammatory response from becoming so intense that it disrupts normal sleep activity.

Researchers are particularly interested in whether intervention during the earlier stages of Alzheimer's could improve sleep and quality of life before substantial memory loss develops.

"If we can target that process, it might help with quality of life, attention, cognition and confusion," Macauley said.

The proposed drug strategies remain experimental for this purpose, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether they can safely produce similar benefits in humans.

A Simpler Way to Detect Early Alzheimer's Changes

Beyond identifying a potential treatment target, the study uncovered electrical activity patterns that may help distinguish Alzheimer's pathology from normal brain aging.

The researchers believe these patterns could eventually make EEG a useful tool for detecting and monitoring changes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Unlike more expensive imaging procedures, EEG records electrical signals through electrodes placed on the scalp. It is relatively affordable, does not require surgery, and can be repeated to follow changes over time.

The team envisions EEG becoming a "readily accessible, affordable and longitudinal biomarker of Alzheimer's disease."

A biomarker is a measurable biological sign that provides information about a person's health or a particular disease. A longitudinal biomarker can be tracked repeatedly to monitor how a condition develops or changes.

"Portable EEG systems could allow us to monitor people in their home environments and potentially screen for changes associated with an Alzheimer's disease, without the initial need for expensive or invasive tests," Macauley said.

If future human studies confirm the usefulness of these electrical signatures, portable EEG devices might eventually help doctors identify people who need additional testing.

This could be particularly valuable in rural communities across Kentucky, where patients sometimes must travel long distances to reach specialized medical centers.

Instead of requiring every potentially at-risk patient to undergo advanced testing immediately, local clinics might use simpler monitoring tools to identify individuals who would benefit from further evaluation.

Although this application still requires validation, it offers a possible path toward more accessible Alzheimer's screening and monitoring.

Ultimately, the study suggests that treating Alzheimer's may require looking beyond the plaques themselves and examining how the brain responds to them.

By identifying the immune cells responsible for much of the sleep disruption in their mouse models, the University of Kentucky researchers have uncovered a potential way to restore an essential brain function without first removing the underlying amyloid deposits.

The next challenge is determining whether calming these cells can deliver similar benefits in people, potentially improving sleep, cognition and quality of life long before the most severe symptoms of Alzheimer's develop.

#4969. Expert ‘bat wranglers’ put GPS trackers on California’s bats

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 19, 2026 9:00 AM EDT

October is officially Bat Appreciation Month. In the spirit of the season and conservation, a team of biologists in California recently spent time finding bats at Muir Woods National Monument and Samuel P. Taylor State Park. And while Halloween is fast approaching, the activity had nothing to do with spooky festivities and everything to do with tracking hoary (Lasiurus cinereus) and western red bats (Lasiurus blossevillii).

The goal of their survey was to put GPS trackers on bats from the two migratory species. Hoary bats have grey-white fur and are the most widespread bat species in North, Central, and South America. Western red bats, also known as southern red bats, sport a reddish fur and are found in parts of South America. In redwood forests, bats like nesting within holes inside large trees.

“When they migrate, those little guys can cover a LOT of ground!” the Golden Gate National Recreation Area writes, referencing both species. “By learning about these migratory patterns, we can better understand entire ecosystems like Muir Woods.”

Bats are important members of such ecosystems. They are pollinators, they spread seeds, and they help manage insect populations. To catch these helpful agents, scientists set up a sheer, delicate net called a “mist net” over a creek at dusk. All bats are nocturnal, and when they went searching for their next meal—bugs—they flew into the net.

It was a bit of a trick for the winged mammals, but a treat for the human team. “Expert bat wranglers” carefully took them out of the nets, noted their weight and size, presumably attached the GPS tracker, and then returned them to the wild.

The Golden Gate National Recreation Area’s shared pictures of tiny, disgruntled looking bats carefully pinched between gloved fingers. In one image, an expert holds a bat wing up to the light of a colleague’s headlamp. In fact, illuminated wing membrane patterns literally shed light on the bat’s age and developmental stage.

While this survey focused on two bat species, researchers at the Muir Woods National Monument have previously spotted 10 different species.

#5680. A Better “America First”

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

CHARLES KUPCHAN is Professor of International Affairs at Georgetown University and a Senior Fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations. He is the author of Bringing Order to Anarchy: Governing the World to Come.

What Trump Gets Right—and Wrong—About Foreign Policy

Critics from across the political spectrum have disparaged U.S. President Donald Trump’s “America first” brand of statecraft—and for good reason. He has alienated allies, appeased great-power rivals, launched yet another failed war of choice in the Middle East, and blundered into trade wars that have worsened the United States’ affordability crisis.

But as debate heats up about what the next administration’s foreign policy should look like, Democrats and Republicans alike must be careful not to throw the baby out with the bathwater. Trump is right to recognize that U.S. strategy is in need of an overhaul. Most of his instincts are sound and align with fundamental changes in the international system and in American public opinion. His “America first” foreign policy is a symptom, not a cause, of global disarray and the public’s turn against the establishment; many U.S. voters have been disillusioned by the country’s chronic overreach abroad and the affordability crisis at home. Trump successfully tapped into these grievances by promising to scale back international entanglements and bring back factories and manufacturing jobs.

These are worthy goals, but Trump’s policies have badly missed their mark. He has started new wars and mostly failed to end old ones, and his tariffs have fueled inflation while doing little to resuscitate the manufacturing sector. Trump has implemented his course corrections with marked incompetence, making policy by impulse, gutting the diplomatic corps, relying on an inexperienced inner circle, and prizing loyalty over fact.

The challenge ahead is not to reflexively restore what came before; liberal internationalism long served the United States well, but during an era that no longer exists. Instead of burying “America first,” Trump’s successor should salvage its best elements and implement them more capably. The next version of U.S. foreign policy should marry the realist impulses that shape “America first” with the liberal cause that remains at the heart of the American experience. That composite strategy would see Washington pare back its commitments abroad, reach across ideological dividing lines to work with great-power rivals, operate through nimble coalitions, and repair the United States’ broken social contract and ailing democracy. Foreign policy starts at home: only by redressing the socioeconomic discontent that has cleaved the body politic into hostile camps can the country recoup the bipartisan centrism and democratic vitality needed to anchor a purposeful and steady statecraft.

Two tectonic shifts are leading to a historic turning point. Power is rapidly shifting from West to East and North to South, as Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, and other large developing countries rise. The United States and its democratic allies, assuming they manage to stay partners, will remain an influential bloc, but no longer a preponderant one. As the diffusion of power fuels geopolitical rivalries, Americans are growing weary of entanglement in a fragmenting world. Meanwhile, technological advances made possible by the digital age have undermined the social contract of the industrial era and hollowed out the middle class. As broadly shared prosperity has given way to economic insecurity and inequality, political cleavages have emerged along socioeconomic, educational, and rural-urban lines. Liberal democracy is in retreat worldwide, including in the United States, where the centrist, bipartisan compact that long anchored U.S. statecraft has collapsed.

Enter Trump. By the time U.S. forces withdrew from Afghanistan in 2021, more than 70 percent of Americans deemed the two-decade war a failure. Speaking to an electorate fed up with the never-ending conflicts in the Middle East, Trump pledged “no more wars” as he ran for reelection in 2024. He began his second term by using force sparingly. The single-night air campaign against Iran’s nuclear installations in June 2025 and the in-and-out raid to seize Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in January 2026 succeeded in their narrow aims. Perhaps emboldened by these low-cost wins, Trump then proceeded to launch a full-scale war against Iran in February 2026. By planning for a short conflict and getting stuck in a long one, he fell into the very trap that he swore to avoid.

Given that the United States keeps getting mired in errant conflicts, Congress urgently needs to reassert its constitutional obligation to approve acts of war and oversee retrenchment. The bar for engaging in major conflicts outside the core strategic areas of Europe and East Asia should be quite high. It is past time for Washington to return to its traditional geopolitical vocation: playing the role of great-power balancer in order to preserve stability in the strategic heartland. American strategy toward Europe and East Asia has been a steady and cost-effective success, facilitating great-power peace since 1945. The roughly 150,000 U.S. troops still stationed in these core theaters are a wise investment in extending that track record.

A retrenchment from the Middle East and other peripheral areas does not mean a comprehensive retreat, but it does mean relying far more heavily on diplomacy and economic engagement than on the military. When force is required to counter threats to U.S. interests or allies, the United States should generally limit itself to supporting regional partners and relying on special forces and surgical strikes. As it seeks to end its addiction to military force, Washington could take a page from Beijing: China has spread its influence and maximized its leverage in the global South not through armed intervention but through investments, trade, and the Belt and Road Initiative, the biggest infrastructure project in modern history.

As the United States refocuses its attention and resources on maintaining stability in Asia and Europe, it can nonetheless lighten its load by rebalancing its alliances. Trump made headway by pushing allies to shoulder their fair share of the defense burden; NATO members and Asian allies are substantially increasing their defense budgets. But true to form, Trump has marred this success by taking actions that corrode decades of trust, such as threatening to seize Greenland and repeatedly demeaning allied leaders.

Allies are force multipliers; rather than alienating them, the United States should hold them even closer as they beef up their militaries. Washington should work with allies’ defense industries and military planners to come up with a new division of labor, set force levels, and create a realistic timetable to meet their goals. The United States’ network of defense pacts is poised to get stronger as its allies get stronger, but only if rebalancing occurs with consent, foresight, and respectful treatment of partners.

Adjusting U.S. strategy to the onset of multipolarity also entails building on Trump’s recognition that regional spheres of influence are making a comeback. As power diffuses across the international system, Washington will be neither willing nor able to oversee all the world’s main strategic theaters. Instead, major powers in each theater will play a more prominent role in guiding projects of regional integration, shaping regional order, and providing public goods.

For decades, most American policymakers have denounced spheres of influence. In 2021, Secretary of State Antony Blinken called the concept “a recipe for an international system that falls apart.” Yet since the founding era, the United States has prioritized establishing and defending its own sphere of influence in the Western Hemisphere. During the Cold War, Washington granted the Soviet Union a buffer zone in Eastern Europe while entrenching its own zones of control in Western Europe, Northeast Asia, and the Middle East.

Trump recognizes this double standard. The 2025 National Security Strategy endorses a reassertion of U.S. dominance in the Western Hemisphere and calls on other countries to “assume primary responsibility for their regions.” Trump has exhibited long overdue regard for the strategic interests of other great powers and appropriate caution about the extension of U.S. commitments into their backyards. He has precluded the possibility of Ukraine’s entry into NATO, aware that membership would run afoul of Russia’s legitimate security interests and overextend the alliance. He is also looking for ways to reduce tensions in East Asia, pausing arms sales to Taiwan and canceling military exercises with South Korea.

But even if Trump is right to herald a return of spheres of influence, he has acted with self-defeating excess. In the Western Hemisphere, he has been driving away Canada, treating Venezuela like a U.S. colony, strangling Cuba, ordering strikes of questionable legality against boats allegedly running drugs, and renaming the Gulf of Mexico and Lake Ontario. Instead of working toward a benign sphere of influence that attracts neighbors and promotes stability, Trump has been building a malign sphere of coercion that prompts other countries to run for cover.

Trump is making a similar mess in other theaters. Even if he is right to close NATO’s door to Ukraine, he has failed to convince Russia to end its war of aggression. A vision of a stable security order in Europe is nowhere to be found. And although regional integration offers the best bet for creating the stronger Europe that Trump seeks, he is backing far-right parties hostile to the European Union. Trump’s approach to East Asia is similarly muddled. He holds summits with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and hopes to do the same with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, but his administration lacks a coherent strategy for China or northeast Asia more broadly.

The diffusion of power makes a more regionalized world inevitable, and to maintain order in that world, the United States will need to work with China, Russia, and other countries that hold sway in their neighborhoods. Trump’s successors will need to salvage his defective effort to help guide this transition. Washington needs to exercise a restrained and lawful brand of hegemony in the Western Hemisphere, respecting the sovereignty of its neighbors and pursuing mutual gains. That approach will encourage other powers to do the same in their own neighborhoods. To that end, Washington needs to grant Russia and China more breathing room in return for their readiness to exercise restraint within their regions. Moscow and Beijing might well take a less coercive stance toward their neighbors if the United States were to reduce its own military presence in and designs on their rimlands.

It will be particularly important for the United States to work with China, its only peer competitor, to de-escalate tensions without encouraging Chinese expansionism. On one hand, the United States needs to make allowances for growing Chinese power and ambition. A new regional order in Asia will endure only if the reigning hegemon and rising challenger can come to terms on the distribution of power and influence within the order. On the other hand, Washington cannot allow Chinese aggression against its neighbors. The United States needs to walk a fine line between prudent accommodation and dangerous appeasement. The goal in the long term would be to establish a multilateral system of regional governance—anchored by China but accepted by its neighbors.

Trump’s 2025 National Security Strategy embraces what it calls “flexible realism,” a pragmatic foreign policy that aims to establish good relations with all countries “without imposing on them democratic or other social change that differs widely from their traditions and histories.” This readiness to work across ideological lines is an important corrective to the “democracy versus autocracy” framework that guided the Biden administration’s foreign policy. The United States must navigate the world as it is, not as it would like it to be. Trump is right to reach out to autocracies and set aside the zealous promotion of democracy.

Trump’s flexible realism also entails recognizing the need to form bespoke coalitions of the willing to augment the United Nations and other large multilateral institutions. When it comes to preventing or stopping major conflicts, the UN has largely been a bystander. Support for formal multilateralism has also evaporated in Washington; Republicans, in particular, have embraced a hostility toward institutionalized international commitments. Coalitions of the willing are already emerging as the vehicles of choice for resolving conflicts. China, France, Germany, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States negotiated the 2015 nuclear deal with Iran. Oman, Pakistan, and Qatar have led efforts to mediate between Iran and the United States. Meanwhile, a coalition of Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States has been working to end Sudan’s civil war.

Yet even if Trump’s brand of realism marks a needed course correction, its implementation has been deeply flawed. Trump’s outreach to Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin occurs in fits and starts and lacks a clear set of objectives. Washington’s unilateralism and fondness for ad hoc coalitions have also gone too far. The UN system admittedly falls woefully short on managing most security issues, but it plays an important role in humanitarian relief, global health, and climate change. By slashing humanitarian assistance and withdrawing from the World Health Organization and the Paris climate agreement, Trump has hobbled effective multilateral initiatives. And even though his signature coalition of the willing, the Board of Peace, has potential, it was thrown together haphazardly. The board’s first mission—to pacify and bring lawful governance to Gaza—has been an utter failure.

Whoever comes after Trump should continue his pragmatic effort to work with autocracies. But that effort must be guided by a clear set of strategic objectives, promote geopolitical stability, and be anchored by the sustained work of experts, not just a handful of financiers. Experienced U.S. diplomats should thoroughly prepare summits and identify concrete deliverables with their foreign counterparts. Summits between world leaders are critical opportunities to advance the cause of global stability and must be treated as such.

Greater reliance on coalitions of the willing is the right move, but they need to be fit for their purpose. The Board of Peace is certainly not. It has over 25 members, including countries that carry little diplomatic weight, such as Kosovo, Mongolia, and Paraguay. Instead, the board should function as a concert of great powers, both democratic and autocratic, tasked with managing crises and overseeing and building consensus on matters of global governance. Unlike the UN Security Council, which is often paralyzed by veto, this steering group would forgo binding rules and formal charters. It would strive for consensus through the give-and-take of diplomacy, providing a venue for sustained engagement that currently does not exist.

Even as it sets up a great-power steering group to manage core security issues, Washington should resume supporting the UN to advance human welfare. The World Food Program, the World Health Organization, and the Paris climate agreement may not be perfect, but Washington should try to make them more effective, not undermine them. And when the United States defects, others follow. France, Sweden, and the United Kingdom all slashed spending on foreign assistance after the United States gutted its own budget. Washington needs to be a leader, not a laggard, on issues of human welfare. Doing so is not only a matter of altruism; the United States itself will have to grapple with migrants fleeing failing states, pandemics, and the dangers of climate change.

Finally, although the United States should continue to practice ideological pluralism and work with autocracies, that effort must be paired with a revival of democracy at home. Americans have no choice but to learn to live comfortably in a politically diverse world. Yet they should do so while upholding the original version of U.S. exceptionalism, which was to spread republican ideals by serving as a democratic exemplar. Americans should not give up on their messianic mission to bend the arc of history toward more freedom. But instead of seeking to foist democracy on others, they should pursue this mission by refurbishing the American experiment and demonstrating that representative government can outperform the autocratic alternatives.

The antiestablishment wave that Trump rode to power in 2016 and 2024 is animated by discontent with the quality of life at home at least as much as overreach abroad. Much of Trump’s voter base feels left behind by automation and globalization, displaced by immigrants, and sidelined by progressive social and cultural trends. Trump has responded by cracking down on immigrants and attacking “DEI”—diversity, equity, and inclusion. At the center of his domestic agenda is his pledge to rebuild the middle class. As he ran for reelection in 2024, Trump promised that higher tariffs would turn the United States back into a “manufacturing powerhouse,” with plenty of factory jobs to go around.

Trump is right to want to tame inequality, scale back some of the excesses of globalization, and secure supply chains of critical goods. But his protectionism won’t revive the manufacturing sector. Automation, not free trade, is the main cause of the decades-long decline in manufacturing jobs. Despite years of tariffs and industrial policy, manufacturing jobs have continued to disappear. And the rate at which machines replace humans will pick up as firms grow reliant on artificial intelligence. Around 80 percent of the U.S. workforce already works in the service sector, and most will not be headed back to the production line. In the meantime, Trump’s tariffs, combined with the supply chain disruptions caused by the conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East, have significantly raised the cost of living—the main reason that Republicans are heading into the midterms in deep political trouble.

Instead of promising to claw back manufacturing jobs that are gone for good, the next administration should train Americans for the jobs of the future. The public sector must work with the private sector to fashion an education and employment ecosystem fit for the digital age. In the decades ahead, most Americans will work in information technology, data processing, education, finance, health care, travel, hospitality, leisure, and retail. And as AI disrupts labor markets in many different sectors, workers will need transferable skills that will allow them to adapt. Expanding worker retraining programs, investing in the ailing public school system, and improving access to community colleges will do far more than tariffs to rebuild the middle class—and are the first steps in forging a new social contract. A credible plan for getting Americans back on their feet is critical to repairing the nation’s democracy and steadying its statecraft.

Even if the Democrats take back control of one or both chambers in the November midterms—which appears likely—they will struggle to change Trump’s course. He ignores Congress on most issues, but especially on matters of foreign policy, over which the legislative branch has limited power.

Accordingly, Americans have little choice but to ride out the Trump era and try to limit the damage that he does by defending democracy at home. But getting ready for the next administration requires drawing the right lessons from his errant presidency. Trump has begun implementing a needed overhaul of U.S. grand strategy, but he is incapable of turning good instincts into sound policy. It will be up to his successor to better align U.S. grand strategy with a changing world and provide the judgment and competence missing from Trump’s time in charge.

#5676. Europe’s Credibility Gap

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

CLAAS MERTENS is a Research Fellow at the Blavatnik School of Government and Worcester College, University of Oxford.

NGAIRE WOODS is Professor of Global Economic Governance and Founding Dean of the Blavatnik School of Government at the University of Oxford.

Dependence on America Has Left It With Little to Offer Would-Be Partners

The second Trump administration’s National Security Strategy declared that “the days of the United States propping up the entire world order like Atlas are over.” That sentiment has animated much of the administration’s foreign policy over the past two years, and its approach to Europe has been no exception. In 2025, the United States suspended military aid to Ukraine, and in May of this year, it announced it would remove 5,000 troops from Germany. President Donald Trump has threatened to seize Greenland, the territory of a fellow NATO country, and promised to levy tariffs against member states that opposed the effort. When the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February, they neither consulted nor warned European powers, which have been left to face the war’s economic consequences largely alone.

Europe has not stood idly by amid this onslaught. It stepped in to fill the gaps left by the United States’ withdrawal of financial support for Ukraine in 2025. During the Greenland episode, European countries facing tariffs convinced Trump to back down by threatening massive retaliatory trade measures against the United States. And the continent has begun to deepen its other relationships. The EU has set up a $168 billion defense-loan scheme, with Canada as its first non-European member. Earlier this year, it finalized the largest trade agreement in its history, with India, and signed a long-delayed deal with the South American economic bloc Mercosur.

These measures have been encouraging departures from the bureaucratic indecisiveness for which the EU has frequently been criticized. But despite its leaders’ newfound interest in self-sufficiency, Europe remains dependent on the United States. In fact, some of its attempts to stand on its own have made it more dependent on the United States. European defense planning, command, nuclear deterrence, and military enabling systems still rely on American capabilities. Increased defense spending buys American weapons. As of 2025, the United States supplied more than half of the EU’s liquefied natural gas imports. Many of the continent’s most promising technology firms still move to New York or San Francisco.

This continued dependence has in turn weakened Europe’s credibility with other countries seeking new security, technology, and economic ties of their own. For decades, the European offer to these countries relied on the continent’s relationship with Washington. As long as the United States underwrote European security and welcomed Europe’s integration with its economy, that offer was an attractive one. Now that Washington has begun to treat both arrangements as negotiable, however, it no longer is. The weaker the transatlantic relationship becomes, the more Europe needs other countries’ export markets, raw materials, defense industrial capacity, and solidarity against coercion—and the less able it is to convince those countries of its worth as a partner. Europe needs a better offer, grounded in its own capabilities rather than those of its mercurial ally.

European countries have long presented themselves as leaders on issues such as the environment, consumer protection, and high regulatory standards. To neighboring countries, Europe dangled membership in and association with the EU. Beyond the continent, it promised developmental and humanitarian partnerships in the form of aid through the EU and national agencies. Until recently, these were appealing terms: 13 countries have joined the EU since 2004, and more than 140 countries accepted some form of aid from EU or member state programs.

The United States upheld the underlying security and economic order that made these offers possible. EU aspirants could count on Washington to underwrite Europe’s defense through NATO, and and non-European countries could count on a U.S.-led rules-based economic order, within which they could easily access European aid, markets, and investment.

But the U.S. retrenchment has accelerated the emergence of a more bellicose “might is right” disorder in which different needs have risen to the fore. Countries are now seeking security partnerships to shore up their defense capabilities, stable trade relationships to protect against economic warfare, and financing to make up for cuts in aid and increases in the costs of government borrowing.

For example, when Ukraine turned to Europe in the early 2010s, it did so because of the prosperity and modernization that economic integration promised. But Kyiv now needs intelligence and weapons to fend off Russia, which Europe can deliver only through the United States. For countries similarly seeking security assistance and cooperation, dependence on such a fickle partner is a liability.

Partners hit by Trump’s tariffs need access to reliable markets to export goods and services once destined for the United States. But Europe has felt the need to defend its own economies instead of opening up its markets to them. For instance, the European Commission drastically increased tariffs on steel and reduced its quotas for duty-free imports, blocking India, South Korea, and Turkey from selling into the European market—precisely as those countries were searching for alternative markets to the United States.

Other countries need financing for infrastructure and the energy transition. Europe has the firepower to meet these countries’ needs, but its commitments take far too long to be realized as completed projects. And as in security, the U.S. withdrawal has left Europe adrift. Joint G-7 packages to finance clean energy buildouts in Indonesia, South Africa, and Vietnam, for example, suffered when Washington withdrew its support in 2025.

Europe has lately shown some ability to rise to the occasion. In 2025, EU member states’ combined annual military expenditure was approximately $461 billion, less than the United States’ $954 billion, but higher than China’s $336 billion and Russia’s $190 billion. When the United States wound down its aid to Ukraine in 2025, European governments made up most of the shortfall and have since committed to financing Kyiv’s war effort through 2027. The continent also has an existing defense industrial base, two permanent UN Security Council seats, and nuclear forces. Partners just beyond the core add more: Ukraine has a battle-tested army and an innovative drone industry, and Turkey has NATO’s second-largest army and access to the strategically important Black and Mediterranean Seas.

There is similar cause for optimism in trade and finance. The EU’s 2026 trade agreement with India removed nearly all tariffs for both parties, reduced nontariff barriers to trade, and made it easier for Indians and Europeans to temporarily work in each other’s markets. The EU’s Global Gateway investment program, Europe’s answer to China’s Belt and Road Initiative, passed its $335 billion mobilization target ahead of schedule.

These individual successes show that Europe has the potential to present partners with a more compelling offer. Yet much of that potential remains unfulfilled.

Europe has substantial assets across security, trade, and finance, but it lacks the internal cohesion needed to bundle them into a comprehensive package to attract other countries and bolster its own autonomy. When Europe could fall back on the United States’ leadership in institutions such as the World Trade Organization and NATO, internal disunity was less of a problem: American military commanders not only supported but organized European defense, and U.S.-backed rules governing global trade opened foreign markets to European exports, reducing the need to negotiate bilateral agreements. Without that leadership, the continent has been left to overcome its internal fragmentation on its own.

Nowhere is that fragmentation more apparent than on security. European leaders have committed to improving the continent’s defense in response to Washington’s wavering on NATO, but defense budgets, industries, and forces remain primarily national. As governments prioritize negotiating the best deals for domestic firms and other constituencies, conflicts arise: joint projects fail, purchases overlap, redundant R & D efforts waste precious resources. These frustrations have ultimately led the continent to simply purchase more U.S. systems. The EU’s Security Action for Europe loan program aims to reverse this trend by financing member states’ joint purchases of European equipment. But loans alone will neither overcome distributional conflicts nor create common command structures, standards, and logistics.

Europe does negotiate its trade deals with one voice. But many agreements need to be ratified in each country, leaving them at the mercy of domestic lobbies, from farmers to automakers, which seek to protect their markets at the expense of the continent’s collective interest. More than a decade after Canada and the EU negotiated the Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement, for example, just 17 of the organization’s 27 member states have ratified it. The 2026 trade deal with Mercosur, in the works for decades, was immediately contested, politically and legally, by individual member states and their representatives in the European Parliament.

On finance, Europe does not lack money but channels it through a byzantine set of EU instruments, member-state agencies, development banks, and changing flagship initiatives. Partners are left to navigate this morass of funding sources, each with their own procedures, timelines, and conditions. In Ghana, for example, Europe is present through EU programming, member-state development agencies, the European Investment Bank, and Global Gateway initiatives. When the Trump administration’s cuts to the U.S. Agency for International Development hit Accra, a more coordinated Europe might have been able to make its finance more visible, usable, and politically credible.

Many of these challenges predate the U.S. retrenchment. But they were less damaging to Europe’s other relationships as long as Washington upheld an imperfect but generally secure global order. Now that U.S. leadership is no longer part of the European offer, though, they loom much larger for potential partners.

The United States is not the only great power to have weaponized its chokepoints. Beijing and Moscow have also used their dominance over important industries coercively. Europe’s problem is that attempts to reduce the continent’s exposure to Russian and Chinese chokepoints too easily increase its dependence on the United States. Weaning itself off Russian pipeline gas means buying American energy. Procuring rare-earth elements outside China’s supply chains means cooperating with Washington on critical mineral exploration. When, for instance, Beijing weaponized its demand for French cognac by imposing duties on the liquor in October 2024, the industry’s best alternative was the U.S. market—until the Trump administration levied tariffs of its own. A more enticing offer to other partners must be built on economic ties and capabilities that are not beholden to American commitments.

To make such an offer, Europe must first reduce its reliance on U.S. defense commitments and equipment, moving toward becoming a more equal partner within NATO. Although Europe is unlikely to replace the United States as a global security guarantor, it needs to be able to offer third countries viable security partnerships, sustained deterrence in its neighborhood, and the ability to act with coherence when American priorities shift.

It can begin by working faster and more efficiently with its current friends. Since 2022, Poland has bought South Korean tanks, howitzers, aircraft, and rocket artillery; more recent agreements have moved beyond off-the-shelf purchases toward technology transfers that enable production, service, and repair in Poland. Japan has joined with Italy and the United Kingdom to develop a next-generation combat aircraft through the three countries’ collaborative treaty-based Global Combat Air Programme. The EU-India Security and Defense Partnership signed in January 2026 provides a framework for cooperation spanning maritime security, emerging technologies, and the defense industry. Europe should build on this approach, making use of its partners’ strengths to benefit both their and its own security.

European dependence goes well beyond security, however. Europe has the scale, capital, universities, industrial base, and regulatory capacity to be a tech power. But a lack of later-stage financing, fragmented product markets, and shortages of specialized talent and compute have too often driven European entrepreneurs, including the founders of such high-tech leaders as Hugging Face, Arm, and Wise, to start, expand, or list their companies in the United States.

These companies might be persuaded to remain in Europe if they could count on investments and a truly single market. But only a tiny percentage of European pension fund assets are currently invested in venture capital. The EU has announced a slew of initiatives to increase venture capital funding, but it still lacks anything comparable in size to the biggest U.S. funds. The harder tasks are to integrate capital markets, reduce regulatory differences between member states, and dismantle barriers to cross-border investment and commerce. A Europe-wide financial system and consumer market would make investment more attractive, keeping the continent’s most promising innovators from crossing the Atlantic in search of funding and customers. A competitive tech apparatus would give Europe not just control over the leading technologies it produces but also the ability to offer partners a viable alternative to the U.S. innovation ecosystem.

Europe must also reduce its energy and raw-material dependence. As the war in Iran has shown, exposure to volatile fossil fuel markets can harm overreliant economies. The International Energy Agency has noted that renewable energy has partly insulated European economies from truly disastrous fossil fuel price shocks. But the risk of shortages of refined petroleum products, especially diesel and jet fuel, is a daily reminder of the continent’s ongoing reliance on imports over which it has no control.

Integrated energy markets offer a way to prevent such dependence from forcing U-turns on foreign policy. After Russia’s February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Germany, which in 2021 had imported more than half of its gas from Russia, initially opposed an embargo on Russian oil and gas before reversing course in May of that year. When Russian gas subsequently stopped flowing, Berlin was spared shortages and a more severe economic crisis largely thanks to other European countries, which provided emergency supplies. The continent seems to have learned the right lessons from the episode: the European Commission’s 2026 work program, its annual legislative agenda, explicitly commits to building a “genuine Energy Union” through electric grid upgrades and the removal of cross-border bottlenecks. The more resilient its energy system becomes, the more reliable a partner Europe will be.

In raw materials, on the other hand, it has little hope of achieving self-sufficiency. The European Commission’s planned Critical Raw Materials Centre will gather market intelligence, coordinate joint purchasing, and facilitate stockpiling. But demand-side measures alone cannot produce the raw materials the continent needs. Europe could start to build a global network of partner countries by creating the conditions for investment in mines and refineries across Africa, the Americas, and Asia. Because mines, processing plants, and the infrastructure to transport their outputs require long planning horizons and prices are volatile, joint actions could make projects more financially attractive and less risky and would ensure the minerals produced have a market.

Finally, Europe needs the ability to retaliate when China or the United States threatens to choke it. Europe’s response to U.S. tariff threats exposed how difficult it can be to get European powers to agree to retaliate. But it does have a powerful tool at its disposal. In 2023, Europe created its Anti-Coercion Instrument, a bundle of economic countermeasures, including tariffs and restrictions on imports or exports and restrictions on access to EU public procurement, foreign direct investment, and intellectual property rights, that in essence permits European countries to weaponize other countries’ dependence on economic interactions with them. A global ACI, expanded to pool non-European partners’ resources for more powerful joint retaliatory measures, would not solve every problem for Europe and its partners, but it could swiftly convert some of Europe’s strongest assets into a partnership offer with real security benefits.

Europe’s relationship with the United States was founded on shared values, mutual reliance, and reciprocity. It enabled European countries to focus on rebuilding their economies after World War II and on building institutions to avert future wars on the continent. The tumult of the last two years has revealed that Europe took the benefits of that relationship for granted. Today, the continent faces different threats than it did 80 years ago, and it can no longer count on the United States to come to its aid. Rather than mourn the loss of its partner in Washington, Europe must find new ones built on the recognition of a different kind of mutual reliance: that most countries have little hope of resisting great-power coercion if they go it alone.

Building these new relationships to reduce European dependence on the United States would not mean shutting out Washington entirely. Both sides would still benefit from security cooperation through NATO, from continued trade and investment, and from an integrated international financial system. Cooperation with the United States would remain valuable but no longer indispensable. Europe will get better deals from the Trump administration and its successors if it can negotiate from a position of strength, undergirded by more diverse partnerships.

Those partnerships will not emerge overnight. Nor will a new coalition become a unified strategic actor immediately. But an improved European offer will set that coalition on the right path. As with all important endeavors, the hardest part is getting started.

#5315. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."

#4487. Battlefield 6 devs to fix "Secuty" skin typo, but five-pistol outfit was intentional

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Battlefield Studios has responded after fans accused it of using AI to generate cosmetics after spotting a "Secuty" typo on a Battlefield 6 Season 5 skin.

The studio acknowledged "candid feedback" from players in a statement on social media, insisting it had "hear[d] your concerns".

And though the statement did not mention AI - either to admit or deny using the generative tech - BattlefieldComm said that while the 'Secuty' typo will be addressed by a "correction update", an outfit with five pistols is intentional.

"First, let's dive into the 'Secuty' typo patches on the McNeil character," the studio wrote. "A correction update has already been planned for the Season 5 Phase 2 release. However, we recognise that letting a known visual error go live without quick acknowledgement creates confusion and erodes trust. We are reviewing how we communicate known visual bugs so you aren’t left wondering whether something was intentional or overlooked."

The five-pistol-touting Midnight Mirage skin, however, is described as an "intentional creative concept" and "a nod to over-the-top action movie tropes where an operative inexplicably has another backup weapon strapped to every spare inch of gear".

"That said, we understand how this could look unintentional when released alongside other cosmetic anomalies," the team added, and, specifically addressing the typo, said: "We take pride in the craft of our world and our gear, and seeing visual oddities slip through is frustrating for everyone."

Cosmetic Quality & Recent FeedbackOver the past week, we’ve seen conversation, memes, and candid feedback around a few of our latest skins and cosmetic items—specifically the standard issue McNeil skin with “SECUTY” typo.You called it out clearly, and we hear your concerns.…

The message did not say how the typo happened or address fan accusations that generative AI was used.

Season 5 launched in Battlefield 6 this week as planned, and though most of the new additions - new maps, weapons and vehicles, and gameplay changes - were well received, the season began on a low note due to those aforementioned accusations of generative AI use and a major hit to XP gain.

#4495. The PS6's memory and storage alone reportedly cost more than $680

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The PS6's memory and storage reportedly cost more than $680 between them.

That's according to leaker Kepler_L2, who wrote that "RAM + NAND alone is over $680" in a NeoGAF thread in which Kepler_L2 was replying to a forum user who doubted an earlier cost estimate and suggested Sony could subsidise the PS6 to launch at $680.

In June, insider Kepler_L2 - who has previously made claims about hardware that have turned out to be true - claimed the console's bill of materials had climbed to close to $1,000, up from their earlier estimate of around $760.

It follows Sony Interactive Entertainment boss Hideaki Nishino telling investors in June that "as a principle, we do not intend to sell hardware at significant losses".

Kepler_L2 didn't say how they arrived at the figure, and Sony has not, of course, confirmed any PS6 specs or pricing.

We already know that console manufacturing is being heavily impacted by the ongoing component shortage, driven by the proliferation of AI datacenters, the war in Iran, and accusations of price fixing.

It's therefore possible that we'll have to wait a while for Sony's next console - a console that is very likely to ship without support for discs and go all-in on Sony's controversial 'all-digital future'.

Despite plenty of consumer outcry, the company has remained bullish in its commitment to scrapping discs by 2028 (the earliest, at this point, we're realistically going to see the PS6 on the market). It's also likely that Sony is going to push harder into subscriptions and cross-generational games.
