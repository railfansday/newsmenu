#4204. Tenebris Somnia Review – A Hair-Raising And Entertaining Throwback

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Tenebris Somnia channels the old-school appeal of classic point-and-click adventures, survival horror, and full-motion-video (FMV) experiences, then smartly modernizes them to create a creepy, entertaining experience. Surviving the twisted threats of a demonic dream world is a tightly packed adventure that rewards you with what’s essentially an impressive indie horror film peppered throughout. It’s a nightmare you may not want to wake up from.

The blend of live-action and in-game storytelling works well in Tenebris Somina’s adventure, which begins when protagonist Julia visits the apartment of her filmmaker ex-boyfriend, Ivan, after suffering recurring nightmares of him being massacred by monsters. What begins as a well-meaning check-in on his status spirals down an unsettling rabbit hole when Julia’s worst fears are realized and then some. The dialogue-free tale presents a scary but fascinating exploration of obsession, both in art and in romance, that constantly delivers surprising, and at times upsetting, revelations during its eight-hour runtime. While Tenebris Somnia sometimes leans a little too far into “tell, don’t show” territory when explaining the cool lore of its supernatural threats, I never felt lost or confused about the wild things that regularly unfold on screen.

FMV experiences are nothing new, but Tenebris Somnia’s live-action scenes are a highlight. They’re well shot, sport impressively gruesome practical effects, and even present visual clues that make them important to gameplay. These scenes are additive, never taking too much time away from the in-game action, and they feel well placed to elicit the strongest emotional response. I also appreciate how the live scenes fit thematically, as much of Tenebris Somnia’s tale centers on a horror film shot by Ivan and Julia that significantly impacted their former relationship; at times, it’s like watching a movie about the movie. While I enjoy smaller moments, like opening a door to trigger a live-action jump scare, the filmmaking succeeds in selling Julia's emotions and the cast's reactions. Seeing real fear, concern, and determination in Julia’s eyes as she navigates a dreamworld made up of Ivan’s memories helped endear me to what would otherwise be a featureless pixel sprite.

Gameplay may look unimpressive due to its old-school adventure game presentation, but Tenebris Somnia’s spin on familiar survival-horror mechanics hits the right notes. Taking place entirely within a dreamlike version of a four-story apartment building, you’ll traverse floors collecting and combining objects and deciphering clues to solve well-crafted puzzles. I’m impressed by how well the game’s puzzles make the most of its limited graphical presentation, including clever scenarios involving mirror/screen reflections I won’t spoil, and how consistently creative they are. One devious exercise even utilizes the game’s limited save points as an unexpected resource in a way that changes how you approach your adventure going forward. No matter how strange puzzles become, they are never opaque, requiring just enough out-of-the-box thinking to make me feel smart when I connect the dots.

Battling grotesque monsters (which, naturally, look even creepier in live-action) also feels good, with melee and gunplay sporting deliberate animations reminiscent of classic adventure games without feeling mechanically clunky. Taking on multiple enemies by swinging a pipe or blowing them away with a shotgun feels better than it looks, and the most intense encounters end just as they approach the line of becoming overwhelming.

Boss battles, perhaps most unexpectedly for a largely puzzle-focused adventure, are fun and distinct from each other, be it wave-based bouts against small gremlin-like foes, protecting a fragile effigy of yourself from a parade of demented house servants, or battling literal mirror images of a tentacled flesh monster the Resident Evil series would be proud to claim. Managing limited ammo and health strikes a good balance of feeling tense but not oppressive. Playing conservatively is rewarding, as few non-boss threats need to be engaged if you can evade them well enough. But when I did find myself down to my last bullet or on the verge of death, Tenebris Somnia lets you feel the pinch just long enough before dropping a box of bullets or a health vial at the right moment.

Tenebris Somnia mines the enjoyable nostalgia of playing the horror games of yesteryear without feeling dated in the worst way. Weaving a solid indie horror film adds to the experience rather than feeling like a gimmick. The result is a captivating, well-executed survival-horror romp that maintains a hair-raising atmosphere with a fraction of the pixels of other genre giants.

#4802. The Tiny Chair Has Always Had Big Ideas

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Children’s Wishbone Chair is a design icon made for someone too young to know it’s an icon. The fun-size version of the 1949 classic, released by Carl Hansen & Søn in 2024, maintains the signature curved back and Y-shaped support of the original design as well as the chair's meticulous, 100-step production process.

The mini Wishbone is one recent example of adult favorites being remade for a younger (and smaller) customer, a practice that goes back decades but has gained fresh attention in recent years. Ligne Roset’s Mini Togo, introduced in 2007, shrinks Michel Ducaroy’s foam lounge chair for five-to-12-years old. Soon to follow was a child-size version of Mies van der Rohe’s Barcelona chair and Faye Toogood’s Roly-Poly. Paola Lenti premiered a full Kids Collection of downsized sofas, poufs, chairs, and tables at Milan Design Week in 2025. This year, Hamburg’s Museum für Kunst und Gewerbe places these launches within a much longer history. “Mini Furniture: Chairs for Children,” on view through the first of November, gathers more than 130 children’s chairs from two centuries, including high chairs, miniatures of adult classics, and furniture meant to support children’s motor, social, and creative development.

Hamburg’s Museum für Kunst und Gewerbe features an exhibit called “Mini Furniture: Chairs for Children,” on view through the first of November, gathering more than 130 children’s chairs from two centuries.

A chair is one of the first objects that asks a child to arrange their restless body to fit adult architecture. And across two centuries, children’s chairs have been used as a means to carry out competing adult agendas: They encourage the toddler to sit properly, do things independently, or use their imaginations.

By the late 1800s, Thonet had added child-size cane seating and high chairs to its catalog. A 1931 English model in the MK&G exhibition bears the straightforward name Correction Chair. It was used to straighten posture and enforce discipline in homes and schools; a small chair meant to prepare children for the larger, meaner world.

On the other end of the spectrum we have Erich Dieckmann’s 1925 children’s ladder chair, also included at MK&G, which doubles as a set of steps for reaching higher shelves. Montessori philosophy, meanwhile, preaches the virtues of the “prepared environment”—a child-centered space designed to encourage independence. Here, low, lightweight furniture allows children to move objects and begin activities without asking an adult for help. n each case, the furniture changes scale not simply to accommodate a smaller body, but to give the child greater independence.

Designers eventually accounted for the reality that a seated child rarely stays seated for long. Charles and Ray Eames’s molded-plywood Elephant—as seen in this modular home outside Madrid—designed in 1945, could be sat on, ridden, or treated as an animal. The idea that a child’s chair can do more than offer a place to sit continues to shape new designs. When industrial designer Henry Julier developed his children’s chair for USM, the Swiss furniture maker known for its modular system of steel tubes and ball joints, he couldn’t simply shrink an existing design. He had to find child-friendly proportions using those same parts. "At that scale, sitting, climbing, leaning and playing can become pretty interchangeable,” he says. The finished chair behaves more like a stool, while its “back” doubles as a handle that makes it easier for adults to move.

For many of the adults buying them, miniature chairs are design objects as well as children’s furniture. Lora Appleton, founder of children’s design gallery Kinder Modern, has seen interest in collectible children’s furniture grow as design-conscious parents, influencers, actors, and other high-profile families embrace living stylishly with children. In designer Abigail Turin’s Carpinteria kitchen, for example, children’s Panton chairs share the room with Eero Saarinen’s Tulip chairs—child-size furniture absorbed into the family’s design vocabulary rather than confined to a separate children’s world.

"Naturally, the purchasing decision is made by an adult, and many parents appreciate children’s furniture that integrates harmoniously into the overall aesthetic of the home,” says Knud Erik Hansen, CEO and third-generation owner of Carl Hansen & Søn. While children's furniture has "often been viewed as something transitional," to be discarded when the youngster grows, he says, the Children's Wishbone is designed to become an heirloom that can carry memories between generations.

A mini Jens Risom chair sits in front of custom laminate intarsia cabinetry in this San Francisco home by AD100 interiors firm The Archers.

Scaled down versions of adult designs, while beautiful, aren't always practical, Appleton says. Unlike furniture conceived around children’s needs, such as the simple bent-plywood pieces architects historically designed for schools, an adult icon made smaller can cost nearly as much as the original to produce, be outgrown quickly, or lack the stability and proportions needed for a child to use it independently. “Often, it’s just cute, but not functional,” she says. A miniature chair can succeed as an image while failing as a chair, eventually becoming, in Appleton’s experience, a catchall for clothes and miscellaneous bedroom debris.

Appleton evaluates children’s furniture according to safety, size, function, and play-ability: Can the child move it without help from a grownup? Can small fingers get caught? Will it remain useful as dexterity and interests change? She has watched children turn a miniature Panton chair on its side to support a fort, hide behind its curves, and wedge stuffed animals into its crevices. When she tested prototypes with her son, he often crawled beneath them and ignored the expected use entirely. Those detours would often be the inspiration that produced new design ideas.

Every tiny chair begins as an adult proposal for what it should be. It may be meant to teach posture or independence, invite play, cultivate an appreciation of craft, or eventually become an heirloom. The child makes a counterproposal: Turn it sideways and build a fort. The best tiny chairs leave room for that possibility.

#3921. Natalie Wood’s Daughter Says Her Movie Star Mom Was Not “Tragic or Doomed…. She Had a Wonderful Life.”

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

For four decades, the name Natalie Wood has almost always been associated with the same image: a November night, a yacht off the coast of Santa Catalina Island, and dark waters. It’s an unfair way to remember one of the most versatile actors that classic Hollywood ever produced. Before the tragedy, Wood had an extraordinary career and made her own decisions about her life and work. According to her daughter, her mother “held the reins.”

Born in San Francisco in 1938 as Natalia Zakharenko, the daughter of Russian immigrants, she began working in front of a camera when she was just four years old. At nine, she won over America in You Can’t Live on Dreams Alone (1947). She was one of the few child stars who made the transition to adult acting. At 16, she starred in Rebel Without a Cause (1955) alongside James Dean and earned her first Oscar nomination. Two more nods would follow, for Splendor in the Grass (1961) and Love with a Stranger (1963). That’s three nominations in less than a decade, in an industry that rarely let actresses break free from the “pretty girl” role. These are in addition to other films that have stayed part of cinema’s collective memory, such as West Side Story (1961) and Gypsy (1962).

Her personal life was intertwined with Robert Wagner’s. They married in 1957, when she was 19, and divorced in 1962. She later married British producer Richard Gregson, the father of her daughter Natasha, born in 1970. In 1972, Wood and Wagner remarried, and in 1974 their daughter Courtney was born. It was one of Hollywood’s most celebrated second chances: a home always filled with friends, animals, and children, frequented by the likes of Fred Astaire, Bette Davis, Gene Kelly, and Audrey Hepburn. It’s also where Wagner raised Natasha, as if she were his own daughter.

Natalie Wood with her daughters Natasha and Courtney.

It all came to an end on November 29, 1981. Wood, at age 43, drowned off the coast of Santa Catalina during a weekend aboard the family yacht, the Splendour. The official investigation concluded it was an accident. But in 2011, the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Office reopened the case. In 2012, the medical examiner changed the cause of death to “drowning and other undetermined factors,” and in 2018, investigators designated Wagner as a “person of interest.” But he has never been charged with any crime, and none of these reviews have led the justice system to accuse him of anything.

While the courts have not charged Wagner, headlines have debated his involvement for nearly 45 years. Sensationalist books, television specials, true-crime documentaries, podcasts, and talk shows have judged and condemned him without conclusive evidence, without a trial, and without an opportunity to defend himself. Every anniversary, every reopening of the case, and every new statement from a witness from four decades ago has reignited the same insinuation—sold as a revelation—dredging up the family’s pain.

Those who carry the least blame have paid the price. Natasha and Courtney lost their mother when they were 11 and 7 years old, respectively, and they grew up watching her death being written about on magazine covers and seeing the man who raised them—the very same man who supported them through their grief and kept Natalie’s memory alive at home—being singled out. Today Wagner is 96, and Natasha doesn’t want the last thing the world remembers about him to be an accusation that no court ever formally made. “I think it’s reckless and dangerous when people make up lies or assumptions, and then they become the truth that’s passed around,” she says. “It’s just so absurd that somebody as kind and generous and loving as my stepdad has been maligned in this way.”

Natasha Gregson Wagner has chosen another way to tell that story. In 2020, she coproduced the documentary directed by Laurent Bouzereau, Natalie Wood: What Remains Behind, an intimate portrait of her mother as an actor, wife, and mother—not as a victim. And through scent, Natasha has found a more personal way to keep her mother’s memory alive. In 2016, she launched “Natalie,” a perfume inspired by Jungle Gardenia, the fragrance her mother wore. Today, that first creation is at the heart of L’Amour Mère, a perfume line dedicated to the love between grandmothers, mothers, and daughters. It’s complemented by C-Love—inspired by her daughter Clover, Natalie Wood’s granddaughter and one of the faces of the campaign—and Lyublyu, which means “love” in Russian and pays tribute to her grandmother. Gardenia, jasmine, and tuberose: It’s the scent of Natalie Wood, bottled by her daughter.

Vanity Fair España spoke with Natasha about the scent of her childhood home, the stars who visited her, James Dean, and her biological father, as well as her daughter Clover and the letters she’s compiling for a book. Everything except the tragedy.

Vanity Fair España: Your mother was an icon, but you were very young when she died. What is your earliest memory of her?

Gregson Wagner: Well, luckily I have so many memories of my mom, and we were so close that, even though she was only alive for 11 years of my life, I feel like she gave me a lifetime of memories, enough to power me through my own life… One of my first memories was when my mom and my daddy, Gregson, my real father, when they broke up, my mom took me and my Aunt Olga and her best friend, Mark Crowley, on a ship, the SS Rafaelo, to Sardinia.

I love that question. Two things. My mom burned Rigaud candles, the green Cyprès ones made of wax. So that smell, which is like cypress, and then her gardenia perfume, and then roses. We had lots of roses in the garden, and so she put them in the house a lot. Those are the scents that I remember. And food. Oh, my gosh. Well, our house was like a hub. My parents loved to entertain. Gene Kelly lived down the street, and Fred Astaire lived nearby. Sir Lawrence Olivier was also a good friend of theirs. In recent weeks, Google has been automatically connecting Android users' phone numbers to every Google account they are signed into on their devices—without requesting consent to do so. This means that if you are logged into personal, work, and disposable accounts on your Android phone, Google may have silently verified and linked your number with all of them even if you've deliberately kept them separate. If you that's not something you want, there's a way to fix it.

As Android Authority reports, some Android users have recently received confirmation emails from an official Google address stating that their phone number has been verified. Google has required occasional phone number re-verification but appears to have updated its support documentation about a year ago to make this feature opt-out by default. As part of this process, Google also adds your phone number to all Google Accounts that are signed in on the device, including current accounts and phone numbers and any that are added or updated in the future.

If you have an Android device, you can disable automatic phone number verification, which should prevent Google from linking sign-in accounts together. You can find this toggle in your Settings app under Google or Google services and preferences > All Services > Privacy & Security > Phone number verification. (Note that the exact path may differ by device.) You should periodically check to ensure this toggle remains off.

Updating this setting does not disconnect phone numbers and Google accounts that have already been linked. You can find out which are which via your Google Account Personal Info page. Select your profile picture in the upper-right corner to go through each signed-in account and open the Phone section to select and delete any numbers you don't want associated.

Finally, Malwarebytes advises scrolling down to the Usage section on the Phone page to view which services are listed as well as reviewing your Security & sign-in settings. If you added your phone number for two-step verification or to a separate app like Google Pay, deleting it from the Phone page won't remove it entirely from your account, and you'll need to update those settings separately. Automatic phone verification shouldn't be the culprit here, but it's useful to audit what your number is being used for and on which accounts if you want to keep things separate.

#3932. Taika Waititi’s ‘Klara and the Sun’ Doesn’t Understand Kazuo Ishiguro’s Book

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An age when many people are convinced robots will eventually destroy us all probably isn’t the ideal moment for Taika Waititi’s feel-good adaptation of Klara and the Sun, wherein an Artificial Friend (Jenna Ortega) helps her grief-stricken owners realize, “It’s okay to be broken.” True, Ishiguro’s 2021 sci-fi novel was prescient in anticipating a loneliness epidemic that would put us under the thrall of machines programmed to approximate human conversation. But Waititi, who wrote and directed the film, forgoes any attempt to capture the book’s existential depth, offering a manic, lighthearted robot romp instead.

Apparently, the awkward marriage between solemn source material and Waititi’s quirky tone was intentional. When Vanity Fair caught up with Ishiguro last week, he described his own book as “bleak.” For the film, however, he wanted something different: He sought out Waititi’s “kind of wacky, bordering on surreal humor.” The Nobel Prize laureate compared this adaptation, which hits theaters October 23, to an artist covering a song and making it their own. Unfortunately, not all covers are Whitney Houston’s “I Will Always Love You.” To get a sense of how very different these tunes are, read on to learn more about how the novel and film diverge—with commentary from Ishiguro himself.

Ishiguro’s first-person narrative takes place inside the mind of Klara, a keenly observant Artificial Friend, or AF—a robot companion designed for socially isolated teens. In Ishiguro’s dystopia, many children are “lifted,” meaning their genes have been edited for superior traits (though cruelty and selfishness remain). They receive remote learning from experts instead of attending school. This prepares them for the universities that, with one exception, accept only lifted students. Klara serves as a beguiling guide to this dark world, slowly piecing together what motivates humans. As Ishiguro puts it, “Klara’s story is a kind of expedited version of a typical human life. We see her going from virtually a baby to an elderly person…. It’s kind of a distorted mirror.”

Klara appears programmed to lack most negative emotions: She never demonstrates cruelty, jealousy, or anger, though she does seem to experience love, fear (for the well-being of others), gratitude, and happiness. Her perspective—consistently serene and largely without self-interest—lends the novel a restrained and gently contemplative tone. Klara sees people more clearly because her view isn’t filtered through aggression or egotism. But even if it makes her more capable of love, that limitation also makes her less human. Ishiguro says the novel interrogates what we mean when we talk about love: “We tend to put a rather loose term on a whole bunch of emotions, and call it love, as though nothing needs to be said after that about this rather mystical thing.”

In the film, though, viewers don’t have access to Klara’s endlessly curious interiority or the philosophical questions it raises. Instead, Ortega’s Klara conveys the broad, plucky winsomeness of a YA heroine. Eager to please and endowed with the clumsiness of a toddler lacking spatial awareness, she pratfalls her way through the film’s early scenes, tumbling off the store’s window display and later slithering down Josie’s stairs. Those visual gags feel like an odd fit for the material; Ishiguro is known more for low-grade existential sadness than slapstick. That’s not to say his novel lacks whimsy—but it’s subtle and mostly driven by solar-powered Klara secretly making earnest appeals to the sun, i.e., praying. Ortega’s Klara, on the other hand, is made fun of for mawkishly thanking the sun for its “super-duper nourishment.”

Amy Adams and Jenna Ortega, Klara and the Sun, 2026.

Waititi’s film also changes a key element of the novel’s plot, drastically lowering the stakes. In Ishiguro’s novel, parents must decide when their children are young whether to put them through the gene-editing operation that makes them “lifted.” The procedure is dangerous; it can lead to debilitating sickness or even death. Klara learns Josie’s older sister, Sal, was lifted, got sick, then died. Yet even in the aftermath of her terrible grief, Josie’s mother, Chrissie, doubles down and chooses to put Josie through the operation as well. Josie also gets sick, and Klara is presumably purchased as her AF to help care for Josie.

The novel forces readers to wrestle with the uncomfortable fact that Chrissie would rather have two dead children than one who isn’t part of the dominant class. One could read it as an allegory for the current debate over how parents should educate their kids. Yet the novel doesn’t portray Chrissie as a monster, but as a regular mom facing monstrous choices. Ishiguro tells me he’s asking us to confront the nature of love: “Is the mother showing love towards her daughter, or is it something else?” he asks.

Amy Adams’s Chrissie is more sympathetic and less interesting. For one, she’s artsy—an interior designer with a tastefully bohemian home. (In the book’s vaguely outlined bureaucratic world, Chrissie holds some nebulous “high-ranking” position.) Most crucially, she doesn’t make the tortured choice to have Josie gene-edited in the wake of Sal dying from the same procedure—because, in the film, Sal has died of natural causes. That convenient revision shields Adams’s character from the critique of being so status-obsessed that she knowingly risks harming her child. Instead of gambling with her kid’s life, Adams’s Chrissie reveals her steeliness when she attempts to drown Klara, which doesn’t happen in the book. While a shot of Klara vomiting large quantities of water might get an easy laugh, it’s a switch that makes Chrissie much less provocative.

And it’s not just Chrissie who gets a Hollywood flattening. Newcomer Mia Tharia’s Josie is a sickly genius who’s invariably kind to Klara and precociously self-aware in the vein of YA teen heroes everywhere. In Ishiguro’s novel, she’s often kind—but also cruel, dismissive, selfish, insecure, and in denial. The novel’s Josie seems to have an inkling that her mother plans to replace her with a robot in the event of her death, but she never allows herself to fully grapple with that painful truth. In Waititi’s telling, Josie has figured out her mother’s plan. The two of them work through their resentments, with Adams’s character telling her daughter, “I was never trying to replace you; I just didn’t want to lose you.” Which is not only cloying, but confusing: She definitely was trying to replace her!

What we know about the contours of the film’s dystopia we mostly learn from Natasha Lyonne’s Store Manager, who regales her robots with bedtime stories about a pre-WWIII world where something called the internet existed before it got taken away. (There are humanoid androids in this world, but no internet?) But in the novel, we’re drip-fed other information about Ishiguro’s universe. For instance, Josie’s father, Paul (absent from the film), was a brilliant engineer who now lives off the grid in a “community” with other ex-white-collar workers, where they fashion weapons to defend themselves in tribal conflicts. He’s something of a doomsday prepper, but he credibly tells Josie that he’s living more authentically now than in his old life.

If Chrissie represents someone who opts into the ruling system, Paul’s character shows that there are choices even within this world’s oppressive parameters. The book doesn’t weigh in on which path is better; either includes decent people complicit in evil systems. “Most of us lack perspective,” says Ishiguro. “There’s something tragic about it…. Decent people trying really hard to contribute, yet because of this lack of perspective, they might be contributing to something bad.”

The other character in the novel who represents an alternative way of life is Rick, Josie’s best friend and love interest who isn’t “lifted.” Aran Murphy’s Rick gets short shrift in the film, but in the novel, he’s Ishiguro’s most decent (human) character: someone who plays the hand he’s dealt gracefully, teaching himself robotics and caring for his sick mother and Josie. He and Josie formed a “plan” when they were children to spend their lives together, though one senses Josie only holds onto it to keep her despair over her illness at bay. And once she miraculously recovers from her illness, both Klara and Rick fade to the background as she prepares for college. Meanwhile, Rick makes new friends, presumably with other non-lifted kids. Their separation doesn’t feel like Romeo and Juliet so much as the normal, bittersweet way of things: a youthful attachment that gradually dissolves as both kids find their footing in their respective social classes. In Waititi’s film, Rick isn’t given a future outside of Josie; he literally disappears from the film once she recovers.

The film’s last scene shows Klara at a beach that resembles a Florida RV retirement community, where she and other AFs listen to Lyonne’s Store Manager tell them the story of the Little Mermaid. Waititi depicts death as literally a day at the beach. That’s a far cry from the novel’s somber ending: Klara has been dumped at a junkyard where AFs go to wait out their “slow fade.” We’re to understand this is a kinder option for Klara, who could’ve been taken apart for research. Her mind is glitching as she goes over her memories, trying to put them in the right order. Suddenly, she sees Store Manager, who sits down to chat. It seems Manager has an interest in historical technology and has come to the yard to collect souvenirs and look for her old AFs.

Ishiguro’s Klara gives Store Manager a brief report of her life. After some pat reassurances, Store Manager gets up and says goodbye. Then we get the novel’s last lines: “She stopped and turned, and I thought she might look back one last time at me. But she was gazing at the far distance, in the direction of the construction crane on the horizon. Then she continued to walk away.”

It’s a gut punch. For a second, we think we’re getting a cinematic ending: Manager gazing one last time at Klara from a distance. But even though that conclusion would be many times less schmaltzy than the actual film’s ending, Ishiguro won’t give it to us. To the manager, Klara—who’s lived an extraordinary life—is a technological curiosity, no more interesting than a piece of construction equipment. As Ishiguro told us, the book is “bleak.”

#4211. Dispatch's Anniversary Update Brings Commentary Track, Storyboard Mode, And Mobile Port

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Dispatch, a superhero-themed workplace comedy from last year, celebrates its one-year anniversary today. While it was released episodically, the first batch dropped on October 22. It was received well, with strong performances from a star-studded cast (including Breaking Bad star Aaron Paul) and filled a niche left vacant by Telltale Games' absence from the gaming world in recent years. Today, developer AdHoc announced a batch of anniversary content, and some of it drops today.

First, there's a new way to play Dispatch: storyboard mode. This option lets you play through the whole experience using the original animatics – rough placeholder sketches that the artists used to sync images up to the voice-over. It's a fun behind-the-scenes look at the game's development that we don't get very often. In that same vein, the cast and crew recorded DVD-style commentary tracks to play over the game, making jokes and sharing insight into the development process. A press release says there are over 16 hours of commentary from the game's development team (like art director Derek Stratton and creative directors Dennis Lenart and Nick Herman) as well as some of the cast, including Aaron Paul, Laura Bailey, and Erin Yvette.

Storyboard and Commentary modes are included in the game's deluxe edition, which you can upgrade to for $10.99. Both modes are available today.

If you haven't played the game yet, AdHoc has announced a new way to do so; Dispatch is coming to mobile platforms on December 9. It's been rebuilt to support touch controls and vertical play, and it will be available on iOS and Android. You can even play the first half of the game for free – the first four episodes are included, but you'll need to pay $19.99 to play the rest.

Finally, AdHoc announced new merch, including a book created alongside publisher Lost in Cult called Dispatch: Design Works, which is both an art book and a "making-of" book. There's also a live game soundtrack concert in Los Angeles featuring Thot Squad, Bershy, Primer, Caroline Kingsbury, Fartbarf, and Dispatch composer Andrew Arcadi with the SDN Company Band. It will be held at Avalon Hollywood on December 3, but it won't be livestreamed, so you'll need to grab a ticket to see the event. The press release states that people who signed up for updates from adhocstudio.com will be the first in line to purchase tickets.
