#5792. Volkswagen tells ID.4 owners to limit charging to 80% as part of a recall, or it will do it for them

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Volkswagen told certain ID.4 drivers to limit charging to 80% as part of a recall by October 21. Unless drivers opt out, VW will automatically “apply a temporary vehicle setting” remotely.

Earlier this month, Volkswagen issued a recall impacting 22,524 ID.4 electric SUVs model years 2023 to 2026.

In a letter sent to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) on October 6, Volkswagen urged owners to only charge their vehicles to a maximum of 80% battery capacity until a fix is ready.

Although the root cause is still unknown, Volkswagen said that “certain charging profiles combined with a state of charge over 80% may trigger thermal propagation,” or a fire within the battery.

In an email sent to owners with vehicles included in the recall, Volkswagen said that unless they opt out by October 21, it “intends to apply a temporary vehicle setting that limits charging to 80%.” The update will be done remotely and reflected in the mobile app on or about October 27, 2026.

You can accept or decline the change in the myVW app by selecting Accept Setting or Decline Setting by October 21. It will be shown as a 93EX High-Voltage Battery Settings notification.

Several ID.4 owners are already receiving the update. The recall follows a field study as part of an ongoing review of Volkswagen vehicles not included in previous battery recalls.

Volkswagen Group’s Product Safety Committee requested “additional clarifications” from the battery cell supplier, SK Battery America, about production and a potential clean date.

In the US, the company said it had received 22 claims between January 18, 2024, and August 07, 2026.

Volkswagen is still working on a remedy. Letters informing owners of the safety risks are expected to be mailed out on November 27, 2026. Once a fix is available, VW will send additional letters.

Owners can contact Volkswagen’s customer service at 1-800-893-5298 with questions or concerns. VW’s recall number is 93EX.

You can also contact the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration Vehicle Safety Hotline at 1-888-327-4236 or visit NHTSA.gov for more information. The NHTSA campaign number is 26V630.

Volkswagen’s recall extends beyond the US market. A company spokesperson told the German newspaper Handelsblatt that “several thousand” Volkswagen Group EVs are affected, including the ID.3, ID.4, ID.7, ID. Buzz, and the Cupra Born.

#5800. Electrify America’s biggest charging station yet opens in theme park territory

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Electrify America just opened its biggest charging station yet, with 24 DC fast chargers.

The new station is in Orlando, at Sand Lake Corners South shopping center on South John Young Parkway. Each of its 24 chargers can deliver up to 350 kW.

A 24-charger station gives a busy destination like Orlando more room to accommodate both everyday charging and vacation traffic.

The location puts drivers within reach of Interstate 4, Orlando International Airport, Walt Disney World, and Universal Studios. And because it’s in a shopping center, there are places to grab something to eat or run errands while charging.

Florida is a pretty logical place for Electrify America to go bigger. The state had roughly 450,400 registered battery electric vehicles in 2025, according to the US Department of Energy’s Alternative Fuels Data Center. That’s the second-largest total in the US, behind California. Those figures count fully electric vehicles separately from plug-in hybrids.

All 24 chargers at Sand Lake Corners South feature CCS plugs only. Electrify America is also piloting NACS connectors at selected stations, including a separate location at the Florida Mall in Orlando. Drivers can check connector availability in the Electrify America app.

Electrify America says it now operates more than 350 chargers at over 60 stations in Florida. Another Orlando site, with 10 chargers at 10226 Curry Ford Road, is expected to open in the coming months, although the company hasn’t announced a specific opening date.

#2670. Aspect analysis: Leipzig never found a trigger – LC

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

von Next Generation am 20.10.2026 in den Kategorien Uncategorized mit 0 Kommentaren

A 6-1 reads like a game in which everything fell apart. Look at how the goals came about one by one and a different picture emerges: it was not six separate problems but two or three, which Bayern punished in seven variations. No pressure on the man in possession, marking problems in central areas, and a back line that did not work with the movements in front of it – that chain runs through practically every goal conceded.

This aspect analysis therefore takes the goals rather than the game as its material and works back from there to the patterns.

Bayern triggered very high throughout, stayed tight to their opponents and pushed through centrally without hesitation. Vandevoordt was pressed from Bayern’s right, with the clear intention of keeping him away from the left-hand build-up route and forcing the long ball. Leipzig’s front line of Nusa and Brajan Gruda repeatedly angled their runs deep against Nathaniel Brown and Konrad Laimer.

Bayern had one extra central player dropping into midfield in this phase, usually Kimmich. Had Leipzig played short to the right, he could have pressed through onto Raum; in practice he stayed central and pushed through from there. That left Leipzig with the long ball, and it was ineffective: it produced almost no regains, so the enforced forward play brought neither relief nor territory.

Going the other way, Leipzig got no access at all, and the reason was Bayern’s rotations. Kimmich dropped right, Pavlović left into a back three, with Upamecano holding a very wide position. Against this shifting three-man build-up Leipzig never found a trigger, because the assignments reset with every rotation – whoever was supposed to step out of midfield first had to work out who he was picking up. The handover of pressing duties out of central areas failed repeatedly.

In front of that, Bayern staggered their shape so that Leipzig’s compactness became the problem. Olise moved into the ten space at times, Nathaniel Brown operated in the half-lane and ran beyond from there, Luis Díaz stayed wide left. Saibari and Kane pinned several opponents centrally while regularly pulling out of the central lane. Because Leipzig pressed very narrow, this produced exactly what Bayern were looking for: once the opponent was committed near the ball, the switch followed, and on the now open side stood a one-on-one.

That already builds the bridge to the goals. The isolated one-on-ones in which Luis Díaz got his three finishes were not individual moments but the product of this sequence of pinning and switching.

1-0, Luis Díaz (12.) – The first goal already contains the full diagnosis. Kimmich receives centrally without any pressure and can turn out with an open body shape. Ahead of him Saibari and Kane occupy the central zone in front of the box, which forces Orban and Lukeba to step out – so far, the intended part. The full-backs, however, do not push up with that movement and stay too deep, while Raum’s position plays the runner onside. Klostermann stays outside and leaves the inside lane unsecured, and that is exactly where Luis Díaz starts from. Out of that unprotected lane Díaz runs into the back line and Kimmich chips the ball in behind. Not one bad decision, but four players whose decisions did not fit together.

2-0, Aleksandar Pavlović (26.) – Same cause, different route. Kane drops deep and Leipzig do not shift across as a unit – the reactions showed it clearly: arms thrown up, gesturing, hands dropping again, the beginnings of frustration. Kane gets the free body shape, and with him the consequence is predictable: the long ball, high or flat. Because Leipzig are so narrow centrally, the ball flies straight through the entire zone in front of their back line. Luis Díaz moves actively into the passing lane to control it while Klostermann tries to get into his running line and does not make it – Díaz reaches the ball first. From the one-on-one he wins, Díaz dribbles centrally at Orban, which creates a four-against-three on the break. Díaz pins Orban and Lukeba centrally, Pavlović overlaps on the left after holding a high, left-leaning position all evening and threatening the back line. Two touches, left foot, finished away from the defender.

3-0, Michael Olise (34.) – Here Bayern’s press shows itself at its clearest. From a Leipzig throw-in, the man-orientation is fully visible: consistent pushing through, clean handovers. A misplaced pass from Raum then cannot be defended, because neither the tracking nor the duelling is good enough to disturb the pass. Kane can open up half-way. Centrally, Olise comes inside from the wing, and Banzuzi and his partner fail to pick him up, letting him run through – against a player of that variability, an invitation to the key pass. Luis Díaz runs beyond and drags Orban with him, Klostermann is pinned wide by Saibari and can no longer get back into central areas. Olise finishes against the goalkeeper’s movement, across his standing leg into the far corner, catching Vandevoordt completely on the wrong foot.

4-0, Luis Díaz (47.) – After a regain, Bayern switch quickly to the far side and once diagonally through, putting Díaz in a one-on-one again. He holds the ball in the dribble, and Leipzig fail to solve the situation in three steps: the centre is not closed, Orban is too late stepping out, Klostermann does not get into the duel. That Vandevoordt leaves the far corner open is the final piece of an isolated one-on-one that should never have arisen.

5-0, Harry Kane (53.) – Structurally the most interesting goal. Leipzig play a long ball without preparing it and react to the consequences contradictorily: the back line does not push up at all, while the midfield line and both forwards counterpress forwards. The ten space opens up enormously, the gaps between the lines become huge – and Olise is standing in that zone. One pass from Kim is enough to take six Leipzig players out of the game. Olise runs unopposed at the back line and lays it off to Díaz, who is in a one-on-one again; Brown joins for the two-against-one and shifts into the inside lane. Klostermann does not pick up his run from the deep half-space, and centrally Kane again drops into Orban’s blind side – a pattern that stood out across the whole game. The result is a two-against-one against the goalkeeper.

6-0, Luis Díaz (69.) – From a set piece Bayern create a five-against-four out wide. Gnabry pins opponents and makes the space for Díaz, Orban again takes one big step in the block and does not get into the duel. The same scene as the 4-0, only from a different phase of play – a sign that this is a behavioural pattern rather than a snapshot.

6-1, Andrija Maksimović (90.+1) – Leipzig’s goal shows in reverse what their own game had been missing. Bayern drop into a deeper block here, and Leipzig combine centrally before going wide. Gruda gets into a one-on-one against Brown, Upamecano steps across – but from above, with too wide a gap and without closing the centre. That opens the one-two, which Leipzig take immediately. Because the whole situation narrows onto that combination, the space behind it stays unoccupied, and Maksimović can take two touches on his left and finish unopposed.

The sequence of goals reads like a repetition. Five of Bayern’s six come from the same combination of no pressure on the ball centrally and a back line that does not manage the space behind it. Luis Díaz stands in an isolated one-on-one three times, Orban is twice too late with a step that is too big, Kane repeatedly drops into his blind side. Klostermann certainly did not have his best day – but it would be too easy to settle the bill with him. In the decisive scenes he faced situations that functioning protection in front of him should never have allowed to arise in the first place.

Behind it all lies not an individual problem but a structural one: Leipzig pressed narrow and found no trigger against Bayern’s rotating back three. That very compactness made the switch of play the opponent’s most effective tool, and at the end of every switch stood a one-on-one that Leipzig lost. The scoreline is not the story – the story is that the same scene worked six times.

#4298. Ex-Call of Duty designer "who survived the crunch" in order to launch Black Ops 1 & 2 calls out executives "raking it in" from sales of remasters

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Highlights a fundamental flaw in how our industry values its talent."

A former long-time Call of Duty developer has called out Activision executives profiteering from the re-release of games like Black Ops 1 and 2, whereas the developers who contributed "blood, sweat, and tears" to make them gained nothing.

Gavin Goslin worked at Treyarch for more than 15 years as a level artist and level designer, and in a LinkedIn post reacting to low effort re-releases of Black Ops 1 & 2 this year, said: "They are printing money off of work completed over a decade ago.

"As a developer who actually worked on both BO1 and BO2, seeing these charts is bittersweet. I poured my life into those games, spending countless, brutal hours in crunch alongside an incredibly talented team to make sure they were perfect. Seeing our blood, sweat, and tears still dominating the charts in 2026 is amazing, but it's hard to watch the suits rake in giant financial windfalls from our old code while the actual creators don't see a dime of it."

The post was subsequently picked up by various gaming websites - a recirculation Goslin reacted to in a separate post on LinkedIn today. "I am glad this critical conversation is gaining traction," Goslin said.

"To watch executives rake in massive financial windfalls off our old code, while the actual creators face unemployment and don't see a dime of that new compensation, highlights a fundamental flaw in how our industry values its talent," Goslin added. "If legacy games are going to print money for publishers in 2026, legacy rewards and protections need to exist for the developers who survived the crunch to build them."

We have asked Activision for comment about these claims.

The Call of Duty: Black Ops 1 & 2 re-releases, released in July, were reportedly a huge success for Activision and owner Microsoft, selling something like 11 million copies and raking in nearly $450m. This is despite the fact they are not remasters but simple re-releases. US data-tracker Circana not only highlighted Black Ops 1 & 2 as the highest earning game in July, but called it the fifth highest-earning game of the year. It's a remarkable success for simply re-issuing these roughly 15-year-old games.

#4496. The demo for city-flattening fighting game Godzilla: Destroy All Monsters Melee Remastered is a roaring good time, no nostalgia required

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Despite being a lover of the King of the Monsters, I was unfortunately too young to experience Pipeworks Software's Godzilla trilogy of 3D kaiju-fighters back its PS2/Gamecube-era heyday (there was a PS2 in the house as a young child, but I also had no agency to ask for games or money to buy them). As such, I'm not necessarily the intended audience for Godzilla: Destroy All Monsters Melee Remastered, a remake of Pipeworks' first entry back in 2002, but I can gladly tell you thanks to my time with the Steam Next Fest demo that nostalgia is not needed to enjoy this classic made anew.

For readers who were introduced to the Godzilla series via more recent entries such as the Hollywood Monsterverse, 2016's Shin Godzilla or 2023's Godzilla Minus One, DAMM (yes, that's the acronym I'm using) might seem more than a little silly at first. The monsters look more like people in rubber suits than titans that once ruled the earth, and they fly around like ragdolls into the stratosphere when hit particularly hard. This is all very intentional, and beautifully recreates the charm of the Showa (1954-1975) era of Godzilla films, in which the series went from utterly harrowing debut Gojira to the game's namesake, an event movie in which an absolute fuckton of monsters had it out like action figures being slammed together. I really can't overstate how well this game captures the vibe of the Showa era's blend of silliness and seriousness.

As for the 1v1 monstering experience of the remake itself, it's largely unchanged from the original game. The demo offers a singleplayer 'Destruction' mode which lets you pick a monster of your choosing to absolutely wreck an unsuspecting city — the demo would only let me choose Seattle as a stage, which I found kind of hilarious; that poor Space Needle — and gain a high score for causing as much mayhem as possible. The star of the show, though, is Versus, an arena mode that can be played locally, against CPU opponents, or online. The controls take a moment to get used to, with each monster feeling a bit sluggish (unsure how intentional this is) but handling well after some time understanding that you're just going to keep pressing block by accident, forever. There's also pickups across the stage to give you or your opponent little boosts, including one displaying the Atari symbol that summons Mothra for an air strike (sure!). The online function seems to work decently well, playing out just as an offline versus match would with only the occasional added jitter (though, admittedly, no connection indicator that I could see).

One thing I do find a tad odd about this demo is its choice of playable roster. Obviously, it's going to be very limited, so there's just three monsters to choose from: Godzilla, King Ghidorah and Gigan. The first two are a given, but Gigan confuses me - while definitely a popular monster, one of the remake's selling points is the addition of the Showa-era Mechagodzilla, which previously didn't appear in the series until the later Godzilla Unleashed. For a demo with a limited cast, why not let people play as the one monster completely new to the game?

All that is to say, I really do recommend this demo whether you played the original or not. And if you like the vibe of kaiju smacking each other around, might I also recommend watching a few of the Showa era films? Start with the original and go in release order, of course. It's fun to watch in real time how Toho takes itself less and less seriously.

#5808. I bought a $199 Walmart e-bike, and what arrived shocked me

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

I’ve tested e-bikes that cost $10,000. I’ve tested e-bikes that cost $500. But until recently, I’d never tested a brand-new e-bike that cost less than a nice set of bicycle tires.

So when I stumbled across a $199 e-bike for sale through Walmart’s website, my curiosity got the better of me.

To be clear, I wasn’t expecting it to be good. I wasn’t even really expecting it to be acceptable. This was more of a morbid curiosity experiment: What exactly does $199 buy you in the world of e-bikes?

Apparently, the answer is: more than I expected, but also fewer pedals than I expected.

The bike in question is from a brand called “Bropath”, and it’s a tiny folding e-bike with 14-inch wheels, a 350/500W rear hub motor (disparity based on which marketing blurb you read), and a surprisingly large 576Wh battery.

On paper, that’s actually fairly impressive, especially for the rock-bottom price. The bike claims a top speed of 20 mph (32 km/h) and around 20 miles (32 km) of range. There’s a throttle, pedal assist, a suspension fork, mechanical disc brakes, a rear rack/basket that also houses the battery, and even a folding frame.

Things started getting interesting as soon as I pulled it out of the box, which you’ll definitely want to check out in my unboxing and testing video.

The bike was mostly assembled, but the battery basket wasn’t actually attached to the rack. There were holes where bolts clearly should have gone, but no bolts. I checked the box. I checked the packaging. I checked the instructions.

Eventually I resorted to temporarily ratchet-strapping the entire battery basket to the bike, which is not generally a step included in professional bicycle assembly.

Then, just as I thought I was ready for a test ride, I noticed another small problem: the bike didn’t have pedals.

Not “the pedals weren’t installed.” There simply weren’t any pedals in the box.

My guess is that an accessory box containing the pedals and battery mounting hardware was forgotten during packaging, or perhaps I received a re-packaged bike and they were left out. Fortunately, I live, eat, and breathe bikes, so my garage contains enough miscellaneous bicycle parts to make a small bike shop jealous. I eventually found two long bolts that fit the battery basket and a spare pair of pedals.

Before that, though, I did what any reasonable person would do: I rode it like a scooter with my feet resting on the bare cranks.

And that’s where things got surprising: the darn thing actually worked.

The little e-bike pulled away under throttle and moderately quickly got me cruising at nearly 20 mph (32 km/h). The brakes, which look like they were manufactured from approximately the same grade of metal as a soup can, actually stopped the bike reasonably well. The motor had decent power, the throttle worked, and once I installed pedals, the pedal assist worked too. On that note though: the single-speed design and small 14″ wheels meant that once the pedal assist kicked in, you’d quickly be going faster than your feet could keep up on the pedals. With that ghost pedaling in effect, the pedal assist quickly became useless, so don’t expect to ride this as a pedal-assist bike.

To test out the cargo capacity of the little battery basket, I took it on a shopping trip and managed to haul home several bags of groceries. I wouldn’t load it down with several cases of beer, but light duty grocery runs seems possible.

Is it a good e-bike? I wouldn’t go that far. But for $199, it was shockingly adequate. And I mean that as a compliment.

Yes, there are plenty of compromises. The suspension fork feels like a wet noodle, to the point that I’d probably rather have a rigid fork. The tiny 14-inch wheels combined with the cheap suspension make rough pavement and gravel paths fairly unpleasant. The seatpost is so short that even I can’t get a particularly good pedaling position, and I’m not exactly an NBA center.

And like I mentioned, the single-speed drivetrain also means that once the pedal assist gets you moving quickly, your legs can’t really keep up with the gearing. You wind up ghost pedaling while the motor does most of the work.

I don’t know what cells are inside that 576Wh battery, and I certainly wouldn’t assume the battery has the sort of third-party safety certification I’d prefer to see on an e-bike. I don’t know how many years – or months – the suspension fork will last. I don’t know how the folding mechanism will hold up after hundreds of cycles, because it definitely doesn’t look robust.

And if something does break, you’re probably largely on your own. At this price, you’re not paying for a nationwide dealer network or a robust customer service department. This is basically a white-labeled budget e-bike, and part of the $199 experience is accepting that you will likely become your own mechanic.

That’s why I wouldn’t recommend this bike to most people.

If you can afford to spend more on a quality e-bike from an established company with better components, a certified battery, replacement parts, and real customer support, I think you should. When people ask me what the most affordable e-bike is that is still good quality, I generally send them to a $799 Lectric XP Lite-series e-bike. By the time you start upgrading parts on a bike like this $199 Walmart bike, adding likes better fork, brakes, drivetrain, or other components, you could have simply bought a better e-bike in the first place.

For someone who genuinely only has $200 to spend and needs transportation, this thing is transportation.

It got me to the store. It got me to lunch. It carried groceries. It cruised at nearly 20 mph. Once I supplied the missing parts myself, everything basically worked (and I assume most people will be luckier than me and get all the parts in the box).

Good enough is better then nothing. It fills a gap. Most of China is riding around on sub $300 ebikes. Those folding bikes are getting very cheap I've seen them for around $50 fob. It's amazing they have brought the cost down for folding bikes so much 15 years ago they were several times more expensive then a regular bike. Now a folding ebike for less then the fancy brands and stores were trying to sell a folding bike. As long as a it last a few years and doesn't blow up it's a win for personal transportation.

I went into this experiment expecting a $199 e-bike to be almost comically terrible. Instead, I found something that was undeniably cheap, questionable in several places, and certainly not something I’d choose as my everyday e-bike – yet was also far more functional than it had any right to be at this price.

There’s a big difference between “good” and “good for $199.”

But somehow, against all odds, it might actually be the latter.

#4504. "It doesn’t have to always be triple-A": Galactic Racer and Zero Company’s success has Lucasfilms wanting more smaller-scale Star Wars games

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Star Wars: Galactic Racer has been fucking everywhere recently, seemingly both due to its actual quality as a game and also due to vast swathes of people falling in love with little mole guy Biddy Blas. The success of Fuse Games' Galactic Racer follows on from the similarly warm reception of Bit Reactor's XCOM-esque Star Wars Zero Company (though that success didn't prevent staff furloughs, which are at least starting to be undone in some cases), making it clear that there is a market for Star Wars games outside of just the blockbusters like the Battlefront series. The vice president of Lucasfilm Games, Douglas Reilly, seems to agree, stating in an interview with The Game Business that "Good ideas can come from a lot of different places. You don't have to necessarily be a Top Five publisher to make a great Star Wars game."

Reilly's words do come loaded with the context of what the Star Wars series has been in the games space for many years. Disney's deal with EA, for it to be the priority publisher for Star Wars games, produced DICE's Battlefront revival and the Star Wars Jedi series from Respawn, but did result in the Star Wars franchise being kept primarily to guaranteed moneymaker concepts, like the live service shooter or the third-person singleplayer action game. Star Wars Outlaws was a step outside of this, but Ubisoft's attempt didn't quite manage to be the shining star it had hoped for.

Zero Company and Galactic Racer, in contrast, are strategy/tactics and racing games respectively, which while not necessarily harder to sell, are definitely a little more niche. Despite this, they were supported (again, asterisk attached to Bit Reactor there) by Lucasfilm Games and ended up as some of the most well-received Star Wars games in recent memory. Reilly notes that "The goal has been to find the best partner who makes that experience come alive inside the galaxy far, far away", which does describe both studios' attempts pretty well.

It's worth noting that Bit Reactor is made up of ex-XCOM staff, and Fuse Games was originally founded by Criterion staff who'd had previous experience with Lucasfilm from working on the modern Battlefront series. Which adds some weight to Reilly's comment that "We have a high quality bar, and that means typically we work with more experienced teams than ones with no track record. But we’re exploring all kinds of things, large and small, with partners that are going to surprise folks."

I'm not a Star Wars girl myself, but in the general interest of less safe art, I hope this leads to some real weird swings, like a management sim making the clones on Kamino, or a dating sim featuring only the non-humanoid aliens (give us freaks something, please). In the non-hypothetical future, there's still Fate of the Old Republic to come, alongside another Jedi game from Respawn and, at some point before the sun explodes, the 'paused' Knights of the Old Republic remake. #3937. How to Lock Your Phone Number Against eSIM Hijacking on Verizon, T-Mobile, and AT&T

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your phone number is the skeleton key to your digital life, and carriers have spent years handing out copies to anyone with a decent script. Resetting a bank password, recovering an email account, approving a crypto withdrawal: All of it can run through a text message sent to a number that a call-center rep can reassign in minutes.

The good news is that all three major U.S. carriers now let you lock your number with a toggle. Here's how the attacks work and where to find each switch.

A SIM swap starts with research. Attackers collect your name, address, and account details from data breaches or phishing, then contact your carrier pretending to be you. If they convince someone to move your number onto a SIM they control, your calls, texts, and two-factor codes go to them instead. A close cousin is port-out fraud, where the attacker opens an account with a different carrier and arranges for your number to be transferred there.

The scale is hard to pin down, but the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged 982 SIM swap complaints and about $26 million in losses in 2024, down from 2,026 complaints and $72.7 million in 2022. That decline looks encouraging, but the figures only include crimes that victims bothered to report to one federal portal.

eSIMs removed the plastic card, not the weak link. Researchers at the Russian firm F.A.C.C.T. found that attackers now break into a victim's carrier account with stolen or leaked credentials, generate a QR code, and scan it on their own device. Your legitimate SIM is deactivated at the same moment. There's no store visit and no phone call to a rep.

Intel 471 also flagged a provider that let the activation code go to email instead of SMS, which gave attackers a second inbox to compromise. That provider has since closed the loophole, but the lesson stands: Every shortcut for locked-out customers is also a shortcut for criminals.

Carriers now offer three overlapping defenses. A SIM lock blocks SIM or eSIM changes and device upgrades; a port-out lock stops your number from moving to another carrier; and a PIN adds a password check when you contact customer service or request a transfer.

I'd treat the PIN as the weakest of the three. PINs aren’t bulletproof because company reps can still override them. A lock that grays out the option entirely is much harder to talk your way around.

In its 2023 order, the FCC instructed wireless providers to offer customers a free account lock and to notify them of SIM change and port-out requests. Compliance deadlines were pushed back until federal paperwork review finishes, though, so don't assume every carrier has implemented these features fully.

AT&T bundles everything into a single toggle called Wireless Account Lock. According to AT&T's support page, it blocks device upgrades, SIM and eSIM swaps, IMEI changes, number changes, transfers to another carrier, and edits to authorized users and billing info. You can still pay your bill, change plans, and unlock a device.

Open the AT&T app on a device that's active on your wireless account, and sign in.

Prepaid customers follow a different path: Sign in, choose your number, tap Profile & Settings, then Account Info & Preferences, and select Turn on under Wireless Account Lock. AT&T texts a one-time code to confirm.

Only users with primary or secondary access can flip the switch, and AT&T emails the account holder and texts every active number whenever the lock changes. I like that. A silent change is the whole point of these attacks, so noisy notifications are half the defense.

Verizon splits the job in two. SIM Protection blocks unauthorized SIM and device changes, while Number Lock blocks your number from being transferred to another carrier. Verizon's own documentation notes that Number Lock alone doesn't prevent SIM card changes, so you want both switched on.

Tap the Me tab, then Edit profile and settings.

Find SIM Protection and toggle it on for each line you want covered.

After you disable SIM Protection, SIM changes and upgrade orders stay blocked for another 15 minutes. And if you ever port out, Number Lock must be off before you can generate a Number Transfer PIN. Two toggles is slightly more homework than AT&T's single switch, but it lets you lock the number while leaving device upgrades open, which is handy if you swap phones often.

T-Mobile also uses two features. SIM Protection blocks SIM and eSIM changes, and Port Out Protection blocks transfers to other carriers. Both are free.

To enable SIM Protection on a postpaid account in the T-Life app:

Toggle protection for the whole account or for individual lines.

Port Out Protection works differently since it's an add-on you apply to each line separately. In T-Life, go to Manage, then See Plans, then Manage add-ons, and check the box next to Port Out Protection. Repeat for every line on the account.

On T-Mobile's side, authorized users can add either feature, but only the Primary Account Holder can remove them. The per-line setup is tedious on a big family plan, though it's worth the ten minutes. T-Mobile also recommends setting an account PIN of 6 to 15 digits, which you can only change from the primary holder's phone while on T-Mobile's network with wifi off.

A lock protects against changes made through your carrier—it doesn't protect against someone who already has your carrier login, who might be able to switch it off.

AT&T at least requires its app on a device active on your account, which raises the bar. Still, researchers recommend unique passwords and two-factor authentication on the carrier account too.

Remember to unlock before you upgrade your phone or move an eSIM, because every carrier will block you otherwise. Flip it back on afterward.

The bigger fix is to stop letting your phone number guard anything valuable. Switch your bank, email, and crypto accounts to an authenticator app or a passkey where you can. Think of the carrier lock as a seatbelt: You'll rarely need it, and you'll be glad it was buckled the one time you do.
