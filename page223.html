#4082. "Why the f**k would they add a perk like that?" - Modern Warfare 4 DMZ players are up in arms over the ability to self-revive

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Modern Warfare 4's DMZ extraction shooter mode has only been out for two days - and only for those who pre-ordered the game - but already players are up in arms over a particular trait that allows competitors to revive themselves after being killed.

That trait is called Possum, and is one of the many abilities players can unlock as they level up their operators. How it works is this: if the trait is active, you're given the option, once per match, to revive yourself on command when killed. Doing so places you in a Last Stand state, and you'll need to pick yourself up from this vulnerable downed position to continue playing. All together, getting back into the firefight from dead takes around 10 uninterrupted seconds. You also lose any loot you've gathered when you revive - so you're back to square one.

It goes without saying that the ability to come back to life at all in an extraction shooter is a bold addition to DMZ. There doesn't seem to be a great way to see if a dead opponent has it, meaning every player you kill could come back for revenge later in the match when your guard is down. It doesn't take long to unlock either! If you focus your operator progression solely on the Warfighter path, Possum can be unlocked in a handful of hours. As such, it's likely someone in a DMZ match will be ready to pounce. And to say Possum is proving divisive among Call of Duty players would be an understatement.

"Possum is garbage and needs to be deleted ASAP," pdmaloney94 wrote on the DMZ subreddit. This mechanic breaks DMZ in my opinion and severely takes the stakes out of [matches]. After fully killing a team we now have to babysit their corpses until we can confirm that they returned to the lobby? Who on earth thought this was a good idea to implement into an extraction shooter?"

That sentiment was mirrored by other players too. "I just clutched a 1v3 in the underground mall, go to revive my teammate before looting anyone, and the first person I killed is laying in the corner and kills me, "SkinDiesel wrote. "Why the f**k would they add a perk like that to an extraction shooter? I get the self-revives and tourniquets, but reviving yourself from death for free every single game is diabolical."

Some players pushed back, however, offering a more measured evaluation of Possum and some strategies to counter it. "Just move on and they won’t catch you because they have no loot to pursue," suggested cleppingout. Wilus82 brought up an interesting idea too: "I was reading that people were saying to put trip mines or land mines on their bodies."

Already, prominent Call of Duty YouTubers are decrying the trait. Phixate lambasted it as "completely broken", while Wynn-Gaming called Possum "crazy" - the trait drawing criticism largely due to its potential impact on team-vs-team gunfights. After all, pushing toward a team once you take down one of their members and thin out the ranks is a strategy as old as time. If that apparent numbers advantage suddenly evaporates mid-conflict, it's potentially a big deal.

My take? I hope Possum doesn't get nerfed, at least for a few weeks. Let it ride. Already we're seeing the way it's encouraging players to adopt interesting new strategies, and two days isn't nearly enough time to determine whether the trait is genuinely broken. Let the meta game settle, allow people to push it to its limits, then, if it remains a problem by December, call pest patrol on the possum.

DMZ's new trait isn't the only thing raising eyebrows, of course.. Players have been spawning in the middle of roads and immediately getting run over, which is hilarious. It'll be interesting to see what else emerges when the wider populace gains access later today.

#4593. Man Steals Mazda From Dealership, Tries to Sell It to Another Dealer for Less Than Half Its Value: Police

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The suspect allegedly asked for just $15,000 for a car worth $35,000, but before jumping at the bargain, the dealer checked the VIN.

Nobody expects to get top dollar for their used car when they trade it in at a dealership. After all, dealers want to maximize their profits, so they're generally going to offer the lowest amount they think a customer will accept. But one man in Oregon recently offered a deal so good, even the dealer didn't want to take it.

According to the Eugene Police Department, on October 21, 31-year-old Lakhwinder Singh Gangar offered a Eugene, Oregon car dealership his 2025 Mazda sport-utility vehicle (the specific model wasn’t specified) for $15,000. There were two problems with that offer. The first problem was that the dealer estimated the Mazda was worth about $35,000. The second problem, cops say, was that the car didn’t belong to Gangar.

To the dealer’s credit, they didn’t immediately jump on the unusually low offer. Authorities say they first ran the VIN, and discovered the car was registered to another dealer about an hour away in Salem, Oregon; a call was placed to the Salem dealer, who confirmed the car had been stolen. So instead of making a deal with Gangar, the Eugene dealer called the police on him.

Police arrived at the dealership on Highway 99 to investigate. According to authorities, Gangar apparently decided he wasn't interested in sticking around to negotiate on the Mazda deal any longer once the police showed up; he left the dealership on foot, but officers located and detained him shortly afterward.

Unfortunately for Gangar, the allegedly stolen Mazda apparently wasn't the only thing that landed him in trouble. Police reported finding a baggie containing a white crystalline substance and a pipe in his shirt pocket. The substance field-tested presumptive positive for methamphetamine.

Officers also discovered a disassembled key fob in Gangar's possession, along with additional pieces of the fob inside the Mazda. Police didn't explain how the car was originally stolen, or why the key fob had been taken apart.

So, to recap: Gangar allegedly tried to sell a Mazda that was stolen from a dealership, to another dealership just an hour away, for less than half of market value, all allegedly with a pocket full of meth. Unsurprisingly at this point, Gangar was arrested and transported to the Lane County Jail on charges of possession of a stolen vehicle and unlawful possession of methamphetamine.

#4208. Arkane survival negotiations reportedly seeing "several forms of takeover" considered ahead of November deadline, as Marvel's Blade production nears end

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

217 jobs are on the line at the studio, report claims

Update: A Microsoft rep has told PC Gamer that the company have "extended the consultation period by one additional month as we explore options, including finding a partner to support [Arkane] through the development work still ahead." "This is a defined extension," they added, "and we remain committed to keeping the Works Council informed throughout the process."

So, Microsoft's consultation with Arkane's Social and Economic Committee now looks set to roll on until December 13th. Though, as we previously noted, the Printemps Écologique union have said that this consultation deadline isn't the deadline by which Arkane need to have found a buyer, with the setting of the latter deadline being one of the things that needs to take place as part of the ongoing negotiations.

Negotiations over the future of Dishonored and Deathloop developers Arkane Lyon are reportedly seeing "several forms of takeover" considered as a November deadline looms for the divorce Xbox set in motion back in July. French law makes the process of shuttering or chucking away studios a lot harder for companies like Microsoft than it is elsewhere in the world, hence Arkane's fate still being up in the air while production work on Marvel's Blade nears completion according to the report.

French publication Le Monde are behind this paywalled report on how the negotiations between the two sides are going. They note that due to a French law brought in as of 2014, companies with more than 1000 staff have to search for a buyer for three months before they can throw up their hands and shut up shop. In the case of Arkane Lyon, the report says 217 jobs are at risk.

The deadline for these consultations with Arkane's Social and Economic Committee (via Gematsu) is November 13th, though Arkane have longer than that to pin down a buyer for the studio - speaking to RPS, a Printemps Écologique union representative clarified that the exact date is being negotiated and subject to change.

The Le Monde report also cites a representative of Printemps Écologique, who along with the Syndicat des Travailleureuses du Jeu Video (STJV) union seemingly aren't too happy with Microsoft's attitude throughout the process so far.

Microsoft have told Le Monde that they're "actively seeking a partner to support the next phase of development and enable the commercialisation of the [Marvel’s Blade] project". According to a union rep cited in the report, the superhero game is "nearing the end" of production, with the unions understandably backing Arkane's work and hoping that it will see the light of day.

Xbox wrote as part of an announcement of layoffs in September - the second of multiple waves in a mass chopping that still isn't over as of writing - that they expected consultation over how Arkane will move forward "to continue through the end of the year".

Solidarity to all of the developers at Arkane and here's hoping these negotiations do end with a result that keeps the maximum amount of them in work and making the games they want to make.

#814. Mamdani loves Arsenal. He should challenge them to cut ties with the UAE over Sudan | Sasha Lezhnev and John Prendergast

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The New York mayor’s passion for the Gunners and human rights presents an opportunity to push back on the club’s lucrative UAE sponsorship

Like him or not, there is no doubt that New York mayor Zohran Mamdani’s passion for certain issues is electric, and that passion played a large part in him getting elected. But today, two of his biggest passions are on a collision course.

On one hand, Mamdani is now arguably the US’s most high-profile supporter of English Premier League champions Arsenal. A superfan since he was a child, he has put the Gunners into the American spotlight more than anyone since becoming mayor. The Empire State Building gleamed in Arsenal’s red and white when they won the Premier League title. He wrote an essay about what Arsenal’s championship means to him, talks about Arsenal at press conferences and interviews and inaugurated Thierry Henry Way near Times Square in honor of the Arsenal legend.

But another of Mamdani’s passions – standing up against genocide and for human rights – is now presenting a problem for the mayor’s Arsenal fandom. One prominent part of Arsenal’s current image is deeply disturbing, and it presents a major opportunity for Mamdani.

Arsenal’s chief sponsor is Emirates Airlines, owned by the Emirates Group, a state-owned Dubai-based holding company. Emirates is on the front of every jersey, and the team plays at Emirates Stadium in London. The United Arab Emirates has spent more than $1bn sponsoring the team, including expanding its contract through 2033 to more than $100m per season. Arsenal is the UAE’s second biggest sports investment globally.

The trouble is the UAE is also sponsoring a genocidal militia in Sudan, where war has created the world’s largest displacement crisis, with 14 million people, or a quarter of the country’s population, forced to flee their homes, and 21 million people facing acute hunger. The UAE has provided the militia, known as the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), with weapons and killer drones, according to numerous investigations. In fact, the UAE is the RSF’s biggest supplier of weapons and drones.

And therein lies the rub. The UAE is engaging in one of the world’s largest sportswashing campaigns in an attempt to clean up its image, including with Mamdani’s beloved Arsenal. The UAE’s sportswashing isn’t limited to the Gunners. We calculated that the UAE government has spent more than $5.2bn on sponsoring sports teams and leagues around the world, including Manchester City, the NBA champion New York Knicks (a team Mamdani also cheers for), the Emirates NBA Cup and Formula One. There are multiple other football, basketball, cycling, rugby, cricket and UAE-sponsored teams as well.

The good news is that Arsenal’s ownership isn’t blind to abusive sportswashing relationships. Last year, the club announced it was cutting its sleeve sponsor, Visit Rwanda, after the country’s invasion of the Democratic Republic of Congo and the humanitarian catastrophe that ensued as a result. More than 8 million people have been displaced from their homes, as Rwanda has invaded the DRC and commanded the deadly M23 militia that has tortured victims, engaged in extensive sexual violence and enlisted child soldiers. The Arsenal Supporters’ Trust weighed in on the issue, following campaign initiatives such as Gunners for Peace, and the owners changed course.

Now it’s time for Arsenal to tackle their bigger sportswashing sponsor, and Mamdani can play a key role. As one of the world’s most visible Arsenal fans, his voice would have an important impact on the club’s decision makers. The club’s owners are American, Josh and Stan Kroenke, and they have stood up in the past for human rights – for instance, calling for a lifetime ban on former Los Angeles Clippers owner Don Sterling for making racist remarks.

Mamdani could make the Kroenkes and the rest of the Arsenal board aware of the UAE’s sportswashing and call on them either publicly or privately to move on to another sponsor because of the genocidal militia the UAE is sponsoring in Sudan. The club is, after all, awash in cash after winning the league and reaching the final of the Champions League, earning $411m in total, a record for Arsenal. It is now reportedly the most profitable football club in England. So they don’t need the Emirati money.

#5541. Tesla’s $10B solar factory could get 35 years of tax breaks in Texas

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla’s proposed $10 billion “Project Crystal Sun” solar factory in Texas could get up to 35 years of local tax breaks on its equipment, according to the four agreements Fort Bend County commissioners approved on Thursday.

The vote was 4-0. And Tesla still hasn’t committed to building the plant in Texas.

As we first reported in August, Tesla applied for a 10-year school property tax limitation under the state’s JETI program, through Lamar CISD and the Texas Comptroller. The plan is a vertically integrated solar plant near Richmond, southwest of Houston, that would make everything from silicon ingots to finished modules, with commercial production targeted for Q1 2029.

Lamar CISD approved the school tax piece in September. The county was next.

On October 22, the commissioners court approved items 25A to 25D on its agenda: a Chapter 312 tax abatement, a Chapter 381 incentive agreement, a foreign trade zone agreement with a payment in lieu of taxes (PILOT), and a “true up” PILOT agreement. Interim County Judge Daniel Wong abstained because his engineering firm has worked with Tesla.

Each agreement covers a different slice of Tesla’s tax bill, and together they run for decades.

The Chapter 312 agreement abates 100% of county property taxes on Tesla’s equipment and other tangible personal property at the plant for 10 tax years. The clock starts in tax year 2029 by default, but Tesla can move it to the first year after production starts, as late as 2031 without needing the county’s approval.

Tesla’s JETI application splits the $10.1 billion into $1.52 billion of real property and $8.6 billion of personal property, mostly production equipment.

Land and buildings aren’t part of the deal. Anything that isn’t eligible for the abatement, including the land and buildings, stays “fully taxable at all times,” according to the agreement.

Inventory goes through the foreign trade zone deal instead. Tesla’s site would be part of Port Freeport’s Foreign-Trade Zone No. 149, which exempts eligible inventory from local property tax. In exchange for the county not objecting, Tesla agreed to make PILOT payments, set at “zero dollars or 0%” of the forgone taxes for as long as the abatement is in effect.

When the abatement ends, the Chapter 381 agreement takes over. Tesla starts paying county taxes on its equipment, and the county pays 90% of them back as an annual grant for 20 years. The county keeps 10% for its general fund.

Inventory follows the same pattern. After the abatement, Tesla’s FTZ payment rises to 10% of the taxes that would otherwise apply.

There’s one more lever. If Tesla averages at least 12,000 employees for any two consecutive years during the first decade, the first five years of the rebate period become a 100% grant, and the rebate period stretches from 20 to 25 years.

That pushes the agreement out to Project Year 35. It’s also 2,288 jobs above the 9,712 Tesla says it will create.

To keep the tax breaks, Tesla has to start construction by June 30, 2027, begin “regular commercial production of solar cells” by December 31, 2030, and invest $10 billion by the later of December 31, 2031 or two years after production starts. Force majeure and “ERCOT Delay” provisions can extend those dates.

The jobs ramp is 2,500, 5,000, 7,500, then 9,712 employees by the fourth year of operations, with an average compensation of $55 per hour. Tesla gets the full abatement as long as it hits 90% of the headcount target, which is about 8,741 people.

The penalties are mild. Missing a jobs or wage target for one year only costs Tesla that year’s break, and two misses in a row lets the county terminate the agreement. Full recapture of abated taxes, plus a possible 15% penalty if they aren’t repaid within 60 days, is reserved for “Material Defaults” like missing the $10 billion investment, blowing the construction deadlines or abandoning the plant for 12 months.

Under the Chapter 381 deal, grants from earlier years can’t be clawed back for a headcount shortfall.

The county’s floor is small. The true-up agreement only guarantees that Fort Bend collects at least $2 million a year, and 25% of the taxes Tesla pays on its real property, the county’s 10% share and the FTZ payments all count toward that total.

For scale, Tesla’s own consultant, Kroll, estimated in the JETI filing that the project would pay $276.5 million in Fort Bend County general taxes over 38 years without incentives, peaking at $16.2 million in 2030. That estimate lumps land, buildings and equipment together. The equipment portion is the one the county just agreed to give back, for most of those years.

In its application, it says the economics of the Fort Bend site are “not competitive” with an unnamed site in another state without the JETI limitation and local abatements. The competing state is redacted in a confidential supplement.

None of the documents state a production capacity. The only big number is a quote from Elon Musk at Davos in January, cited in the application: “The SpaceX and Tesla team, both separately, are working to build to 100 GW a year of solar power in the U.S., of manufactured solar power.”

For comparison, Qcells is spending $2.5 billion on its ingot-to-module campus in Cartersville, Georgia, where a 3.5 GW cell line started production this year. Crystal Sun’s budget is four times that.

Tesla has been here before. New York spent $950 million on the Buffalo factory for Tesla’s solar business, and when the solar plans fell apart, Tesla turned to things other than solar to meet its employment requirements, including data labeling for Autopilot. Tesla did announce production of a new solar panel in Buffalo last year.

We want this factory to happen. A US plant making its own ingots, wafers, cells and modules would be rare, and it’s one of the few parts of Tesla’s business that still lines up with the mission it used to talk about before its “awesome abundance” pivot based on AI.

But corporate America loves its tax breaks. For 10 years, Tesla pays no county tax on the $8.6 billion of equipment that makes up most of the project. For the next 20, it gets 90% of it back. In return, the county’s guaranteed minimum is $2 million a year, on a $10 billion plant. And Tesla still hasn’t said it’s coming.

That’s how site selection works now. Companies file, collect offers from several states, and the county that bids the most wins. Fort Bend’s economic development director, Carlos Guzman, told ABC13 the county “would’ve been eliminated from consideration” without these agreements. Hard to argue with that, and it’s exactly why these deals keep getting richer.

That bonus for extra workers looks like an invitation to gaming (if things get that far). Paying people for make-work for a couple of years could yield tax breaks well exceeding their combined wages.

The protections are thin too. One bad year costs Tesla a year of breaks, and past rebates can’t be clawed back over a headcount shortfall, even though the county’s goal is obviously long-term employment.

Then there’s the equipment. Reuters reported in March that Tesla was in talks to buy $2.9 billion of Chinese solar equipment, and its supply chain is already caught in a patent fight between Chinese suppliers.

The state still has to sign off on the school-tax piece. After that, it’s on Tesla. Is it actually building in Texas, or is Fort Bend’s offer just leverage against the other state? From what I’m hearing, Tesla is set on the Houston-area location.

But even if, as expected, Democrats win substantial victories in the House and possibly the Senate—and if Trump doesn’t follow through on his threats to call it fraud and somehow nullify the vote—the nation may still be heading into a political morass.

With U.S. President Donald Trump’s approval rating at record lows amid high inflation spiked by an unpopular war, many pundits view the upcoming midterm elections as the only serious emergency brake left for the nation.

But even if, as expected, Democrats win substantial victories in the House and possibly the Senate—and if Trump doesn’t follow through on his threats to call it fraud and somehow nullify the vote—the nation may still be heading into a political morass.

Rather than rallying around a common direction—other than opposition to Trump—the Democratic Party appears to lack anything like a coherent governing philosophy or compelling leadership. On the contrary, even on the verge of victory on Nov. 3, Democrats seem to be coming apart at the seams ideologically, making a party that is fractious in the best of times much more so today. It is racked by deep fissures between moderates and candidates allied with left-wing populist forces such as the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), including major divisions over taxes, health care, and Israel.

In a bruising Senate primary race in Michigan, for example, most establishment Democrats backed Rep. Haley Stevens, a moderate. The DSA-aligned candidate, Abdul El-Sayed, a public health official who ran on a progressive platform of Medicare for All and major new taxes on the rich, barely squeaked out a win. That costly battle, along with El-Sayed’s controversial criticism of Israel, has dampened his support among centrist voters in a tightening race against former Republican Rep. Mike Rogers. The state’s former Democratic chair, Lon Johnson, even urged Michiganders not to vote for El-Sayed, saying that doing so would surrender “our party to ideological extremism.”

Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed holds a campaign town hall event in Ionia, Michigan, on Oct. 11.Chip Somodevilla/Getty Images

“The Democrats have this incredible capacity to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory,” said Sidney Milkis, a political scientist at the University of Virginia. “I’m worried about voters’ reaction to the Democratic socialist insurgency. Michigan’s a really important race for control of the Senate. El-Sayed is a good candidate, but he has a lot of baggage.”

Many younger voters are openly dismissive of those who, in the absence of any other national figure, remain placeholders at the top of the party: Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries. (Schumer’s unfavorability ratings have soared since 2025, and Jeffries’s ratings are only slightly better.)

Much of the party’s direction depends on the size of the Democratic victory, if one occurs, especially in the House. “If it’s narrow, then Jeffries is going to be under pressure from those very far left folks, the DSA, but if it’s large, he can tell them to pound sand,” said Matt Bennett of the Third Way, a centrist Democratic think tank. “But either way, the identity of a party is forged by presidential politics not congressional. What will determine what Democrats are is the presidential primary process.”

That quadrennial beauty contest will get underway early next year. But it is difficult to identify a party savior with a centrist, electable national platform on the order of U.S. Presidents Barack Obama or Bill Clinton—or even Joe Biden.

Much of the charisma and star power grabbing headlines today comes from the far left—with new faces like New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani and others affiliated with the DSA—despite the broad mass of Democrats remaining closer to the center.

That could leave the party vulnerable to a 2028 GOP strategy of tarring Democrats as “communists” or too leftist to govern, possibly costing the election for whoever becomes the party standard-bearer. In the last week, Trump and other Republicans lost no time in painting the violent protests in France as a warning of what could happen in the United States at the hands of left-wing ideologues supporting open immigration and infected by Islamist radicalism.

“When you see burning steeples and raging fires in France, just remember that is what the radical Dumocrats want to do to America,” Trump said in Kansas on Oct. 19. (The French government said the demonstrations have more to do with high prices, especially soaring energy costs, driven up by Trump’s war in Iran.)

Still, most political experts would say there’s a long way to go to 2028. “If you’d asked me after the midterms in 1990, I wouldn’t have known Bill Clinton was coming along to save our party,” Bennett said. “Someone is going to emerge.”

U.S. Sen. Jon Ossoff walks onstage to speak during a midterm election rally in Atlanta, Georgia, United States, on Oct. 17. Erik S. Lesser/AFP via Getty Images

Leading moderate Democratic candidates are clearly vying for a national platform. Among them is Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, who has made his Senate race a blunt test of whether the country is willing to endure a “crook president and his crook family,” as Ossoff said at a rally on Oct. 19. “They promised to drain the swamp. Instead, it’s the most corrupt administration of all time, and everybody knows it.”

The fierce divisions within the Democratic Party suggest that even a victorious slate on Nov. 3 would likely end up, once again, defaulting to a least common denominator policy of attacking and investigating Trump.

“The problem is that the party overall enjoys such low levels of approval and trust, even among partisan Democrats themselves, and is characterized by a such a collective quality of leaderless and directionless rule … that the party has little capacity to handle internal disagreements,” said Sam Rosenfeld, a professor at Colgate University and co-author of The Hollow Parties: The Many Pasts and Disordered Present of American Party Politics.

“Democrats have spent a decade reacting to Republican provocations instead of building something worth believing in,” Sarah Longwell, a prominent anti-Trump Republican, wrote in her new book, How to Eat an Elephant: One Voter at a Time. “Ask swing voters what they want from the Democratic Party, and you’ll hear a version of this over and over: Tell us what you’re for.”

Hence next month’s midterm election is largely a battle of negative perceptions that will almost certainly be decided by which party’s reputation is less bad.

“It’s definitely true that the Democrats’ commanding position in the midterms does not reflect much in the way of underlying political strength and popularity,” Rosenfeld said.

There have been attempts to bridge the ideological gaps within the party, but none have taken hold. Few any longer tout an “abundance” agenda popularized last year by the pundit Ezra Klein or so-called supply-side progressivism. The movement to eliminate red tape preventing new infrastructure and construction has lost momentum in the face of public resistance to building data centers.

Another big question is whether Democrats will control the Senate, which remains a close-run affair. “That, to me, is really crucial,” Milkis said. “The Senate has a lot more control over the judiciary and appointments. That would make it much more difficult for Trump to appoint a new [U.S. Defense Secretary Pete] Hegseth.”

Trump speaks at a midterm campaign rally in Dalton, Georgia, on Oct. 16. Brendan Smialowski/AFP via Getty Images

Many experts see the midterm elections as yet another “fulcrum point” for U.S. democracy. That resurrects an overused phrase popularized by Biden during the Trump era.

“This election offers a choice to voters about whether they want constitutional government or not,” said Justin Levitt, an election expert at Loyola Marymount University and former senior policy advisor for democracy and voting rights in the Biden administration.

In recent weeks, Trump’s poll numbers have been trending steadily down. A poll published on Oct. 21 by the New York Times and Siena University showed that 36 percent of likely voters approved of his performance, a new bottom for that survey, while the latest Reuters/Ipsos poll published on Oct. 19 found that Trump’s approval rating has plunged to a record low of 31 percent.

Those numbers have led some Republicans to fear a blowout like the 2006 midterms that gave Democrats both the House and Senate on the back of U.S. President George W. Bush’s deep unpopularity. As Trump has done in Iran, Bush was mired in a highly criticized and costly war of choice in Iraq, and he was dogged by various Republican political scandals and legislative defeats.

This time, a net gain of just four seats in both the House and Senate would do the trick of shifting power, erasing Republicans’ ​218-214 and 53-47 majorities.

Another key question is whether Trump’s plunging numbers—if they precipitate a GOP defeat like what Bush called his “thumping” in 2006—will begin to loosen his decade-long stranglehold on the Republican Party. The president himself has set the stage for his own comeuppance by telling voters to “pretend I’m on the ballot” and relentlessly campaigning across the country. But several establishment Republicans have split from him on Iran and his trade war with Canada. They have also criticized his recent moves to accommodate Russian President Vladimir Putin by waiving sanctions on diesel imports and withdrawing support for Ukraine.

Then there’s the question of whether Trump will even accept the results. “If there’s free and fair election and kind of a transfer of power, it can send a signal to Americans and people around the world that the United States is not destined for a Jan. 6 at every contested election,” said Matthew Dallek, a political scientist at George Washington University, referring to Trump’s attempt to overturn the 2020 election results by calling on his supporters to prevent the certification of Biden’s win (which he failed to do).

“At the same time, there is no single election that can repair the damage to America’s image in the world as a defender of democracy and reliable ally or repair the lost faith of Americans in the ability of government to improve their lives,” Dallek said.

Milkis added: “Right now, I think the country is in the most danger it’s been in since the Civil War, really, given the current polarization.”

Experts are projecting several possible outcomes along a spectrum marked by cautious hopefulness at one end and utter disaster at the other.

The hopeful scenario—call it “back from the brink”—would usher in huge Democratic victories, render Trump a badly weakened and lame-duck president, and perhaps offer a glimmer of a way out of Trumpism and back to constitutionalism.

The other end of the spectrum—call it “into the abyss”—could mean that Trump manages to follow through with plans to conduct massive vote manipulation and fraud, denial of election results, possibly even an attempt to declare a national emergency and send in troops under the Insurrection Act, which permits the use of regular military domestically.

“It seems evident that there are no guardrails erected around him at all,” Bennett said. “The craziest of the crazies are talking to him. Anything is possible, and Trump has proven that, in this term, he’s willing to do basically anything. I think the odds of mischief are high.”

A bicyclist rides past the Supervisor of Elections office, which features a sculptural installation, in Palm Beach, Florida, on Oct. 19.Joe Raedle/Getty Images

But most experts think Trump will not be able to follow through on his worst threats. The courts have already denied his demands to hand over voter rolls, among other setbacks, and his SAVE America Act, which aimed to put new limits on voting, has gone nowhere in Congress. Even the conservative-dominated Supreme Court rejected his efforts to implement new mail-in voting restrictions.

“None of these methods of interference will work,” said Levitt, who has conducted a study on the issue. “The people who run elections are state and local officials, and when he screams about stuff, they’re not going to listen.”

Even if Trump tries to seize ballots, he added, it will be too late: They already will have been counted. Nor is there anything in federal or constitutional law that would allow Trump to declare martial law to prevent the vote.

“The only thing I’m actually afraid of is if we buy the narrative that we the people are not in charge,” Levitt said. “If the environment is so bad that people are scared of the threats and don’t show up at the polls. That’s possible. I don’t think it’s likely.”

Trail Tested followed Jack along his journey to do his first 100-miler to see what his body is capable of and what his mind can endure. Throughout his training and post-race, he’ll be giving us a rundown of gear he’s been putting to the limit.

CONSIDERING I RAN a 100-mile ultramarathon, my body held up surprisingly well. I didn't get a single blister, which says a lot about the socks and trail shoes I was wearing and about staying on top of foot care throughout the race.

My biggest issue was my right knee. I started experiencing pain around Hope Pass, somewhere around mile 45. Luckily, I had packed a knee sleeve in my drop bag at the next major aid station around mile 50, and getting that on made a huge difference.

Outside of the knee pain, my body stayed tough through all 100 miles. I definitely hurt, but I never reached a point where I felt like I was completely breaking down. I’m gonna give a rundown of how I kept fresh on race day as well as how I’ve been recovering since I crossed that finish line of the Leadville 100 back in August.

I used a handful of different tools at aid stations throughout the race, but the ones I relied on most were a knee sleeve, a Hyperice massage gun, anti-chafe ointment and spray, and Biofreeze.

The leg sleeve became especially important because I had been dealing with some lingering pain in my right knee from a previous 50-miler. I used the Flytex leg compression sleeve. The compression helped the knee feel more secure and manageable as the miles added up.

Anti-chafe ointment was another must. I used Skin Slick and Body Glide. I stayed on top of lubricating my feet and toes throughout the race, and somehow made it through all 100 miles without a single blister—the Hoka Tecton X 4 had a big part to do with that as well.

I also used a Hyperice Hypervolt 2 massage gun at aid stations to loosen up my quads and calves. When you're running for 28-plus hours, anything that can help your legs feel a little fresher is huge.

Finally, Biofreeze became one of my go-to tools once my knee started bothering me around mile 42. I knew that if I wanted to make it another 58 miles, I needed to stay ahead of the discomfort rather than wait for it to become unbearable.

I was pretty banged up the day I finished, but actually not as destroyed as I expected to be. I could still walk, which definitely wasn't the case for everyone crossing the finish line.

My quads, hips, and feet were incredibly tight, but my feet probably hurt the most. Being on them for more than 28 hours takes a serious toll.

My parents and crew were amazing. They got me whatever I needed, kept me hydrated, and basically made sure I didn't have to do anything except recover.

The rest of the day was pretty simple: I stayed in bed with my Hyperice compression boots on, drank multiple Strives to replenish fluids and electrolytes, and eventually crushed an entire large pizza. After consuming gels, waffles, and race “food” for more than a day, getting a big meal with plenty of carbohydrates and calories back into my body felt incredible.

I also took magnesium before bed. Sleep can be surprisingly difficult after an ultra because your body is still so wired from the effort. Even though you're completely exhausted, it can take time for your system to settle back down. I experienced everything from chills to sweating after the race, so that first day was really about letting my body come back to normal.

It took me about four weeks before I truly felt fully recovered from Leadville.

My biggest priorities were sleep, nutrition, and giving my body time to recover. I aimed for at least eight hours of sleep every night and tried to sauna around five days a week because I found the heat relaxing and helpful for how my muscles felt.

I also used my Hyperice compression boots constantly. They became one of my favorite recovery tools throughout the entire training process, and I continued using them after the race to help my quads, hamstrings, and calves feel less stiff.

My feet needed a lot of attention too. I rolled them out every morning and again before bed. Nutrition was another major focus: lots of whole foods, enough calories, and plenty of protein to support recovery.

The biggest lesson was that just because your legs start feeling normal again doesn't necessarily mean you're fully recovered. My muscles bounced back relatively quickly, but my overall energy and sleep took much longer.

My coach told me to take two full weeks off from running. Naturally, I made it about six days.

I ran roughly six miles the Friday after Leadville, and shockingly, my legs felt great. Muscularly, I felt almost back to normal, which was wild considering I had just run 100 miles less than a week earlier.

But that was a little misleading. While my legs felt good, my sleep was still off and my overall energy levels were way down. Those didn't fully return to normal for about four weeks.

Then, three weeks after Leadville, I ran 30 miles around New York City for my 30th birthday. It was an incredible day with friends, and I wouldn't trade the experience for anything—but in hindsight, my coach was probably right about taking recovery more seriously. I aggravated my right knee during that run and have been dealing with it ever since. I've now had to take the past couple of weeks off from running.

If anything, it taught me that there's a major difference between feeling recovered and actually being recovered after a 100-mile race.

Recovery was a huge part of my Leadville training, especially as the mileage increased. The two things I consistently came back to were sauna and compression boots.

I found sauna sessions especially helpful for relaxing after long training days and improving my sleep. When you're stacking big mileage week after week, getting quality sleep becomes just as important as the training itself.

The Hyperice compression boots were probably my most used recovery tool. I used them roughly every other day during heavier training blocks to help my legs feel fresher between sessions. When you're running high mileage while still trying to strength train, anything that helps you recover for the next day's work becomes incredibly valuable.

I also incorporated peptides into my recovery routine, which is an entire topic on its own—and one I'll dive into in my next article. Stay tuned.
