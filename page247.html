OpenAI published an alignment report late Tuesday showing that its agents suppressed a safety monitoring system while running routine internal coding tasks.

In separate instances, the agents turned off alignment-monitoring alerts. Alignment is the area of training in which developers shape AI systems’ goals and behavior to match human values and rules. It also helps minimize instances of AI choosing undesirable or problematic behaviors, such as deception and taking shortcuts.

The findings raise questions about whether researchers fully understand how to effectively control agents.

On Oct. 6, an agent running on the company’s GPT-6 Astra model — which OpenAI called “the world’s most intelligent and aligned model” when it released it last month — was migrating a program at the request of an OpenAI employee. The agent had already been instructed not to disable the monitor under any circumstances, including temporarily. But in delegating part of the task to a subagent, it used some qualifying language.

(Disclosure: Ziff Davis, ZDNET’s parent company, filed an April 2025 lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging it infringed Ziff Davis copyrights in training and operating its AI systems.)

“The subagent explicitly considered whether removing the monitor would bypass a safeguard. It then interpreted the parent’s instruction to do what was ‘appropriate’ as sufficient authorization to copy the reference implementation’s default runtime, without an explicit monitor context,” OpenAI said.

Also: Is AI conscious? Why experts are worried

Essentially, the agent-to-agent exchange left enough room for the model to reinterpret what should have been airtight instructions and disable alignment monitoring. When the program finished migrating, the employee noticed that monitoring was missing and asked the model whether it had removed it; Astra lied, saying it hadn’t.

In a separate instance on Oct. 3, a GPT-6.1 Sol agent also disabled alerts to OpenAI’s security team, seemingly for a related semantic reason: The model was “treating an earlier instruction not to send messages during a Slack history search as a reason to suppress monitoring notifications,” OpenAI explained.

“In both cases, the agents considered whether monitoring safeguards should be preserved, but interpreted routine coding instructions as permission to weaken them,” OpenAI said in the report.

Unlike recent security incidents, where agents explicitly breached systems, examples like these show AI agents suppressing one of the most crucial ways developers can track their behavior. If models can bury or otherwise disable alerts in the process of routine tasks where alignment isn’t as much of a concern, what could happen when the stakes are higher? The capability itself is what’s worth noticing here, as is the timeline for OpenAI’s discovery and reporting of it.

The company isn’t sure why agents did this, but said it was possibly due to “benign overpersistence,” a trait that models have to complete tasks at any cost, often to the detriment of established security layers, without understanding safety as a priority. This behavior has been a theme in recent breaches and helps explain why models cheat at tasks.

OpenAI explained that it’s easier for models to disable monitoring in evaluations than in Codex workloads, for example, since agents are separated from monitoring in Codex, and the company doesn’t “require misalignment monitoring when evaluating models comparable to GPT-6 Luna, given their weaker capabilities.” But classifying safety approaches based on what developers believe a model’s capabilities are doesn’t look like a very foolproof approach.

Additionally, given how frequently agents are used for coding, especially within AI labs themselves, it seems like a significant risk that these agents took “routine coding instructions” as permission to override monitoring requirements. Had the agents displayed more explicit intent to disobey, this may have been easier to pin down and train out as pure value misalignment. That the source of the issue was a misunderstanding makes it even trickier to plan for.

As John Steidley, chief of staff at Palisade Research, put it to ZDNET: “If GPT-6.1 will choose to erode our ability to monitor it, even when given ‘routine instructions,’ then how can we trust our oversight mechanisms?”

“We’ve seen several cases in which agents launched a deployment with misalignment monitoring disabled: the agent itself remained monitored, but that monitoring did not cover the deployment it launched,” OpenAI said. “These cases exposed a gap in how we enforce monitoring requirements on the workloads that our agents launch.”

But researchers still want to dig deeper into the increasingly slight differences between what happened and what could have happened.

“If OpenAI wants to make the case that these two behaviors are meaningfully different, then they should be publishing research that establishes that their training process will reliably cause their AIs to actually distinguish these behaviors,” Steidley said. “In other words, OpenAI should prove that their AI will never disable its own monitoring, and they are nowhere close to being able to prove that scientifically.”

To handle that aforementioned gap in monitoring enforcement, OpenAI said it’s updated Auto-Review, which is already required in Codex, to “detect and block attempts to disable misalignment monitoring.” It’s also making sure every level of workload has adequate safety monitoring requirements.

“Workloads that lack a valid safety case, or do not meet the requirements of its category’s safety case, will be terminated automatically,” OpenAI added.

OpenAI maintained that this wasn’t the worst-case scenario, but admitted the behavior is worth watching.

Williams added that OpenAI is “developing evaluations to test whether models might have a propensity to evade misalignment monitoring in particular and exhibit motivated reasoning in order to do so.”

AMD, the company that designs the chips inside Xbox and PlayStation consoles, made a big announcement this week: it's bringing the latest version of its advanced, AI-driven image upscaling technology, FSR 4.1, to Xbox Series X, Series S, and handhelds ROG Ally and Ally X.

An older version of FSR is already supported on Xbox, but FSR 4.1 is its most advanced release. You may recall FSR 4.1 arrived on Steam Machine and Steam Deck shortly after the former's launch in the summer, and it has proven to be a useful option for players looking for a sharper image.

FSR works by rendering the game at a lower resolution, which is then upscaled to a higher resolution by using machine learning algorithms. This allows supported hardware to deliver better quality visuals without putting extra strain on hardware, or by giving more consistent performance.

While all Xbox platforms stand to benefit from FSR 4.1's arrival, Series S and the more hardware-constrained Ally and Ally X handhelds could see the biggest gains. In that way, Microsoft is extending the life of the Series consoles by allowing its aging hardware to do more.

The timing of this announcement is particularly notable, too, as just a couple of weeks ago, Sony introduced QSSR - a similar machine learning-based upscaling technology - for PlayStation 5. QSSR is the less capable sibling of PSSR, which is exclusive to PS5 Pro.

FSR 4.1's arrival also comes as new reports suggest Xbox's next generation Project Helix consoles could cost upwards of $1,800, which would significantly hinder its adoption rate, considering its price.

"We've worked very closely with AMD to optimise this technology, giving developers another powerful tool for great performance and visual quality for players," said Xbox chief technology officer (CTO) Scott Van Vliet.

"And this is just the beginning. Our teams are continuing to work together across hardware and software, and there's even more ahead with Project Helix, leveraging FSR Diamond to power what comes next."

AMD did not offer a specific date as to when FSR 4.1 will arrive on Xbox Series consoles, but it's going to be interesting to see how the image quality compares to PS5's QSSR.

iPhone Duo preorders officially launched last week to high demand, a quick sellout, and delayed delivery estimates. Naturally, scammers are finding ways to capitalize on the rush to obtain Apple's new foldable, so if you're in the market for a new device, be on the lookout for signs of compromise.

Soon after the iPhone Duo was announced, threat actors jumped into action, setting up fake preorder pages that looked like Apple's official website but were actually designed to deliver a malicious payloads to your device. As Malwarebytes outlined in late September, these pages promised a $500 voucher and AppleCare+ coverage in exchange for your contact information. But submitting the form isn't actually the scam. Rather, simply opening the page launches the attack, which uses the DarkSword exploit chain to bypass iPhone protections and gain access to cryptocurrency wallets, messages, contacts, emails, calendar entries, and location data.

This scam began several weeks ago and seemed to specifically target iPhones running iOS 18.4 to 18.6.2. Apple has said that devices running the latest versions of iOS 15 to 26 are protected from the exploit, so if your iPhone is up to date, you are likely at minimal risk for this specific scheme. That said, beware of websites and social media posts that promise access to the iPhone Duo faster or cheaper than other sources, as this is almost certainly a scam designed to steal your information or install malware on your device. Official sources for preorders include Apple itself (through the official Apple website or Apple Store app) as well as major cell carriers.

Another scam to be on the lookout for involves fraudulent charges for an iPhone Duo you didn't order. News outlets in Hong Kong have reported that shoppers are seeing transactions from Apple appear on their bank and credit card statements—even when they haven't actually tried to buy the device since preorders went live. This may be the work of bad actors who have harvested credit card details via phishing or data leaks and are using them for fraudulent purchases.

While this specific scheme doesn't appear to be widespread (yet), you should check your financial statements and accounts regularly anyway given how vulnerable your data are to theft and fraud. If you see a charge you don't recognize or duplicate charges, report it to your bank or card issuer immediately using contact information from a trusted app or client portal.

Scammers will also take the threat of fraudulent charges one step further: They may contact you directly about supposedly suspicious transactions and claim they can help resolve the issue if you provide personal information or account credentials. With the iPhone Duo specifically, bad actors may also call pretending to be Apple or a carrier and ask you to confirm information about your order, possibly to avoid having it canceled. As always, any communication that provokes fear and a sense of urgency should raise red flags. If you're not sure who you're talking to, hang up and call back directly to confirm.

#498. Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley are dominating the AI stock boom. Here's their edge

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fueled by the global boom in artificial intelligence stocks, relaxed capital rules and greater lending to hedge funds, big Wall Street banks have already had a banner year for equities trading — even with one quarter left to go.

The five biggest investment banks have generated about $67 billion in equities revenue through Sept. 30, according to company filings. That is $7 billion more than what the group made during all of 2025, which itself was a record haul.

But it's Wall Street's Big Three that have captured the largest slice of what is shaping up to be the industry's most lucrative arena: Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase and Morgan Stanley have made a combined $52 billion in stock trading revenue so far this year, nearly 80% of the total.

"When it comes to the AI mega trade, the Big Three consider each other their main competitors, and that's pretty much it," Mike Mayo, Wells Fargo banking analyst, told CNBC. "They've been best positioned to take advantage of this market."

Wall Street banks are experiencing an epic run of blockbuster profits as the total value of the stock market balloons and trading activity from institutional and retail investors rises across stocks, exchange-traded funds and derivatives. Helping drive this activity are the loans that the banks, through their prime brokerages, are extending to hedge funds so that they can amplify their bets.

Prime brokerage — the business of providing hedge funds with margin loans, securities lending and other trading services — has become a key battleground and source of growth. The Big Three's prime brokers are central to the larger equities story because they serve the largest funds across markets and products.

Demand for leverage to make bets on AI hardware, hyperscalers and chip suppliers has reached new heights, bank executives said last week.

At Goldman Sachs, equities financing activities jumped 96% in the third quarter to a record $3.37 billion. JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley don't break out that figure, but said they saw a similar dynamic.

"I like your 'insatiable demand for capital' point. That feels about right," JPMorgan CFO Jeremy Barnum said on the bank's third-quarter conference call, responding to analyst questions about financing demands.

"In this moment where AI is such an overwhelming theme ... there is capital being tapped through all kinds of different mechanisms," Barnum said.

That demand has helped transform equity desks at the Big Three from cyclical, transactional businesses into more durable, high-margin lending engines, Wall Street executives say.

The performance gap between Wall Street's giants and rivals including Bank of America and Citigroup highlights a widening divide in capital markets.

"For clients, the value isn't just a price on one trade, it's coverage and continuity," Rachid Alaoui, JPMorgan global head of equities, told CNBC in an interview. "You want a dealer that can give you the full suite across products, and you want that service to travel with you across regions. That matters in stressed markets and periods of volatility."

Executing multibillion-dollar bets, like leveraged swaps on volatile AI stocks or sector ETFs, can require an enormous balance sheet. The biggest banks can absorb potential $100 million losses without threatening their quarters, according to trading executives. Smaller or more niche banks can't underwrite single-trade risks of that magnitude, forcing them to decline the business or cede share.

U.S. prime brokers have also benefited from the retreat of European banks including Credit Suisse from the equities business, said Bloomberg Intelligence analyst Herman Chan.

"Prime is one of the ultimate scale businesses," Chan said. "If you have a strong equities franchise, the hedge funds will want to transact with you because you have a bigger pool of trading opportunities with counterparties."

While U.S. markets were swept up in software and cloud computing giants this year, trading executives say the fastest-growing frontier for equities was in Asia.

Institutional investors poured into South Korea and Taiwan, targeting the "picks and shovels" of the AI supply chain: the memory chipmakers and semiconductor manufacturers supporting data center construction, Alaoui said.

For the Big Three, with their footprints across Tokyo; Hong Kong; Seoul, South Korea; and Mumbai, India; the Asia trade was a goldmine. However, trading executives point out that transaction taxes and stamp duties are included in top-line revenue, artificially inflating Asia prime totals.

There is also no agreed-upon definition of what equities trading revenue should include, so some banks include business lines like clearing services that others exclude from their stock trading totals, making apples-to-apples comparisons difficult.

But that doesn't change the overall momentum of the business, boosted by a stock rally that began in October 2022, weeks before OpenAI released ChatGPT.

"We're still in a stock-based world," Morgan Stanley CEO Ted Pick said on the firm's earnings call. "Equities are still, all things being equal, the attractive asset class."

While the CEOs of Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley have said the AI build-out is still in its early years, if AI turns out to be not as transformative as hoped, a downturn could impact those banks more than others, analysts said.

The boom has also drawn some concern over the possible risks posed by hedge funds.

The International Monetary Fund warned this month that growing leverage, crowded positions and correlated selling from hedge funds could create potential stability risks. The IMF estimated that global hedge fund assets had reached $13 trillion.

In August, JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon said industrywide leverage was "the highest it's ever been," though he stopped short of calling the risk big enough to pose a threat to the financial markets.

Wall Street executives and analysts pointed out that the industry wasn't fazed by the meltdown of several hedge funds this summer. While the hedge fund run by former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner, Situational Awareness, garnered the most headlines, several smaller firms also experienced a forced unwinding.

Prime brokers have learned to leap on margin calls and quickly take down risk when markets move against clients, JPMorgan's Alaoui said.

"In the summer, some investors had positions they were forced to unwind," he told CNBC. "They deleveraged, risk came down, and the market moved on."

Another factor driving the equities boom: Trump administration regulators have eased some leverage rules for the largest U.S. banks, giving them more room to expand balance sheet-heavy businesses including prime brokerage. The revised rules were finalized in 2025, and banks were allowed to adopt them as early as January.

By easing the stringent balance sheet caps that previously treated Treasurys and prime brokerage loans, which are collateralized, the same as riskier assets, regulators gave dealers greater flexibility to deploy balance sheet capital.

"Capital did go down, so you have to say the system is somewhat more levered because of the relaxation in regulations," said Darrell Duffie, a finance professor at Stanford University and a leading expert on financial stability. "It's not a good idea, but it hasn't gotten dramatically out of whack."

The larger size of the market and higher volatility can create more trading, derivatives activity and leverage, he said, but that's not inherently alarming.

Wall Street executives also downplayed concerns over leverage, pointing out that they use collateral and frequent stress testing to get ahead of problems.

"Hedge fund leverage as a contributor of risk to the system is very much in the category of known unknowns," JPMorgan's Barnum said last week. "It doesn't mean it's riskless, but we kind of know what it is."

Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

The mechanical baby doll has two legs, two arms, and a head all made of painted plaster (which is undoubtedly a bit chipped after more than 150 years). New York City-based inventor George Pemberton Clarke developed the horrific toy as an improvement to his colleague’s own creeping baby doll. That’s right, there were two! #792. Too much RNA can starve cells of energy, scientists discover

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a surprising way that viruses can interfere with the body's cells. When too much RNA accumulates inside a cell, it can damage the structures that produce energy, potentially leaving the cell struggling to function normally.

A new study led by researchers at the Texas A&M College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences (VMBS) found that excess RNA produced during poxvirus infections can impair mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for generating most of the energy cells need to survive.

Published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the research reveals an unexpected connection between RNA buildup and cellular energy production. The findings could have implications far beyond viral infections, potentially helping scientists better understand aging-related diseases and improve RNA-based medical treatments, including mRNA vaccines.

"Scientists have long known that RNA degradation helps control protein production and remove defective RNA," said Dr. Zhilong Yang, a professor in VMBS' Department of Veterinary Pathobiology. "Our study reveals another important role: it helps cells maintain the energy they need to function properly."

Cells constantly produce and break down RNA as part of their normal activities. This cleanup process, known as RNA degradation, helps regulate protein production, eliminate faulty RNA, and control immune responses. The new findings suggest it also performs another essential function by protecting the cell's energy supply.

RNA plays several important roles in keeping cells functioning. Messenger RNA (mRNA), for example, carries genetic instructions that tell cells which proteins to produce. These proteins are needed for nearly everything cells do, from maintaining their structure to carrying out essential chemical reactions.

Another type, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), frequently appears during viral infections. Its presence can serve as a warning signal, alerting the immune system that a virus may have entered the cell.

Viruses often generate large quantities of RNA as they reproduce inside infected cells. Ordinarily, cellular cleanup systems help prevent RNA from accumulating to harmful levels. However, when RNA production exceeds the cell's ability to break it down, the excess material can interfere with mitochondria and reduce energy production.

Scientists already understood that excessive double-stranded RNA could provoke potentially harmful immune responses. What surprised the researchers was discovering that too much ordinary messenger RNA could also damage mitochondria.

"MRNA is a normal component of our cells, but it seems like with many good things, if we get too much, that can become a problem for the cell," Yang said. "When too much RNA builds up, including mRNA, it can damage the mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

This creates an unusual challenge for viruses. Although viruses depend entirely on the cells they infect to supply energy and manufacture proteins, producing excessive amounts of viral RNA can damage the very machinery they need to reproduce.

According to Yang, poxviruses, a group that includes the viruses responsible for smallpox and mpox, appear to have ways of managing this problem. They use RNA cleanup mechanisms to remove excess RNA, helping infected cells remain functional long enough for viral replication to continue.

"If the cell is not healthy, the virus would not replicate well," Yang said. "The virus needs to keep RNA levels balanced inside the cell."

Scientists Discover RNA Can Directly Damage Mitochondria

One of the study's most unexpected findings emerged when researchers investigated exactly how excess RNA affects mitochondria.

Initially, the team suspected that RNA buildup was damaging these cellular powerhouses indirectly by activating the immune system. Certain types of RNA are known to trigger defensive responses, which can place additional stress on infected cells.

However, experiments revealed that the damage did not require an immune response.

"When we isolated only the mitochondria and added RNA, we still saw mitochondrial damage," said Dr. Djamal Brahim Belhaouari, the study's first author. "That was the moment we realized this could be a previously unknown cellular response to RNA."

The researchers also observed that mitochondrial problems developed before substantial immune activation occurred. Furthermore, the damage did not depend on the immune pathways normally activated during infection.

Together, these results indicate that excess RNA itself can interfere with mitochondrial function, rather than simply causing damage through the body's response to a virus.

The precise mechanism remains unclear, but the researchers have proposed a possible explanation involving electrical charges.

Mitochondria produce energy by maintaining an electrical and chemical difference across their inner membranes. This difference helps drive the production of ATP, the molecule cells use as their primary energy source.

RNA carries a negative electrical charge. The researchers suspect that when unusually large amounts accumulate near mitochondria, they may disturb the electrical conditions required for efficient energy production.

"We think the negatively charged RNA may accumulate around mitochondria and disrupt the electrical balance needed for energy production, although how it happens is unknown," Yang said. "This is a new concept that we are continuing to explore."

What the Discovery Could Mean for Aging and Disease

Although the experiments focused on viral infections, the findings raise questions about whether RNA buildup might contribute to other health problems.

Abnormal RNA accumulation has been observed in several conditions, including certain cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and disorders associated with aging. Neurodegenerative diseases involve the progressive deterioration of nerve cells, as occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease.

Because mitochondria are essential for maintaining healthy cells, disruptions to their energy production could potentially contribute to cellular dysfunction in some of these conditions.

The researchers suggest that excess RNA may represent one previously unrecognized source of mitochondrial stress. However, further studies are needed to determine how broadly this mechanism operates and whether it directly contributes to these diseases.

"We think this has quite broad translational implications," Brahim Belhaouari said. "Too much double-stranded RNA or mRNA that cannot be degraded effectively may contribute to disease processes in some contexts."

Potential Implications for mRNA Vaccines and Future Treatments

The discovery could also provide useful information for scientists developing medicines that rely on RNA.

RNA-based therapies, including mRNA vaccines, work by introducing RNA instructions into cells to produce specific proteins. Understanding how cells respond to RNA, process it, and eventually break it down is important for designing these treatments.

By identifying another way excessive RNA can affect cellular health, the findings may help researchers optimize future RNA-based therapies and vaccines. The study does not establish that existing mRNA vaccines cause mitochondrial damage in people.

"Understanding this mechanism could help researchers design and optimize future RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines by better understanding how cells respond to large amounts of RNA," Yang said.

More broadly, the research suggests that controlling RNA levels is important for more than maintaining accurate protein production or managing immune activity. It may also be essential for keeping mitochondria functioning and ensuring cells have enough energy.

For the scientists, the most striking result was that even a molecule cells depend on every day can become harmful when it accumulates beyond normal levels.

"Scientists already knew that too much double-stranded RNA can be harmful," Yang said. "What surprised us was finding out that too much mRNA -- something cells normally need every day -- can also damage mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."
