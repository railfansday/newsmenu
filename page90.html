FirstEnergy’s Monongahela Power contends that the PJM Interconnection’s advanced scheduling of gas-fired power plants during Winter Storm Fern may have violated the grid operator’s rules.

During Winter Storm Fern in late January and early February, PJM relied on advanced scheduling of gas-fired power plants to meet demand instead of using its normal market-driven practices.

PJM scheduled ahead of time more than 50,000 MW a day of gas-fired units from Jan. 26 through Jan. 31, and it scheduled more than 45,000 MW on Feb. 1, according to Mon Power, which has about 400,000 customers in West Virginia. PJM had nearly 89,000 MW of gas-fired generation on its system as of Dec. 31, the utility said.

At the same time, Mon Power’s cheaper coal-fired generation was dispatched by PJM at or near economic minimum levels, according to the utility.

“This phenomenon — Mon Power generation plants having lower-cost energy available yet not being dispatched beyond their economic minimum levels — appears to have occurred repeatedly,” the utility said. “That outcome raises serious questions about PJM’s advance-scheduling practice — questions only fulsome discovery can answer.”

Mon Power said its $145 million loss was “the largest net expense” in its history and, without FERC intervention, would be paid by “ordinary West Virginia citizens.”

PJM has said it needed to schedule the gas-fired units in advance to give them financial certainty so they could procure fuel more than a day before they were slated to run, Mon Power said. However, many of those units were obligated to be available because they had cleared in the capacity market, the utility noted.

“Absent a showing that these units would likely have failed to perform through normal market processes according to existing market rules, PJM’s unprecedented gas-focused advance-scheduling actions were unnecessary and harmful,” Mon Power said.

The Public Service Commission of West Virginia and the West Virginia Attorney General’s Office asked FERC to accept the complaint and investigate whether PJM’s practices during Winter Storm Fern complied with the grid operator’s rules.

“The next winter storm will no doubt arrive before this proceeding ends, and the Commission should ensure that the gaps Winter Storm Fern exposed are closed before it does,” the AG’s office said.

Mon Power was not alone in raising concerns with PJM’s scheduling practices.

Vistra, an independent power producer, told FERC that PJM’s extensive use of committing gas-fired generation ahead of time can distort markets, drive up customer costs and undermine efficient price signals.

Even so, Vistra said it opposes Mon Power’s request for retroactive relief.

“Resettling the market is contrary to Commission policy against market re-runs, would penalize market participants that played a critical role in maintaining reliability during Winter Storm Fern, and would increase reliability risks by creating a disincentive for generators to take the steps necessary to ensure that they can perform in future emergencies,” Vistra said.

Instead, Vistra said, PJM should work with its stakeholders to develop market-based solutions that limit the need for advance commitments.

Rearview cameras are important not only to help drivers see behind their vehicles when backing up, but also to comply with U.S. safety regulations. This is why Ford addressed rearview camera display defects in the Maverick under NHTSA recall 24V684 in September 2024. The fix was an update to the Connected Touch Radio (CTR) software, but apparently, the remedy may not have been completely effective.

NHTSA opened a Recall Query (RQ) to evaluate the effectiveness of the 2024 recall. The safety agency estimates that the investigation covers about 273,301 units of the 2022–2024 Maverick and Maverick Hybrid, which could experience frozen rearview camera images while reversing. Such a failure could provide false information to the driver, increasing the risk of a crash.

The investigation does not establish that all 273,301 vehicles remain defective. But the RQ 26004 raises questions about whether the remedy fully addressed the problem. The 2024 recall stated that a memory leak could result in a frozen rearview camera display. A memory leak happens when software fails to release unused memory.

NHTSA received five Vehicle Owner Questionnaires (VOQs) alleging frozen rearview camera images even after the recall remedy was performed. Although the new investigation lists no crashes, the original recall report says Ford was aware of two accident reports and no injuries associated with the defect.

If the investigation shows that the fix was inadequate, another safety recall could potentially be issued. It could mean owners would have to allocate time to bring their pickup trucks back to dealerships for repairs. That’s become a familiar theme for some Ford owners, especially this decade, as the Blue Oval has led the industry in recall counts in recent years.

Ford has maintained that its high number of recalls reflects efforts to identify and address potential problems as early as possible. Some may view that as an excuse for poor quality control, but, to be fair, the company earned the top honor in the mass-market category of the 2026 J.D. Power Initial Quality Study, suggesting its efforts to improve quality are paying off, at least during the first 90 days of ownership.

While the investigation into the Maverick’s rearview camera issue continues, Ford also recalled about 1.74 million vehicles under two separate safety campaigns earlier this year over rearview camera display problems. One recall involved overheating of the Accessory Protocol Interface Module (APIM), while the other dealt with displays that could flip or invert.

Most people: Not sure where to begin with Echo devices? The best starting point is the Echo Dot. It's an easy-to-use smart home speaker, able to fill rooms with music from your preferred streaming service. It responds to voice commands and is even Alexa+ compatible.

The Echo Dot is the best entry point to Amazon's smart home devices. The Echo Dot is a squat little speaker, measuring 3.5 inches tall, suitable for any countertop, even when you're cramped for space.

Now in its 5th generation, the Echo Dot boasts more vibrant sound with clearer vocals and deeper bass, though it does not have Dolby audio like the Echo Dot Max.

The Echo Dot has a built-in mic and in-app privacy controls, as well as a temperature sensor and motion detector to help automate other smart home devices. Amazon takes smart home functioning quite seriously with its Dot: If you enable sound detection routines, for instance, you can program a particular sound (dog barking, oven dinging, door opening) to trigger a certain Alexa Routine.

You want a smart speaker for the bedroom: Whether you want an Echo smart speaker in every room or just need a smarter alarm clock, the 2024 Echo Spot is made for your bedside. It has programmable routines to queue your morning wake-up and evening wind-down. Plus, it responds quickly and clearly to Alexa commands, so you can play music from the speaker without opening your phone or ask for a weather report as you're getting dressed.

The 2024 Echo Spot improves on the previous models' shortcomings, creating an easy-to-use smart alarm clock that's both useful and good-looking. It's bigger than both the Echo Pop and Echo Dot; at 4.5 inches by 4.1 inches by 4.4 inches, it takes up just the right amount of nightstand space without overcrowding.

Slanted at a slight angle, the upper half of the clock face features a small touchscreen. While we think it's a little too small, it still has vibrant colors. The bottom half is a 1.73-inch front-firing speaker, the same one that comes in the Echo Dot. The speaker produces clear audio that doesn't sound tinny; however, it's meant for small spaces — don't plan on using it for your next party. Alexa also answers quickly to questions and commands, making it easy to turn on a podcast to kick off your morning.

The alarm function works like any daily alarm clock. Snoozing is easy: Tap the top of the device with more than one finger to get another few minutes of rest. Meanwhile, turning off the alarm is slightly more annoying, requiring you to interact with the touchscreen. While it works fine, it would feel more natural to have a "Stop" button on top.

The touch screen is rather minuscule and limited to the small rectangular space on the half-moon top of the clock face. Another big drawback of the Echo Spot comes when you're playing music or listening to a podcast. When audio is playing, the time disappears — a hindrance if you need to know what time it is.

Otherwise, the Echo Spot is a worthwhile entry into the Echo line of devices, offering a well-designed smart alarm clock.

Smart home beginners: Amazon's Echo devices don't just take the form of compact orb-like speakers. The Echo Show line of devices adds displays, and they're perfect for the person who wants to be able to take video calls, watch recipe videos, or check the calendar hands-free. The Echo Show 8 is a great starting point with a redesigned screen and upgraded chip for better performance.

Breaking beyond just audio, we're now jumping into the Echo devices with visual displays, also known as the Echo Show lineup. First on our list is the Echo 8 with its 8-inch HD touchscreen and 13-megapixel camera. It can act as a sous chef in the kitchen, serving up recipes while keeping your hands free, and it can just as easily make video calls to keep up with friends and family or stay productive while WFH (though our reviewer SaVanna Shoemaker noted some issues with Zoom functionality with the previous-gen model). You can also "Drop-In" on any other Alexa devices in your household, including video chat with other Echo Shows.

That's just the tip of the iceberg when it comes to the Echo Show 8's visual feats, though, and the gorgeous display really opens up the door to an incredible amount of enhanced Alexa functionality. The Echo Show 8 can even function as a security camera, and you can check in on what it sees via the Alexa app on your phone while you're away. That said, you can close the camera shutter and shut off the mic for more privacy.

If you're looking for the same capabilities in a smaller size for the bedroom or office, however, the Echo Show 5 is an equally nice choice. Note that the smaller-screened Show 5 won't do as good a job with streaming, pulling up ingredient lists on recipes, or other tasks that require a large display. Before we try to make sense of all your different options, let's just define what an Amazon Echo device is, was, and has become. When this line of devices first launched, the Echo was a tall cylinder that was essentially a speaker that responded to your voice commands, thanks to the artificial intelligence of Alexa. This is still the core of today's Amazon Echo devices, with some still offering just audio and others offering both auditory and visual content. Today's Echo devices also now come in many different shapes and sizes, and Alexa's capabilities have greatly expanded.

Many people tend to use the terms "Echo" and "Alexa" interchangeably, but even though their functions are deeply intertwined, they're actually quite different things. Amazon Alexa is the artificial intelligence system (kind of like its "rival" Google Assistant) that responds to your questions, acts on your voice commands, and does all the myriad other things that Alexa can do — but we'll get to that in a minute. On the flip side, Echo devices are simply the conduit for bringing that service to you. That's why Echo devices take on so many different forms, from spherical speakers to video screens to earbuds you can take on the go.

Echo devices are some of Amazon's flagship products, so you can expect to see their prices drop accordingly during Amazon Prime Day events, as well as other big shopping holidays like Black Friday and Cyber Monday. In 2024, many Black Friday Echo deals continued well into late December.

You can find Amazon Echo devices and accessories for sale at retailers other than Amazon, such as Best Buy, Target, Staples, and even The Home Depot. However, you may not have access to as wide of a selection of styles and colors as you would if you shopped directly through Amazon. You're also not likely to score the best price when shopping from a store other than Amazon, mostly because of all the aforementioned sale periods (and not just Amazon's signature Prime Day) when prices drop dramatically.

Possibly one of the best perks of purchasing an Amazon Echo device is that there's no monthly fee to use it. It's your entry point into Alexa's incredible AI and — aside from the purchase price of the Echo device itself — she and her ever-growing list of skills are totally free to use. The only reason you might encounter a paywall while using your Echo device is if you're asking Alexa to access another service, such as a streaming music service. (And yes, that includes Amazon Music; you may be prompted to start a Music Unlimited subscription if you try to access a song that's not free. Can't win 'em all, I guess.)

According to Amazon, there are more than 30,000 smart home devices marked with the "Works with Alexa" badge — and if something works with Alexa, it works with Echo too. There's a whole host of smart home items you can connect to your Echo device, including light bulbs, switches, locks, thermostats, fans, and smart plugs that can turn pretty much any electronic device into a “smart home” device.

Amazon's own brands (like Blink and eero) make a ton of smart home devices that will link up seamlessly, but Echo devices — especially those with a built-in Zigbee smart home hub — should be able to connect to any brand of smart home device fairly easily with the command "Alexa, discover my devices." (To be clear, though, Zigbee isn't required for connectivity)

Echo devices also work exceptionally well with each other. You can link as many of them together as you want, and you can even have them play the same music at the same time for a whole-home audio experience, or act as an intercom to let your family know dinner is ready.
