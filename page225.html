Bitcoin is trading at $81,615 after a small pullback from the $84,000 area. The price sits right at the 50-day moving average near $80,800, a level that has held through October. At the same time, futures open interest has climbed above $140 billion, which is near the highest level on the chart.

Open interest shows investors are returning. It measures the total value of open futures contracts, and when it rises, more money is entering the market. The chart shows a steady climb since mid-August, when Bitcoin jumped from about $64,000 to above $80,000. Since then, open interest has moved from around $110 billion to the current level of more than $140 billion.

This is important because rising open interest during a calm price range often builds fuel for a big move. The direction depends on which side is more crowded. Higher leverage also increases the risk of sharp liquidations if the price breaks the wrong way.

On the daily chart, Bitcoin trades above the major moving averages. The 100-day line sits near $77,500, and the 200-day line is near $76,200. Both are rising, which supports the trend. Since the August breakout, each dip has found buyers above those levels.

#4602. Tested: 2027 Lexus NX 350h Is Nimble But Not Exciting

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mild updates improve the NX enough to conceivably make it the best-selling Lexus model.

This is the not-all-new but pretty-newish 2027 Lexus NX 350h. It hasn’t grown or shrunk. And it has the same structure as before, carried over with minimal tweaks. But it looks cleaner, has improved tech, and is now available either as a hybrid or a plug-in hybrid. There isn’t a pure combustion version.

Call this the third-generation NX crossover. Or the second and a half. The NX has been Lexus’s second-best-selling model just behind the larger RX crossover, and this Poppin’ Fresh update could be enough to propel its sales beyond those of its larger sibling.

The NX is, like so many Toyota products, built on the bones of the TNGA component set. Precisely, it’s the same TNGA-K platform as the Toyota RAV4’s, with which it shares a production line at the Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada plant in Cambridge, Ontario.

All NX 350h models are powered by the same drivetrain found in the new ES 350h sedan, a 2.5-liter inline-four with both port and direct fuel injection driving an unsurprising continuously variable automatic transmission. On its own the four-pot makes 186 hp, and when combined with the front 160-hp electric motor it nets out at a system peak of 244 hp. All-wheel-drive versions also get a rear-mounted 54-hp electric motor, but the peak output remains the same. The hybrid battery can carry a modest 1.1 kWh of charge.

EPA MPG (City/Highway/Combined): 42/34/38

Lexus has tuned the new hybrid system so well it was hard to tell that it’s all fed through a continuously variable automatic transmission. And that’s a mighty compliment right there. The new system leans into the electric motors earlier than previous iterations, and that smooths out torque delivery noticeably. It also means the four-cylinder engine is almost never climbing up past its torque peak, calmly adding its effort to the whirring electric motors. It’s very civilized, and not that inspiring.

With the strain taken out of the internal-combustion engine, the new NX is quieter than before. And with the mechanical activity tamped down so effectively, other sounds are more apparent. Tire noise from the rear was irritating on some of the diverse pavement around Elora, Ontario, where the press event was held. While 18-inch wheels are standard on the Premium and Premium+ NX 350h models, the F-Sport and Luxury models run 20s. I spent virtually all my time in a Luxury-grade NX.

Keeping the same 105.9-inch wheelbase as the previous NX, this new one stretches out 184.6 inches long overall. That’s 1.1 inches longer than before, and all of that is forward of the front wheels. Yeah, it’s a got a long schnoz. And it still has a monstrous maw of a grille. This NX is also close in size to the original 1998 RX 300 which had a 103.1-inch wheelbase and was 180.1 inches long. It’s 29 years later, but in historical terms, this NX isn’t small.

But the comparison I came away from my drive wasn’t with another SUV but a sedan. The NX drives an awful lot like the new ES with which it shares so much of its mechanical substance and hybrid system. The ES’s wheelbase is more than 10 inches longer, making its ride smoother and more composed than the respectably smooth, composed NX. But the crossover’s shorter wheelbase meant it felt nimbler when asked to change direction and also around the parking lots of Canadian Starbucks outlets.

Untested are the mileage claims. Lexus reports EPA ratings of 44 mpg in the city and 40 mpg on the highway for front-drivers. All-wheel-drive versions are at 42 mpg city and 38 mpg highway, with the F-Sport model one mpg behind that in the city cycle. With a 14.5-gallon fuel tank, total range should approach 600 miles when the right circumstances arise.

This isn’t an expansive interior. The front seats are wonderfully shaped, but there’s not a lot of elbowroom or hiproom. The rear legroom is adequate but not generous. Cargo space is modest. Some buyers will find this all wonderfully intimate. Others will be looking at the RX and thinking that a couple thousand more bucks will be worth it.

It’s the interior driving environment that makes the NX such a pleasant on-road companion. Oversize screens are necessities in this market segment, and the NX has a 12.3-inch display in front of the driver and a well-proportioned 14.0-incher alongside it at the dash center. Neither screen is so large that it overwhelms the cockpit with aggressive graphics or retina-scorching brightness. And the tech interface has evolved for the better.

The NX’s interior is designed to be oh-so soothing, and Lexus has gone minimalist with many controls. There’s a barrel-like knob for the brilliant Mark Levinson–branded sound system, but the ventilation is managed by indistinct capacitive switches that are easy to miss without taking the driver’s eyes off the road. Also, while there’s a wireless phone-charging pad, the USB-C outlets look as if they were stuck below the dash as an afterthought.

The NX is designed with an obsessive focus on everyday usability as an upper-middle-class, suburban driving tool. It’s a well-balanced machine that sacrifices some ultimate utility in favor of nimbleness and cozy confines. Lexus has a lot of other crossovers and SUVs built around different balances for buyers who prefer different compromises.

There’s nothing radical about the new NX and a lot that is very, very familiar. But it’s a precision-crafted sales missile. For many buyers looking at this and the RX next to each other on the polished floor of a sparkling showroom, it’s the more logical and better size choice, before factoring in any consideration of money.

Pricing hasn’t been announced, but it’s unlikely there will be a vast increase from the 2026 edition. The Premium model should start around $49,000 after destination is included, with the Luxury at around $55,000.

The NX’s close relationship with the Toyota RAV4 means the same mechanical package is also available with a nonluxury price tag, but the NX feels special enough that many buyers will be entirely happy to pay extra.

Lexus calls it the Sensory Concierge, which is intended to “provide all occupants with a space as relaxing as their own living room.” It’s a kind of new-age zone-out that adds a performative edge to the climate controls, seat temperatures, and audio system for a relaxing time while parked—and then throws in a fragrance component to be extra silly. It can be tuned to particular moods. There’s Inspire, Radiance, and Revitalize. Not available: Rage, Calamitous Self Doubt, or Knockout Punch.

#4713. Psychologist found the first corporate psychopath—a 17th century Dutch mutineer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:48 PM EDT

The concept of a corporate psychopath is eerily recognizable, depending on your work history. It’s also notoriously misunderstood. The archetype of the manipulative, self-serving manager has regularly resurfaced in popular culture for decades, but concrete evidence supporting the wider concept is debatable. For example, a research study went viral in 2016 after estimating upwards of 1-in-5 business employees fit the “psychopath” bill, only to be retracted two years later. Additionally, psychiatrists widely agree that only about 1 percent of the general population truly can be considered clinically psychopathic.

That said, many of the people determined to claw their way to the top of the company ladder display at least some of the trademark psychopathic behaviors. Many theorists have convincingly argued this has a lot to do with corporate capitalism’s foundational principles, which leave it easily exploited by the most ruthless and manipulative among us.

Clive Boddy, one of the world’s foremost investigators of corporate psychopathy, recently took this idea to its logical historical conclusion. In a study recently published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics, Boddy reviewed one of toxic corporate leadership’s earliest documented examples—and he makes today’s nightmare coworkers sound tame in comparison.

Meet Jeronimus Cornelisz, an all-time contender for World’s Worst Boss. According to archival accounts, the 17th century Dutchman Cornelisz embodied every core corporate psychopath characteristic over 400 years ago while working for the conglomerate that started it all: the Dutch East India Company.

Established in Amsterdam in 1602, the Dutch East India Company is arguably the world’s first multinational, shareholder-owned company. It quickly amassed enough funds and power to rival most sovereign countries through the import and export of spices, tea, luxury items, coffee, wine, and enslaved populations. The Dutch East India Company eventually exerted enough influence to wage its own wars, mint its own currency, form colonies, as well as arrest and execute convicts.

All of this was made possible through a vast fleet of ships,but even the best outfitted vessels sometimes fell afoul of nature. In June 1629, a Dutch East India Company’s flagship called Batavia collided with a reef off the Western Australian coast. Nearly 300 survivors made it to the nearby island shores, but their remote location ensured they wouldn’t survive without help. The ship’s senior officers soon set off in an open longboat on a 1,900-mile journey to present-day Jakarta, then the Dutch East Indies’ capital. Before leaving, they appointed under-merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz to the top corporate position in their absence.

Unbeknownst to Batavia’s departing senior crew, Cornelisz had already spent weeks scheming on ways to incite a mutiny. The plan was to take control of the vessel then steer its copious stores of gold and silver to a new life. Cornelisz apparently viewed getting marooned as an inconvenient detour, and quickly got to work on his hostile takeover.

According to firsthand accounts, Cornelisz immediately consolidated power by swapping fellow council members with loyalists and requiring them to pledge an oath of allegiance to him. Next, he tricked suspected opponents under the direction of a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes to search a nearby island for supplies, then left them to die.

Although his revised plan required any impending rescue ship, Cornelisz’s ultimate goal remained the same—get as far away as possible, then start his own kingdom using riches plundered from Batavia. Over the ensuing months, Cornelisz directed his gang to kill more than 100 of the shipwrecked men, women, and children. Cornelisz often justified the murders under the pretense of conserving food and water, when in actuality he was continuing to eliminate perceived threats. Meanwhile, some of the surviving women were subjected to sexual abuse.

However, Cornelisz underestimated his abandoned compatriots. As the operatically psychopathic Cornelisz terrorized his group of survivors and scrounged for supplies, Hayes’ team reportedly discovered freshwater wells and hunted local wallabies, seals, and fish. The two sides subsequently squared off on multiple occasions after learning of each others’ continued existence until a rescue ship finally arrived in October 1629. After nearly three months, only about a third of Batavia’s original passengers and crew were left.

Management ultimately decided Cornelisz wasn’t internal promotion material, even by Dutch East India Company standards. Nearly everyone involved in his reign of terror was executed. As for Cornelisz and his closest loyalists, authorities eventually hanged them, but not before cutting off their hands.

The tragedy of the Batavia is an admittedly extreme case study, but Boddy argued that it illustrates how corporate structures have facilitated and encouraged psychopathic behaviors since the invention of the corporation itself.

“The same playbook we see in toxic workplaces today appears in this case from 1629,” he said. “He removed opponents, rewarded loyalists, created fear, manipulated followers and pursued power at any cost.”

In recent years, some historians have expressed doubts about details in the Batavia saga after reviewing available evidence and recontextualizing cultural dynamics. Instead of focusing on the machinations of a single ruthless corporate psychopath, they contend Batavia is more accurately a case study in group desperation. But even when viewed under this lens, the chilling event is still a cautionary tale about corporate capitalism’s darker underpinnings.

“Perhaps the most striking finding is that another leader facing the same circumstances achieved completely different outcomes,” Boddy said. “This was effectively a natural experiment in leadership. One group prospered while the other descended into murder and abuse.”

#3135. Shai Gilgeous-Alexander's 20-point streak is over, and it's a record that may never be broken

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

For the first time since Oct. 30, 2024, a span covering nearly two full seasons and an NBA record 141 straight regular-season games, Shai Gilgeous-Alexander was held under 20 points on Thursday in the Thunder's 96-84 win over Denver. He finished with 17 points on 6-of-19 shooting before exiting the game with less than two minutes to play.

Had SGA wanted to chase another three points to keep the streak going, he could have. Thunder coach Mark Daigneault would've left him in the game. But when the Nuggets pulled their starters with a 17-point deficit and 1:43 remaining, Gilgeous-Alexander followed suit.

"He asked out of the game," Daigneault confirmed. "And he's got a historic streak going. Which is a remarkable streak, obviously. And I just think the class that it shows, and the perspective that it shows, for him to say, 'Hey, I'm out now. The game's calling for me to be subbed out.' That was his substitution. Of all the things I admire about him, that's toward the top of the list in terms of moments that I appreciate about him. It shows tremendous class as a competitor."

Gilgeous-Alexander did well to even have a shot at continuing the streak. He missed 11 of his first 12 shots and had just two points at halftime. He made his last push with a patented elbow jumper and a trailing 3 in transition on two of his final three possessions to get to his 17, but that was it. Denver called timeout, and SGA was done.

"I asked him, 'Do you wanna shoot some 3s right here?" Chet Holmgren told reporters of a late-game exchange with his teammate. "And he was like, 'nah,' and then he went to Mark and subbed himself out. I told him he's a better man than me. I would've been shooting some pull-up 3s. But I think it was a selfless moment, and he's a selfless guy."

"I was gonna go out there, but then I saw that [the Nuggets] had subbed, so I was like, 'I should probably sub too,' Gilgeous-Alexander said. "I feel like you should always treat the game with respect. And, God willing, I'll have many more games to start another streak, so it's alright."

"[Shots] that he normally makes didn't go down," Daigneault said. "And [the Nuggets] sold out on him tonight. They really put him in a crowd and really tried to take him away. It's not the first time a team has tried to do that. So the combination of their scheming and him not having his normal stuff."

To Daigneault's point, the Nuggets definitely did show SGA a lot of defensive attention. If he wasn't being outright double-teamed, he was seeing multiple defenders in his line of vision. On a few possessions that he did get to go one-on-one, Christian Braun, who had a hand up on almost every SGA attempt, did about as good a job as you can expect by staying in front and forcing contested misses.

As Daigneault said, this isn't the first time SGA has seen multiple defenders. The Nuggets -- again, we need to highlight Braun's individual defense as much as the doubles; he really kept his chest in front and forced SGA to fade a bit more than even he would like -- did their part to make it tough on him, but he also missed a lot of shots that he normally makes.

More than the misses, it was the shots SGA couldn't even get off that ended this streak. Nikola Jokić left his man, sometimes on the other side of the court, to double his drives before he could turn the corner. Wings left their shooters (typical Alex Caruso treatment) to form a crowd around SGA, who also appears to be operating off-ball a bit more to start the season with Jalen Williams back in the fold (I haven't looked at the data on this yet, but it appears this way).

That's not a huge change. The Thunder have always liked to run SGA through off-ball actions before getting it to him rather than just having him bring it up the court every time, which keeps everyone involved and creates more movement to limit predictability and force defenses to make multiple decisions. But it can leave him out of a few possessions here and there, and if it's a night where the shots he is getting aren't falling and he's also not getting to the free-throw line (only two attempts on Thursday), it's harder to just pile up points on sheer volume.

There's a real chance that this is one of those marks that will last forever. SGA makes scoring 20 points in an NBA game look like child's play, but when you're talking about doing it night after night for damn near two full regular seasons, even the best scorers in the world are going to struggle to even get close to this record.

Consider that after SGA's 141, only one other player in history has managed to even get to 80 straight 20-point games. That was Wilt Chamberlain, who did it in 126 straight games between 1961 and 1963, and again for 92 straight games in 1963-64. After that, Oscar Robertson did it in 79 straight games.

The most consecutive 20-point games Michael Jordan ever logged was 72. That's Kevin Durant's longest run, too. Kareem Abdul-Jabbar's best is 71 straight. Again, these are some of the best scorers in history, and they have barely made it halfway to SGA's 141-game mark. They say records are meant to be broken, but this one could prove to be an exception. At least for a very long time.

The fuse was lit. Nearly 12k likes, 1k reposts, and 3.5k comments later, the team had their most engaged post of the year by some margin. The irony is not lost on me that Bialik’s quote, about how rarely people talk about side effects, sparked such a deep conversation about that very thing.

Even so, no one predicted the post would catch fire the way it did. Our social team’s read is that a few things stacked up: Mayim Bialik is a name our Facebook audience knows, the topic is one where nearly everyone has a stake, and a busy thread tends to get shown to more people, who then add more comments. That’s the hypothesis. What we’ve consistently seen is that posts that lead with a quote get more people talking than those that lead with a headline. You may have noticed this article takes the same approach.

We also know that the comment sections on social media aren’t always the most hospitable of environments, particularly in the health space. What stood out about the lion’s share of the comments we read was how constructive the discourse was. People were leading with deeply personal and often moving perspectives about the life changing impact (for good and bad) of GLP-1s. In turn, they were met with equally balanced and thoughtful responses.

Many of the comments expanded on Bialik’s experience, detailing long and difficult battles with a range of side effects. But plenty told the other side of the story: years of failed diets and struggles with chronic conditions leading to despondency, side effects that went on to ease with time, and life changing results that were ultimately worth the cost.

The line that opened this piece could have come from any of those commenters. It didn’t. It came from Kitty Hough, who is 75 and lives in London. Eagle-eyed readers will have spotted that she has the same surname as me – that’s because she’s my mom. She’s one of two people closer to the Healthline home who spoke to me for this piece and whose stories echo the Facebook post thread, with the good and the hard bits side by side.

Kitty was a hockey player and distance runner as a girl, the kind who could eat what she liked without it showing. Then came children, and a weight that crept up year after year, with decades of diets that “worked for a couple of months only to be back to my former weight and then some.” In late 2024, after a lot of research, she decided to try tirzepatide.

Since then, Kitty has lost 56 pounds. She says her joints hurt less, which she puts down to reduced inflammation. She is more mobile. The constant chatter about food has faded. She thinks harder before she eats now.

“I am a strong believer that obesity is a disease and in my case certainly not the result of overindulgence or laziness,” she says. “GLP-1s have greatly improved my health and self-esteem. After years of struggling with my weight, I have finally found a way to solve the problem.”

Kitty will also tell you what it cost. Nausea. Constipation. Sulfur-tasting burps and rough nights of indigestion. Initial hair loss that has since grown back, and loose skin. Muscle loss. And she misses some of the true joy of cooking and eating food.

But despite bumps in her journey, Kitty remains convinced that the benefits of GLP-1s for her life and health outweigh any drawbacks she’s experienced. “I am conscious that I will probably be on this drug for a long time if not for life. It is truly a miracle drug.”

I also spoke to Ann Marie Maguire, AGNP-C, RN, TCTCN, a nurse practitioner specializing in transplantation and cellular therapy, the sister of Healthline’s Chief Business Officer Christine Macguire, and someone who has lost over 50lbs since starting GLP-1s in 2022.

“I struggled with my weight since my teens and always believed I should be able to manage it on my own. But in my 30s, my knees were hurting just getting out of bed or walking to dinner. I realized how much my weight was affecting my quality of life. Starting a GLP-1 in 2022, alongside healthier eating and exercise, made a tremendous difference. My only regret is not starting sooner,” said Ann Marie.

As a healthcare professional, Ann Marie also identified cost as a major factor that can’t be ignored regarding GLP-1s and their impact.

“There’s understandable debate about the long-term use and cost of these medications,” she says. “But we also need to consider the health and financial burden of obesity, including cardiovascular complications, hospitalizations, surgeries and ongoing medications. The cost conversation should account for the potential benefits of preventing or reducing those complications.”

Ann Marie points to a frustration many patients will recognize: reaching a healthier weight can become the reason coverage gets cut off. “We don’t automatically stop blood pressure medication because a patient’s readings improve,” she says. The treatment may be what’s keeping the condition controlled. Obesity, she argues, deserves the same approach. “Reaching a goal weight can mean treatment is working, and decisions about continuing it should reflect the patient’s ongoing needs, benefits and side effects.”

It’s important to note that this article looks at the experiences of only a handful of people out of the millions who now take GLP-1s. Like all drugs, these medications are not right for everyone. Side effects are common; some of them can be serious, and both the long-term impact and what happens when people stop are still being worked out. Ultimately, your care team, with knowledge of your medical history, is a far better guide than a comment thread.

Yet the evidence now reaches well beyond the scales: large trials have linked semaglutide to fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease and slower kidney decline in people with diabetes and kidney disease, and tirzepatide became the first FDA-approved drug for moderate to severe sleep apnea in adults with obesity.

Stories like Kitty’s, Ann Marie’s, Mayim Bialik’s, and those of the 3.5k commenters on the Facebook post aren’t proof of what will happen to you one way or another. But for a lot of people, my mom included, these drugs have changed what an ordinary day looks like, and in many cases changed what their future can look like.

One of the most significant drug developments of the century so far? Hard to argue with.
