Cody Gakpo has claimed that Liverpool may have been ‘a little bit lucky’ with one crucial moment in their 2-0 win over Brighton on Sunday.

Two goals in the space of five first-half minutes ultimately settled the contest, with the deadlock broken on 17 minutes when Florian Wirtz’s shot took a heavy deflection off an unsuspecting Pascal Gross and was diverted into the Seagulls’ net.

The strike which doubled the lead owed to Bradley Barcola’s persistence on the right to shake off the challenge of Olivier Boscagli and cross for Alexander Isak to supply a first-time finish from close range.

Gakpo was reflecting on the result for Liverpool’s official website when he conceded that the Reds may have benefitted from a stroke of fortune for the opening goal, albeit with an acknowledgement that his team also made their own luck.

The Dutch winger said: “We get the first one from Flo, maybe a little bit lucky but you know sometimes you create the luck yourself, and then we could say, ‘Let’s rest a bit because we are one up’; but we decided that we wanted to go through and the coach obviously is pushing us to keep going.”

#2991. LeBron James explains why he doesn't want to play a 'sustainable role' at center for the 76ers

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

When LeBron James joined the Philadelphia 76ers, he highlighted his versatility as a reason the fit could work. "The luxury of my game is that I can do everything," James said at 76ers media day. "Literally, I can do everything." Of course, just because he can do everything doesn't mean he wants to. There is one job, at least thus far, he'd prefer to hand off to a teammate: playing center.

Considering Joel Embiid's lengthy injury history and the season-long loss of Ariel Hukporti to a torn Achilles, front-court depth is a notable concern for Philadelphia. On Sunday, Embiid missed his first game of the season due to plantar fasciitis management, and part of the replacement plan involved playing James at center. It's a role he can take in small doses, but not one he hopes to maintain for much more than that.

"Well, shit, we got an MVP over there. I'm not here to have a sustainable role at the 5," James said after Sunday's loss to the Detroit Pistons, the Sixers' first defeat of the season. "You got Joel Embiid on the team. ... If I was to say I was signed here to have a sustainable role at the 5, I don't think none of us would actually want that. I can do it, but... we have [Embiid]."

The very early numbers support the notion that James is best served playing elsewhere. James has played 50 possessions at center thus far this season, according to Cleaning the Glass, and the Sixers have a -10 net rating within those minutes. The historical numbers are a bit more complicated. James didn't start playing center consistently until the 2021-22 season, and he did so mostly out of roster necessity. The trends are about what you'd expect: James-at-center lineups tend to be very good offensively and very weak defensively.

Of course, the actual performance numbers are only a small part of the equation. This will be LeBron's age-42 season. He simply is not at the same stage of his career physically that he was when he started playing center more frequently with the Los Angeles Lakers. Keeping him healthy is the priority for Philadelphia, and center is the most physically demanding position on the court for James. That, more than anything, is the reason to minimize these stretches.

When Embiid is healthy, that's fully doable. He was not on Sunday, nor were Hukporti, Dominick Barlow, Jabari Walker or Tacko Fall. "We've only got about three guys left that can possibly play the 5," 76ers head coach Nick Nurse said before the game. James was one of them.

As James said back on media day, he can play center mostly because he can play anywhere. An emergency situation like Sunday hopefully isn't the norm. But the reality of employing Embiid on a supermax contract is that affording reliable backups is almost impossible and he's going to miss time due to injury. If his durability is as much of a concern this season as it has been in the past, the Sixers may not have much choice but to play James at center more than he likes, at least until they can pursue more depth closer to the trade deadline.

However, it carries risks because the brand has a relatively small market share and a limited dealership network stateside. Sounds like trading one uphill battle for another.

#3771. Corruption Allegations Aren’t Susan Collins’s Biggest Problem. That’s Donald Trump.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Susan Collins was concerned. Not in the publicly hand-wringing way for which the Maine Republican senator has been repeatedly mocked, most notoriously for trusting then Supreme Court nominee Brett Kavanaugh’s statement that Roe v. Wade was “settled law.” No, this was a serious political concern.

In late September, ProPublica published a story alleging that Donald Trump’s Department of Justice killed an investigation into whether Collins had given a military contractor favorable treatment in exchange for campaign money. The bombshell dropped just as Collins was heading into the final weeks of her toughest reelection race ever, a contest that could decide whether Republicans retain a Senate majority. The corruption accusations threatened to damage Collins’s strongest appeal to voters: her ability to deliver jobs and funding to Maine. Should she issue a statement denying any wrongdoing and then ignore the ProPublica story as much as possible, trying to cut off its oxygen? Or should Collins punch back hard?

Those strategic choices were the immediate, high-stakes priority. Yet even as her camp calculated, an absurdity hovered in the background: After years of Trump bluntly insulting Collins, could he really have gone out of his way to try to do her a favor? “I don’t know whether that notion,” a Collins insider says, “is ironic or comic.”

Collins and Trump tangled frequently during Trump’s first term as president, peaking with Collins voting in favor of his second impeachment after the January 6 insurrection.

Troy Jackson is the Democratic nominee, a late replacement for the disgraced Graham Platner. The former logger and state legislator can be a stiff speaker, but in four contentious debates, Jackson has effectively blamed Collins for the soaring cost of everything from home heating oil to blueberry processing—by tying Collins to Trump’s tariffs and the president’s war with Iran. Because no matter who the Democrat on the ballot is this November, in many respects, the greatest obstacle to Collins winning a sixth term has long been the leader of her own party.

The two tangled frequently during Trump’s first term as president, peaking with Collins voting in favor of his second impeachment after the January 6 insurrection. The invective resumed when Trump returned to the White House. Along the way, he has castigated Collins as a “disaster,” a “loser,” and “absolutely atrocious,” and declared that she should “never be elected to office again.” Those are just some of the public attacks. In private, the president reportedly tore into the senator in a “profanity-laced rant” over the phone, after she voted in January in favor of curbing Trump’s war powers.

“Like most things with the president, he says exactly what he thinks,” a senior Republican strategist says. Steve Bannon, the on-again, off-again Trump adviser, sees something in Trump’s antagonism that goes beyond the political. “She’s the kind of person he has no time for—the mousy personality,” Bannon tells me. “She’s just a non-event. Even when she’s against something, she’s not really against it. And it’s not like she’s calling him and explaining why she’s not on board. It irks him.” Collins has sidestepped questions about whether she would campaign alongside Trump if he turns up in Maine this fall, but she pointed to a scheduling conflict to explain her absence from an appearance by Vice President J.D. Vance in Brewster in August.

Yet Trump has lately bitten his tongue about Collins, even as she strenuously tries to distance herself from him on everything from July’s fatal ICE shooting in Maine to Trump’s recent imposition of punitive tariffs on Canadian goods. Insiders say the president’s silence is strategic: He needs Collins to win if the Republicans are going to hold their Senate majority. “He’s pragmatic, a practical guy, always has been,” says the senior Republican strategist, who knows Trump well. “Is there any other Republican who could win in Maine?”

Mike DuHaime, a national Republican strategist who directed Senate campaign spending in previous cycles, believes Collins’s familiarity to Mainers will insulate her from the corruption allegations. “The biggest factor in that race,” he says, “is whether voters who still like Susan Collins are going to try to send a message to Trump at her expense.”

You may have heard Maine’s Democrats have had an exciting year. First came the meteoric rise of Platner, an Iraq veteran turned oyster farmer who seemed to be the next great populist hope until he was undone by an unceasing run of scandals, from the Nazi tattoo on his chest to accusations of sexual assault. (Platner has said he wasn’t aware the tattoo was recognized as a Nazi symbol and has since covered it. He has denied the sexual assault allegations.)

Platner’s noisy ascension and implosion didn’t just leave the Democrats looking untrustworthy. For months the saga consumed nearly all the media coverage in the Maine race, turning Collins, the five-term incumbent, into an afterthought. Whenever she did enter the conversation, Collins was commonly reduced to a caricature: the disingenuously naïve, dithering swing vote.

The people in Maine and DC who know her best, though, describe Collins as far tougher than she looks. “People always expect her to break,” a senior Democratic senator says. “She is more savvy and has more integrity than most people give her credit for.” Even a national Republican strategist with ties to the White House offers grudging respect: “She knows exactly what she’s doing and why she’s doing it. She is a damn good politician.”

Indeed. Collins made some mistakes during four debates with Jackson in the past month, including garbling the name of a Maine man shot and killed by ICE. But in the final showdown she also nimbly improvised a response to a protestor, provoking Jackson into telling Collins to “shut up,” a moment that generated helpful headlines.

Collins grew up just about as far north as you can get on the East Coast and still be in the United States, roughly 15 miles from Canada, in the town of Caribou, Maine, population 7,396. In a region known for its poor potato farmers, Collins’s family, as owners of a hardware store and a lumber mill, were relative royalty. “They had serious money,” says Gerry Conley Jr., a former second-generation Democratic Maine state legislator. “And she had a lot of connections.”

“She has not gotten to where she is on charisma," says Republican strategist and Maine native Lance Dutson. "She has gotten there on diligence. In some ways, she still thinks like a staffer.”

Still, Collins’s talents seemed, initially, best suited to working behind the scenes. Her first political job was as a staffer for 12 years to Maine Republican senator William Cohen. She returned north to serve as Governor John “Jock” McKernan’s commissioner of the Department of Professional and Financial Regulation, a role that put Collins at the center of a 16-day state government shutdown that was prompted by a dispute over workers’ compensation rates. “She was by far the smartest person in that administration,” Conley says. “She was dangerous, a tough negotiator.”

Bookish and bespectacled, Collins didn’t seem like an obvious candidate for office herself. It was something of a surprise, then, when Collins ran for governor of Maine in 1994; it was little surprise that she finished a weak third to Angus King, an independent, who won.

But Collins has always been a driven student. While working for Cohen, she met his chief of staff, Thomas Daffron. Daffron—who has shifted between jobs as a lobbyist and a congressional staffer—became Collins’s political mentor and, in 2012, her husband. In 1996, she returned as a more polished campaigner and won the Republican nomination to replace Cohen, whom president Bill Clinton appointed secretary of defense. Collins effectively styled herself a moderate in the vein of Maine senatorial predecessors Cohen, Olympia Snowe, and Margaret Chase Smith. She also demonstrated, not for the last time, a knack for facing an underwhelming opponent. Collins dispatched Joe Brennan, a retread Democrat, by slightly more than five points. In the three decades since, she hasn’t had a genuinely close race.

The battle against Jackson, though, looks like it will go down to the wire: a Washington Post poll, released October 9, showed the Democrat edging to a four-point lead. Bannon, interestingly, believes the Republicans’ intense focus on saving Collins could very well backfire for the GOP, not just in Maine, but across the country. “Iowa is in play, Kansas is in play, Nebraska is in play—because of Susan Collins,” he says. “The Senate majority didn’t move MAGA legislation, like the Save America Act, because John Thune was adamant about protecting his weakest members. And Collins is always number one on that list. If you don’t give the grassroots, low-propensity voters some reasons to show up in a midterm, they just don’t.”

In Maine, Collins’s problem isn’t the MAGA faithful. It’s independents, who make up nearly 33% of the state’s registered voters, and who have soured on Trump. So Jackson and Democrats are relentlessly trying to lash Collins tightly to the president. “Donald Trump owns Susan Collins,” Jackson told reporters recently. With independents, Collins is counting on the thing that has sustained her all these years: Whether you agree with her policy choices or not, she’s stable, sane, and at heart a “county girl,” a humble local, a true Mainer. Platner is gone from the race, but the turmoil he generated still resonates and makes Collins look dependable compared to the Democrats. The crescendo of outsider spending and criticisms could help bolster her appeal. “There’s a cultural norm, deeply rooted in Maine, that she’s one of ours and we’re going to stick with her,” says Tom Connolly, a Portland lawyer and longtime Democratic activist. “She has remarkable survival skills.”

Collins frequently walks through the Senate hallways carrying a large stack of briefing books. This isn’t an affectation. “She’s the hardest working person I have ever experienced in my life,” says Lance Dutson, a Republican strategist and Maine native who was a senior aide on three Collins Senate campaigns and also worked in her Washington office. “She has not gotten to where she is on charisma. She has gotten there on diligence. In some ways, she still thinks like a staffer.”

Collins travels from Washington to her home in Bangor nearly every weekend. In mid-July she marched, to a mix of cheers and boos from the 10,000 spectators, in the 42nd edition of the Moxie Festival Parade, an annual event in Lisbon, Maine. “She didn’t hang out or anything afterwards,” says Angie D’Amours, the event’s cochair. “Angus King [now Maine’s other senator] does, typically. There’s plenty of blame to go around at Old Trafford. Like all downfalls it started with a seemingly small, unconnected moment

And so here we are again, at that point as the nights draw in and Manchester United prepare to sack a manager. Autumn: season of mists, mellow fruitfulness and a crisis at Old Trafford. Sunday’s 3-0 home defeat to Bournemouth, coming on the back of last week’s 4-1 reverse against Como, made clear what many had feared from the moment Michael Carrick was given the job on a permanent basis: that he isn’t up to it.

But then, who could be? When you’ve burned through a long list of managers in 13 years, it’s perhaps worth considering whether the manager is really the problem. Could some blame perhaps go to the director of football, Jason Wilcox, or the chief executive, Omar Berrada, the people making the appointments? Or perhaps to Sir Jim Ratcliffe, the cost-cutting tax exile and minority shareholder with responsibility for sporting operations? Or to the Glazer family, who plunged the club into debt with a leveraged buyout in 2005 and have since made calamitous decision after calamitous decision, reducing the most successful club in English league history to a mid-table laughing stock with a roof that leaks symbolically?

It’s worth remembering where all this began. The United story has taken on the aspect of a classical tragedy and, like all good tragedies, it begins with a small, seemingly unconnected moment, amplified by pride, that ends up bringing the whole edifice crashing down.

In 1999, Mull of Kintyre, a horse owned by the Irish millionaire John Magnier, won the Gimcrack Stakes at York. Traditionally, the winning owner would give a speech at the Gimcrack Dinner, but Magnier was a shy man who hated public speaking so he asked his friend, the United manager Alex Ferguson, to give the speech on his behalf. The racing establishment, though, refused: it had to be the owner. To spite them, Magnier named Ferguson as the owner of another horse, Juniper. The following year, Juniper was narrowly beaten in the Gimcrack Stakes. Magnier, though, was determined. So the year after that, he made Ferguson the owner of Rock of Gibraltar, which had won its first three races. Rock of Gibraltar won the Gimcrack easily and Magnier, waving a metaphorical two fingers at the racing authorities, watched Ferguson give the speech.

Magnier was largely making mischief, but he was also happy to do a favor for a friend, to give Ferguson a better experience of racing, and he benefited from the association of the horse with such a famous name. In addition, Ferguson did not get on well with the United chairman, Martin Edwards, and had a sense that football clubs were becoming good investments. Accordingly Magnier and his associate JP McManus bought shares in United. (The full story is explored by the journalist David Walsh here).

It turned out, though, that Rock of Gibraltar was a much better horse than anybody had realized, winning seven Group One races in a row. The stud fees would be worth tens of millions of pounds. Ferguson believed the verbal deal was that he could choose 50% of the prize money won by Rock of Gibraltar (about £800,000) or 50% of the stud fees, which ended up being worth tens of millions. He went for the latter. Magnier’s recollection was rather different: 5% of the prize money or one stud nomination a year.

Ferguson sued and, with his name on the ownership documents, probably would have won had it gone to trial. But then rumors started to spread about some of Ferguson’s financial dealings at United. Paid actors at United’s annual general meeting raised awkward points, then Magnier and McManus sent a list of 99 questions to the United board, which mysteriously found their way into the Daily Mail. United were highly embarrassed. Fans began to demonstrate against Magnier and McManus at race meetings. A settlement was reached, with Ferguson paid £2.5m to drop the action. But the damage was done.

Magnier and McManus had decided to sell their shares in United, and another shareholder was ready and waiting: Malcolm Glazer. Ferguson backed Glazer, who launched his leveraged buyout. Magnier and McManus sold up in 2005 for £260m, a profit of a little under £100m. Had they waited for Ratcliffe, who bought a similar stake in 2024, they would have received roughly £1bn more than that. For Ferguson, a threat had been dealt with.

The problem was that Glazer and his family would prove some of the worst club owners in English history. For 20 years, United have been addled by debt and have made consistently terrible footballing decisions. Ferguson’s team was good enough, and his personality big enough, that United won five league titles and a Champions League after the takeover, but once his genius had gone, there was nothing left.

Pitching from the stretch improved many things for Peralta, including his command. Location+ is a metric that grades pitch locations not just on whether they're on the edges of the zone, but whether the pitcher puts that pitch in the right part of the zone. Is that four-seamer at the top of the zone? Is the changeup down and away? You won't score well for hanging a slider just because it's a strike.

Peralta posted a solid 104 Location+ with the Mets, which is comfortably above the 100 MLB average. Peralta went from good location with the Mets to great location with the Rays, thanks in part to his new delivery.

The Rays also helped Peralta raise his arm angle a bit, something that is very difficult to do in-season and an adjustment pitchers usually work on in the offseason or Spring Training. He also added extension and drive toward the plate, meaning he's getting further down the mound and releasing the ball closer to the hitter.

With the added extension and higher arm angle came more velocity and more vertical break on the fastball, which is "life" up in the zone. The Rays took away his windup, but they did not create an entirely new Peralta. They simply got him back to the 2025 version of himself, and it all starts with the delivery. Pitching from the stretch has allowed him to execute at a much higher level.

"When I got here, when I sat here with (pitching coach) Kyle (Snyder) and (bullpen coach Jorge Moncada) and Cash, they told me, 'You're gonna do what you want to do here, and we are going to help you to do it. And we know what you need to do to make those adjustments,'" Peralta told MLB.com in September. "We just put everything on the table. I just followed all the instructions, and it's what I am right now."

In addition to the mechanical changes, the Rays also tweaked Peralta's pitch mix, specifically getting him to use his changeup more often. This is a Rays staple. Their pitchers threw an MLB-leading 20.6% changeups in 2026. The next team, the San Diego Padres, threw only 16.3% changeups. The gap between No. 1 and No. 2 was the same as the gap between No. 2 and No. 13.

Peralta's changeup usage jumped from an already healthy 20.8% with the Mets to 29.7% with the Rays. Against right-handed batters specifically, it jumped from 15.8% to 22.2%. Same-side changeups are another Rays specialty. Usually, you throw changeups to hitters on the other side of the plate, but not Tampa. They love same-side changeups and Peralta has bought in.

Between the combination of swings and misses and weak contact, the changeup is Peralta's best-performing pitch, so the Rays have him throwing it more often. "Throw your best pitch more" is a pretty simple concept, but not always something pitchers and teams will adhere to. The Rays got Peralta to pull back on everything else, throw more changeups, and the results have followed.

"We were trying to make adjustments," Peralta said Saturday about his time with the Mets. "We knew, we understood, and we spoke with each other about it. I remember that we were doing the right thing because we were preparing in the right way, but we couldn't do what we were supposed to do."

There are some elements of Peralta's performance with Tampa that likely would not continue across a full 162-game season. That isn't to say he's been lucky -- I hate that term -- but baseball is a game of ebbs and flows, and right now Peralta is riding a wave in which the outcomes have gone in his direction. Over time, they would likely even out, but they haven't yet.

Expected ERA (xERA) estimates a pitcher's ERA and factors in contact quality allowed (exit velocity, etc.), and it views Peralta as basically the same pitcher with the Rays as he was with the Mets. Despite similar contact allowed, he's given up way fewer hits on balls in play (BABIP) and fewer home runs on fly balls (HR/FB%). By comparison, Peralta had a .243 BABIP and 10.4 HR/FB% in 2025.

You would expect Peralta to have a lower BABIP with the Rays than with the Mets for two reasons. One, the Mets were a poor defensive team this season. It was appaulingly bad at times. The Rays rate as an above-average defensive team, especially in the outfield. Peralta has a low BABIP with the Rays in part thanks to his teammates, who are great at catching the baseball.

"That's a huge part, man," Peralta said about Tampa's defense Saturday. "Sometimes I face hitters that I understand in the moment that I have to -- OK, I tell myself, just whatever I'm going to throw, just put it in the zone. Just looking for the hitter to hit the ball because I know and trust all the guys I have behind me, and it's been working pretty well. I just trust them."

The HR/FB% rate is more difficult to explain. Tropicana Field is more home run-friendly than Citi Field, per Statcast's park factors, plus Peralta made road starts at Coors Field, Citizens Bank Park, and Sutter Health Park in Sacramento with the Rays. He pitched in some home run-happy parks yet gave up fewer homers. Peralta may have caught some breaks in the home run department.

Ultimately, none of this matters right now. It has no bearing on how Peralta will perform in Game 3 or a potential Game 7. His BABIP and HR/FB% rate are not guaranteed to regress back to his true talent level this week. They were low during his stint with the Rays, yes, but it happened and it helped Tampa win games. Now he and the Rays just need to continue for another start or two.

"I love that kid, man, I just do," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said about Peralta before that World Series. "If you're around him every day, like this kid is just joyful. It's just a beautiful spirit that comes to do his job. He doesn't look at himself as better than others. He's just a great teammate. If you can't be around him for a while and not feel warm ... Just a matter of time before Freddy broke through because he was great for us for so many years."
