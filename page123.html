We're one week out from Election Day. But political scams and deepfakes are already in our texts, emails and feeds.

You can’t automatically believe everything you see in the age of AI. Generative AI tools like chatbots, image and video generators make it easy for anyone to create realistic media. The capabilities of AI have improved so much that it’s nearly impossible to distinguish real from AI. That’s never been more frustrating or dangerous than it is now during election season.

The 2026 midterm election is on Nov. 3. All 435 House of Representatives seats are on the ballot, along with 33 Senate seats and many other state and local races across the country. Tech issues, such as data center construction, are popular topics among candidates and ballot measures across the country. You can check your voter registration and see who’s on your ballot now using Vote.org’s free tool.

This fall, we’ve seen a number of AI-edited political ads, with and without disclosures about their AI use. Unsurprisingly, we’ve seen AI used in some high-profile races, including to give Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff AI cosmetic procedures and to make it seem like Texas Senate candidate James Talarico had defaced a Bible. Political campaigns and groups have spent more than $16 million on these AI ads this year, a CNN analysis found.

We’ve seen how generative AI can wreak havoc during election season. During the 2024 presidential primary, many New Hampshire voters got phone calls from an AI voice replica of Joe Biden urging them not to vote. This wasn’t sanctioned by the Biden campaign, and AI robocalls are illegal, so the Democratic political consultant behind it was charged with voter suppression and fined $6 million.

Improvements to AI tech since 2024 only make for sharper swords in a bad actor’s arsenal. To protect yourself from being scammed or tricked by AI deepfakes, here are some tips.

Obvious errors like seven-fingered hands in AI images are increasingly rare as AI tech improves. That’s bad news for people trying to visually determine whether something is AI or not.

Instead, it can be more accurate to use online verification websites. These are our top choices:

These websites work by scanning the metadata of an image, video or audio file for hidden signatures indicating it was made with AI. They aren’t foolproof, but they should catch some AI media.

Other services can also identify some AI content for a price, like Pangram’s $20 per month subscription for individual users. Apple is also adding a verification measure called Reference Images, but it’s limited to its newest iPhone 18 Pro models.

Be mindful that any AI detection service claiming a 100% success rate is lying. There are too many AI services available and too little standardization across the industry to get it right all the time.

We know from decades of cybersecurity research that attackers will try to prey on your emotions to get you to fall for a scam. The goal for these bad actors is to cloud your judgment. So one of the best ways to fight back is to arm yourself with knowledge.

For example, if you get a text saying your polling location has been changed with a link to the new one, don’t automatically click it. Go to your state’s board of elections website to verify whether a change has been made – it’s going to have the most official and up-to-date information on polling locations and times.

Check out this map from the US Election Assistance Commission to see the official election contact information for your state. You can also search for your state’s board of elections or check your governor’s website, which may also have voting info readily available. Nonprofit Vote.org can also help you track down your ballot information, no matter what state you’re voting in.

If you’re looking for official policy stances on a candidate rather than relying on potentially fake images or videos, your best bet is to start with their campaign website and platform. There’s no guarantee that the candidate is going to post entirely truthful information, but it should give you some insight into their beliefs on major issues.

It’s cliché but true: Don’t believe everything you see online. Election season is a noisy time, and inevitably, you’re going to run across something that isn’t true. Use your gut and avoid panicking when possible. Fact-checking websites like Snopes.com can be useful resources.

The best way to avoid being scammed or tricked by a deepfake is to plan ahead and stay engaged: Make your voting plan now — many states offer an early voting period beginning in mid-October. Check your state’s voter ID laws. View your ballot in advance and research all the candidates on it, not just the headline races.

If you run into issues at your polling place, you can contact the Election Protection Hotline at 866-687-8683.

#1577. The stat that makes Liverpool’s Premier League campaign so confusing

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Andoni Iraola has had a good start to life in the Liverpool dugout.

Aside from Enzo Maresca, no manager who was new to a club in the summer is higher in the Premier League and while the current Manchester City boss had experience at a big six club, Iraola was making the jump from Bournemouth, where expectation and the spotlight is far smaller.

Liverpool are fourth in the league, one point shy of overachievers Brighton and already six off pace setters City and Arsenal – and yet there is one stat that sticks out like a sore thumb.

Of all 20 teams in the Premier League this season, only one has created fewer big chances than Liverpool so far. That team is Coventry, who are second bottom in the table, and Liverpool’s 11 is almost half of what Arsenal have generated. It’s almost a third of what City have created.

And yet, it is not as if Liverpool are struggling for goals. They are the ninth-highest top-scorers, so why is their big chance creation so low?

The answer is somewhere in both the tactics Iraola deploys and the tactics opponent managers use to try and stifle the most expensively assembled attacking outfit in the league.

Iraola’s style is built upon breaking quickly at pace. Opta measures direct speed which is the average speed of ball movement towards the opponent’s goal line during an attacking sequence and Liverpool are fourth in that, behind only Hull, Brentford and Coventry.

However, unlike those other three, Liverpool often face a back line that is hunkered down. Ahead of the weekend’s fixtures, Opta also measured that Liverpool faced the highest percentage of low blocks, with 28% of the attacks running into them. Conversely, they faced a high block just 15% of the time.

Whereas at Bournemouth, Iraola did not face low-sitting teams with such frequency and so Liverpool are countering only to find the opponent is already sat there waiting. It is why of the 10 fast breaks Liverpool have had this season, just one has resulted in a goal.

In recent years, countering at speed was central to Liverpool’s tactics and a front three of Roberto Firmino, Mohamed Salah and Sadio Mane were able to punish any team that lost the ball too far forward. Jurgen Klopp did though also face the issue of low blocks and the key to that then becomes your playmakers.

For Liverpool this season, Florian Wirtz is arguably the only out-and-out playmaker at the club and his price tag coupled with an underwhelming debut campaign means there is always a lot of focus on what he does.

After plenty of criticism from the likes of Jamie Carragher, his match against Brentford has been highlighted as one which supposedly ‘answered back’ after he created nine chances and yet none of these were big chances.

Of the nine key passes he made, Wirtz combined for 0.53 xA and that is essentially the story of Wirtz. He will give passes that players will shoot from but very rarely plays that killer final pass that presents a goal on a plate for an attacker.

There is another key difference between Klopp’s Liverpool and Iraola’s. Firmino was instructed to drop deep and free up space for Mane and Salah, who both played as inside forwards, to cut in. Isak is staying up high as a more normal number nine would and so Bradley Barcola and Victor Munoz are being pushed out wide.

Wirtz is a symbol of what this current Liverpool is, a team that creates chances but poor ones. According to Understat.com, Liverpool have created 11.83 xG this season, the eighth lowest in the league, but the reason why they are in fourth comes down to a different reason – they have players that are very good at scoring goals.

Alexander Isak is outperforming his xG by 1.3. Cody Gakpo, who is the player most stylistically like Mane and Salah, is also ahead as is Munoz.

Aside from the opening game against the chance-gifting Newcastle, Liverpool have not created an xG higher than 1.8 in a game this season. Against Brighton, they created a lower xG than their opponents and yet won 2-0.

If you were to make a conclusion about Liverpool, you may say this is unsustainable, and yet the opposite is likely true. When you have a squad value over €1 billion you can simply rely on your players continuing to be good, and this tactic may actually help more than hurt against bigger teams who feel more obliged to play high up the pitch.

Other than against City, who Liverpool were in control against until Ryan Gravenberch’s red card, Liverpool have played teams who will have expected to lose to them and so it will be most interesting to see how they perform against teams who would not sit back.

That first game should come on Wednesday in the EFL Cup, but the visit of Arsenal to Anfield next Sunday looks the a serious test of Iraola’s tactics.

#3285. Nvidia, AMD to invest $4 billion in Meta as tech giants deepen AI partnership

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices have said they will invest a total of $4 billion in Meta after the social media and artificial intelligence giant announced plans to raise $95 billion to support its investments in AI infrastructure.

Meta on Monday said it would initially raise $35 billion in underwritten public equity offerings, before raising an additional $60 billion with an at-the-market program.

Nvidia on Tuesday said it would invest $3 billion in Meta's equity offering, with AMD announcing it would invest $1 billion.

Meta's Monday announcement said Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom were interested in purchasing up to $5 billion in shares.

Nvidia announced its investment on Tuesday, adding that Meta would expand Vera Rubin and Vera CPU deployment, and that the two companies would deepen their partnership on Muse, the social media giant's AI agent that launched last month.

"AI agents represent a new computing paradigm — software that understands our goals, reasons, uses tools and gets work done on our behalf," said Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang in a statement.

"This changes how we use computers, from operating applications to working with intelligent agents."

"We're excited to keep working with NVIDIA to deliver the best personal agents and frontier models for billions of people," said Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg.

AMD said that its Helios racks were running at Meta and its EPYC processors were powering Meta's Muse.

"Together, we are building and deploying infrastructure at gigawatt scale and shaping future generations of silicon, software and systems to enable Meta's most ambitious superintelligence models and services," AMD CEO Lisa Su said in a statement.

AMD was up 3% on Tuesday, while Nvidia stayed flat. Meta was down 1%.

Nvidia and AMD have been rapidly building up stakes in companies powering the AI boom.

Nvidia has invested in OpenAI, Anthropic, Intel and XAI and participated in 121 investment rounds so far this year, according to Dealroom, up from just 13 in 2022.

AMD in July said it would make an equity investment of up to $5 billion in Anthropic. The company has been involved in 25 funding rounds in 2026, up from just two in 2022.

Stellar (XLM) is trading at $0.1684, and the daily chart looks vulnerable. The price fell from the October high close of $0.2370 and now sits near the bottom level of recent weeks. Sellers still have control, and the trend line signals are adding more pressure.

The main warning is the death cross. This happens when a shorter moving average drops below a longer one, and it often points to more downside. On the XLM chart, the 50-day average has crossed beneath the 100-day average and is now moving toward the 200-day line at approximately $0.19. Both crosses lining up is why the signal is called a double confirmation. All three lines are now above the price, so each could act as resistance if XLM attempts to bounce.

This leaves room for the price to fall further before a natural bounce appears.

#1262. Chinese brands drop Mbappé as backlash hits LaLiga coverage in China

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Kylian Mbappé is facing the biggest commercial crisis of his career in China. Three Chinese companies announced on Tuesday that they are ending their agreements with the Real Madrid forward, and the fallout has already reached LaLiga’s television coverage in one of football’s most valuable markets.

The trigger was a short clip linked to the French online show The Bridge. In it, Mbappé is asked which meme of himself he likes most, and he refers to a video of “a guy … I think he was a Chinese dictator”, before an edited picture of his face on the body of Mao Zedong appears, as reported by the Associated Press. The footage has not been independently verified and, according to the BBC, the show has since taken the original upload off its YouTube channel.

Beverage firm Dongpeng, the dairy group Mengniu and Milkground, which makes cheese, all issued statements on Tuesday in very similar language. None of them spelled out exactly what Mbappé had said, but each referred to hurt national feelings. Dongpeng’s statement, carried by the state-run Global Times, ended with the line: “Effective immediately, we are terminating all cooperation with Kylian Mbappé.”

Mengniu said it “firmly opposes and has zero tolerance for any words or actions that hurt the feelings of the Chinese people”. Social platform RedNote, whose own content partnership with the player ran out at the end of 2024, said it would take down related promotional material. The Global Times added that Dior, On and Hublot had removed Mbappé content from their Weibo pages, and that new users are currently unable to follow his accounts on RedNote or Weibo.

The row has even reached China’s foreign ministry. Asked about the controversy, a spokesperson said that “words and actions that hurt the feelings of the Chinese people will naturally be met with joint opposition from all sectors of Chinese society”, according to AP.

The part of the story with the widest reach for Spanish football concerns broadcasting. According to the Global Times, Shanghai outlet The Paper reported that China Media Group, the parent organisation of state broadcaster CCTV, has suspended broadcasts of matches involving LaLiga and Mbappé. CCTV’s news apps have also removed content about the striker. Neither LaLiga nor Real Madrid had commented publicly at the time of writing, and there was no indication of how long any suspension might last.

Mbappé himself has not responded either, and The Bridge has stayed silent. The timing is awkward for player and club alike. Only on Sunday he was on the scoresheet as Madrid won the Clásico 2-1 at Camp Nou, a result that came with its own fitness worries over Fede Valverde.

The “dictator” tag has followed Mbappé for some time, partly because of the influence he is said to hold at club and international level, and he has spoken about it before. In a France Football interview in September he said the memes “sometimes reveals a lack of political and human awareness among people”, as quoted by the BBC.

On the pitch, the forward has been in fine form, with goals and assists in recent weeks including the 4-1 Champions League win over RB Leipzig. Off it, the pressure is now on him to address a backlash that is still growing, with Madrid due at Racing Santander on Sunday.

#1883. Frosinone vs Lecce - prediction, team news, lineups

By Jonathan O'Shea | 27 Oct 2026 08:36

After netting four goals in consecutive games, free-scoring Frosinone will try to extend their strong start to the Serie A season when they welcome Lecce to Stadio Stirpe on Thursday.   Sitting five points above their visitors, the Canarini have surpassed all expectations since gaining promotion, and they were involved in an eight-goal thriller last time out.

Frosinone’s comeback campaign has begun in spectacular style, and the southern club sat sixth in the table coming into Serie A’s first midweek round.

Massimiliano Alvini’s men have only lost to Juventus and Napoli so far, making a big impact with their high-energy, front-foot approach.

Though they led within 30 seconds last weekend, the Canarini were trailing Atalanta 3-2 by half-time, before ultimately sharing the spoils when Aleksa Terzic scored their late leveller in a frenetic 4-4 draw.

A star in Serie B last year, opening goalscorer Giacomo Calo has now registered 17 shots and created 30 chances in Italy’s top tier, while star striker Antonio Raimondo co-leads the Capocannoniere standings.

Not only have Frosinone already put 14 points on the board, but a tally of 17 goals is highly impressive: with two more in Thursday’s match, they would set a new record for any promoted side in the 21st century.

Now, having only dropped two points on home turf, they will seek four consecutive Serie A home wins for the first time in their history.

This will be only the third top-flight meeting between Frosinone and Lecce, following two during the 2023-24 season, when the Salentini won at home and drew 1-1 at Stadio Stirpe.

Looking a little further back, Lecce are unbeaten in six against the Canarini, across Serie A, Serie B and Serie C.

The evolution of cancer genetics provides valuable insights as we consider cancer vaccine access. In 1990, a University of California, Berkeley, team led by Mary-Claire King identified the location of the BRCA1 gene, the first gene linked to hereditary breast cancer. BRCA2 was discovered five years later, and by 1996, the first commercial test for these two mutations hit the market. For the first time, people could learn whether they carried an inherited cancer risk before the disease appeared, and take potentially lifesaving action with earlier and more frequent screening, preventative surgery or prophylactic medication.

But patents and price kept testing expensive for years. Guidelines required a family history before testing. And no one in the care system clearly owned the whole process. The science arrived in the 1990s, but the delivery system never caught up. Thirty years later, the test now costs a few hundred dollars, but it’s still not reaching most patients whose cancers could have been caught earlier or prevented altogether.

If we want the coming wave of cancer vaccines to reach patients faster than BRCA testing did, there are several challenges to address now.

Today’s regulatory pathways were designed for drugs that are identical and for devices and tests that perform the same function for every patient. Forcing personalized vaccines into a traditional drug pathway has consequences. Regulatory requirements and market pressure together push drug manufacturers toward narrow indications, one tumor type and one disease stage at a time. Each narrow program carries its own trial costs, and those costs eventually show up in the therapy’s retail price.

There are early signs of movement. A forthcoming $200 million public-private partnership from the National Cancer Institute and Foundation for the NIH, called the Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines Initiative, aims to build clinical trial networks, data standards, and infrastructure to accelerate evidence development for personalized cancer vaccines and bring them to patients within the decade.

A framework that validates the neoantigen-selection pipeline and manufacturing platform, then allows faster extension to new cancers with rigorous post-market evidence, could lower costs and widen access without lowering the bar for safety.

Artificial intelligence can also cut cost and time at nearly every step in between, from tissue analysis to manufacturing quality control.

Personalized cancer vaccines are not a conventional pill or drug that can be manufactured in bulk and shipped — each vaccine is made for a specific patient’s cancer. Consider what it takes to get one patient their vaccine: A tumor sample must be collected and preserved properly at surgery. The tumor and a blood sample are then sequenced, and scientists use complex data pipelines to identify neoantigens and design the vaccine. The vaccine must then be manufactured, shipped, and administered on schedule, followed by years of monitoring.

Today, each of those steps would belong to a different organization, with its own contract, incentives, and economics, with no one accountable for the full delivery chain on something that is n-of-1 to that patient.

If we think of cancer vaccines as a drug, the cost and inconvenience of the cancer vaccine experience will drastically dilute their potential. We need to define the “product” not as what is in the vial but as an integrated end-to-end model of care that delivers a full patient experience and ensures optimal health outcomes.

For an individual patient, preventing a cancer recurrence is priceless. But to a payer, cancer vaccines will look like a very expensive form of prevention, not a required therapy to face a current disease. A health plan that expects members to churn within one to two years has little financial incentive to pay for an expensive “benefit” that another plan will collect.

Similar to cancer screenings, access becomes forced only through patient advocacy and eventual legislation. Coverage that is forced through legislation means that the friction to access the specific therapy or tool is not proactively addressed, let alone removed.

Yet the economics favor prevention of cancer recurrences. A recurrence that becomes metastatic disease can mean years of costly immunotherapy, imaging, and hospital care. Multiyear or outcomes-based contracts could let payers share in those savings, and Medicare, which covers many patients for life, doesn’t face the same churn problem. Employers and direct-to-patient programs may open further paths, though each raises equity concerns.

In practice, clinical guidelines determine what gets ordered, covered, and reimbursed. They are also slow. Many professional guidelines still have not caught up with the technological innovations that have come to market over the last 10-15 years, such as genetic and minimal residual disease testing. Personalized therapies will make guidelines even more challenging, as guideline bodies were built to evaluate a fixed product in a defined population. But if guidelines treat each new indication with a multiyear review, the new therapies will keep outrunning the recommendations.

Guideline organizations, professional societies, and payers should agree now on how they will evaluate platform therapies: what evidence is sufficient to extend a recommendation to a related setting, and how real-world data will be used to update it quickly.

At the recent STAT Summit in Boston, Gary Keblish, a New York City public school teacher, described what he called his path to a successful outcome after a high-risk melanoma diagnosis in 2019. After surgery, he enrolled in an earlier trial of intismeran autogene, and today, he is still teaching. The results presented at ESMO are not an abstraction for patients like Gary — they are the difference between planning around the next scan and planning for next year’s curriculum.

There is still much to learn about how to optimize these vaccines, which cancers they will work in, and which technologies work best. As that research continues, we must create a system within which these vaccines can reach patients as quickly as possible.

#5276. Opinion: Emergency pediatric care is now in permanent crisis mode

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Kohler is a pediatric surgeon in Sacramento, Calif. King is a pediatric intensivist in Seattle.

In the winter of 2022, two parents carried their sick, coughing baby boy into an emergency room deep in California’s Sierra Nevada mountains. The small rural hospital had no pediatric specialists, no pediatric nurses, and no children’s intensive care unit. But it was hours closer than any hospital that did.

The doctor at that rural hospital recognized immediately that the baby’s condition was beyond their capacity to treat. They called regional children’s hospitals like ours for help. But the winter of 2022 was the peak of the “tripledemic” — Covid, influenza, and RSV striking children simultaneously — and every children’s hospital in northern California was overwhelmed. None of us had room for one more.

There was no single call the team at that hospital could make to locate a pediatric ICU bed. They worked a phone tree, hospital by hospital, asking for an opening. Meanwhile, the baby’s condition worsened. As the team tried to insert an infant-sized breathing tube, the baby died.

That patient’s death and others like it were galvanizing for those of us in on the other end of those desperate calls for help. Saying no to our colleagues and the patients they’re caring for feels horrible.

But the extraordinary crush of the tripledemic has continued. In 2026, the unprecedented pediatric patient crowding of 2022 has become a normal Tuesday.

In this new normal, community hospitals across the country are transferring children to dedicated pediatric centers not just because a child is critically ill, but increasingly because smaller hospitals have quietly lost the ability to manage even common conditions. A 1-year-old with an allergic reaction, a 6-year-old with an asthma attack, a teenager with gallstones — community hospitals once handled these patients locally. Now, as they degrade or eliminate pediatric care, that local safety net is disappearing. The nation’s relatively few children’s hospitals can’t accommodate every sick child, much as we would like to. We now routinely turn sick children away and hope one of our colleagues somewhere else will have a bed.

There is another story from that same winter of 2022. Two parents brought their sick baby girl to a small rural hospital in Washington state’s Northern Cascades. For 12 hours, doctors there tried to find a hospital that could take her. Again, no children’s hospitals had room.

Finally, the emergency physician called Washington’s Regional Medical Operations Coordination Center, or RMOCC — a kind of air traffic control for patient transfers that can match critically ill patients to available beds across a region. The RMOCC pediatrician who answered later recalled the terror in the calling doctor’s voice: “Don’t you know I feel like I am having a heart attack every moment I am caring for this tiny baby? It has been 12 hours. I am terrified she will die here. Why can’t anyone help?”

The RMOCC found a hospital willing to make an exception to its infection-control policies and accept the baby into a neonatal ICU. Unlike the California baby, she lived.

The Washington RMOCC closed last year after budget cuts. It is seeking renewed funding in this year’s budget.

There are many reasons for the collapse of pediatric capacity. Most are fundamentally financial. Hospital bottom lines require high reimbursement, high volume procedures, and expensive, complex treatments. Children’s care often reimburses below the cost of care, meaning that pediatric admissions for common childhood diseases lose money. Hospitals are incentivized to abandon pediatric care in favor of complex adult surgical programs and cancer treatments.

There are three changes in health care policy that could save and restore regional pediatric capacity.

First, Medicaid payments for pediatric care need to reflect the costs of that care. Hospitals can’t be asked to lose money on pediatric admissions and ED visits. Unfortunately, coming cuts to Medicaid seem likely to make reimbursement for children’s care worse rather than better. The incoming Congress must act to preserve and augment funding for pediatric care.

Second, regional RMOCCs like Washington state’s should be a standard part of regional disaster planning and a state and national funding priority. At least 19 states had regional or statewide MOCCs in 2023, though many were open only during surges, and some, like Washington’s, wound down with the end of the pandemic. When a sick child arrives at a rural emergency department, the hospital shouldn’t have to resort to a phone tree to find an accepting specialty facility. A single phone call to a regional air traffic control system for patient movement saves time and lives.

Finally, children’s hospitals need support to be regional resources to the smaller hospitals that depend on them for advanced pediatric care. Incentives for providing telehealth consultations, training, and quality improvement support for community hospitals are essential, as children’s hospitals face the same fiscal realities as their rural peers and cannot provide infinite unfunded support to their communities. Remote support from pediatric specialists can reduce the need for transfers and make local care safer, which could drive down pediatric care costs in the long term.

In pediatric medicine, we like to say that “children are not little adults.” Unfortunately, they are also not what health care executives call “priority populations.”

But children deserve to have quality care available in their own communities. They shouldn’t have to spend hours in waiting rooms and hallways awaiting transfer to overwhelmed regional centers. As pediatric physicians, we’ve gone from seeing the crisis coming to living the crisis every day. So far that crisis has been mostly hidden within the walls of our hospitals, but it’s time everyone know: Our children are out of room.

Jonathan E. Kohler, M.D., is a pediatric surgeon in Sacramento, Calif. Mary A. King, M.D., M.P.H., is a pediatric intensivist in Seattle.
