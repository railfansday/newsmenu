#1237. Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could see drivers pay higher running costs while also increasing carbon emissions.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could yet see drivers pay higher running costs while increasing planet-heating emissions.

As part of its "Freedom Means More Affordable Cars" initiative, the US Department of Transportation this week rolled back fuel efficiency rules under the guise of lowering vehicle prices.

The Trump administration's transport agency says US taxpayers will save $138 billion (€122 billion) over the next five years — new vehicles will be $1,300 cheaper on average — since automakers will no longer have to invest in more fuel-efficient fleets.

But this does not account for soaring gas prices as Middle East conflicts choke global supply.

"Any savings on the front end get eaten up and spat out by excess costs of fuel… Fuel economy standards have saved drivers hundreds of billions of dollars," said Democratic Senator Sheldon Whitehouse from Rhode Island in a social media post.

Under the revised Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) rules, vehicle fleets only require an average fuel efficiency of about 35 miles per gallon (14.7 kilometers per liter) by 2031, down around one-third from 50 miles per gallon. Implemented by then-President Joe Biden in 2024, the updated efficiency rules aimed to incentivize the production of low emission electric vehicles and hybrids. The US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) estimates the standards would have saved 70 billion gallons (265 billion litres) of fuel over 25 years.

Meanwhile, nonprofit advocacy group the Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS) says an earlier version of CAFE regulations has saved US consumers about $321 billion at the gas pump since 2010.

As the world's biggest petrol consumer, the US burns through 20 million barrels of oil per day, which is 20% of global consumption, notes Dan Becker, director of the Safe Climate Transport Campaign at the US-based nonprofit environmental group Center for Biological Diversity.

"Transportation is the biggest consumer of that oil, making strong auto standards the most effective way to cut pollution and oil use," Becker said in a statement.

The rules that have been diluted this week would have prevented more than 710 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions by 2050 — the equivalent of running 165 million gas-powered passenger vehicles for a year.

"With transportation representing nearly 28% of US greenhouse emissions, rolling back fuel economy standards is a dangerous setback for near-term climate goals," said Ben Scott, head of Energy Demand at UK-based climate think tank Carbon Tracker. "It risks keeping inefficient combustion engines on the road much longer than climate science demands," he told DW.

But following President Donald Trump's elimination of greenhouse gas emissions standards for light- and heavy-duty vehicles last year, Dave Cooke, senior vehicles analyst for the UCS Clean Transportation Program, said the rollbacks merely compound "a much more significant step backwards."

He said the trend sends "a specific signal to automakers," adding that "the United States is seeking to maximize oil usage to increase oil company profits."

This will be to the detriment of climate targets, with high-polluting oil contributing one-third of global fossil fuel emissions that continue to rise — along with temperatures.

The world just had the hottest August on record as severe heatwaves in Europe caused more than 16,000 excess deaths in Germany alone.

Sales of low emission EVs are down in the US in 2026, especially after Donald Trump scrapped the $7,500 tax credit for new electric vehicles.

American automakers also continue to push back against regulations that would incentivize a switch to EVs, arguing there is a lack of consumer demand.

"It's highly unlikely that the United States will suddenly become awash in electric options when neither the auto industry nor regulators seem interested in providing such choices for consumers," said Cooke.

But Ben Scott believes that while softening US fuel economy standards "will create temporary headwinds", it won't stall an inevitable transition to electric vehicles.

"With EVs delivering vastly lower running costs, electrification becomes a clear-cut economic choice," he said of cars running on "cheaper, domestically secure energy that will drive the transition forward, regardless of short-term politics."

#1057. Nobel Prize in chemistry goes to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Nobel Prize in chemistry for their groundbreaking discoveries related to the origin of chirality and life's building blocks.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry on Wednesday was awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis."

Kagan, who is 95 years old and was born in France, is affiliated with France’s Université Paris-Sud, and Soai, who is 76 years old and was born in Japan, is based at the Tokyo University of Science.

The scientists share the Nobel Prize for answering longstanding questions in chemistry about life's origins.

Specifically, the duo solved an age-old mystery that has long puzzled chemists: some molecules, like amino acids, exist as two variants that are mirror images of one another, but just one of the variants out of the two is found in living organisms.

The two answered questions about why life chooses one variant over another.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the winner in Stockholm.

In chemistry, life's building blocks are what scientists call homochiral, after the Greek words for "same" and "hand."

The Nobel Committee said the chemistry prize recognizes discoveries that have enabled chemists to drive chemical reactions that lead to homochirality.

"Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously," Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said in a news release. "The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."

The duo's discoveries, the news release said, "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals."

#5737. How Sen. Tina Smith fell into politics

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

It was the summer of 1978. The Senate was holding hearings on whether to extend the deadline to ratify the Equal Rights Amendment, which was set to expire the following year. And Tina Smith, who had just finished her sophomore year at Stanford University, tumbled down the steps of the United States Capitol.

“I didn’t hurt myself, but I was absolutely mortified — and truly, every time I walk up and down the steps now, I think about that, and I say, “Be careful! Don’t fall!” the Minnesota Democrat says.

Smith was interning for California Sen. Alan Cranston at the time, running papers and manning the autopen to sign letters to constituents. Four decades later, she was appointed to the Senate herself and later secured her first full term in the 2020 elections.

Last year, Smith announced she would not seek another term. She said her decision hinged on spending more time with her family, including her four grandchildren and aging father.

“I expect to continue to be really engaged in my community, and I’m not exactly sure what that’s going to look like,” she says, adding she hopes to stay involved in combatting “income inequality and massive concentrations of wealth and power.”

Her dad has urged her to consider some leisure time, too: “He said that all sounds really good, Tina, but what are you going to do for fun?”

“[My husband] Archie and I bought a very nice tent for car camping, and we’ve got some cots that we can set up in the tent,” Smith says. “We did a lot of camping on our honeymoon many, many years ago, and then we kind of took a big 40-year break.”

This interview has been edited and condensed.

A: I remember meeting Hubert Humphrey when I was like 5 years old. He came to New Mexico, where my family lived, and for some reason I was one of the kids that was part of the photo op.

My parents were very involved in the Eugene McCarthy presidential campaign in 1968. They weren’t big national political players by any means, but they were sort of grassroots local political volunteers, and my dad was on the school board when I was in elementary school.

I was raised to understand that everybody gets to choose how they want to contribute to their community, but you need to figure out what that way is.

Q: How did you get your internship with Cranston?

A: I was a political science major at Stanford, and my big project as a senior was looking at how fossil fuel companies were leveraging their power on the brand-new Senate Energy Committee. I was also interested in how you could make government work better and actually deliver results for people. Back in the day, we called that public administration.

So of course getting an internship on Capitol Hill was a real dream of mine, and I just applied. At that point, my family lived in California, but we weren’t connected to any of the political elites in the state at all. But I did the interview, and I got the internship.

A: That summer, the Senate was considering whether to extend the deadline for ratification of the Equal Rights Amendment, and I remember going into a committee hearing room and watching and feeling like I was sort of where it happened. It was a great experience, because of course, most of my internship was sitting at the autopen desk and laboriously pushing the paper through the autopen to write Sen. Cranston’s name on the thousands of letters that we sent back to California constituents. Everything was done by paper in those days.

Q: What single moment stands out the most?

A: One day I was running around to deliver papers, and I wasn’t paying enough attention, and I tripped and fell down the steps of the United States Capitol. I was kind of a klutzy kid, and I wasn’t paying enough attention. You know how you leap up and you say “I’m fine, I’m fine”? And then you go home and lick your wounds.

Q: What has changed since the 1970s on the Hill?

A: The intensified security concerns are so different today. And the city is so different too. When I graduated from college, I came back and worked in Washington for a couple of years at the beginning of the ’80s, so I have a cluster of memories. I worked for this little company that had its headquarters on Stanton Park called the Research Council of Washington, and it became a very big company long after I left. It was founded by a guy by the name of David Bradley, who went on to become the publisher of the Atlantic and is still a really good friend.

Q: What full-circle experiences have you had as a senator yourself?

A: When I first came to the Senate in 2018, I went to my desk that I had been assigned to. It was the desk that Sen. Al Franken had sat in, and I opened it up to see who else had sat in it. I looked at the signatures, and lo and behold, there was a signature for Sen. Alan Cranston, which just felt like an incredible coincidence. I only met him maybe once or twice as an intern, and as a 19- or 20-year-old, I never imagined I would end up sitting in his desk.

I’ve always thought a lot about how I want the intern experiences in my Senate office to be. Every intern is able to spend a day shadowing me, just coming along with me wherever I’m going, as long as it’s not confidential. My own experience was limited to going to a couple of committee hearings and then working the autopen, and I wanted my interns to get a real view.

Q: What do you see as the high point of your congressional career?

A: When they passed the Inflation Reduction Act, I remember being on the Senate floor. All of the staffers who had been working so hard on the legislation, on the climate provisions, for so long were all there, and it was an incredible moment. Knowing now how hard Trump would work to try to roll back those things makes it bittersweet, but it was a real sign of what we can accomplish and what we can do to move forward.

A:  My mentor and model has, in many ways, been Walter Mondale. After he was in the Senate and vice president, and after he ran for president, he came back to Minnesota, and he continued to be a really strong voice, both in national politics as well as the person that people would call up if they wanted his advice on how to run for county attorney.

If he hadn’t been that kind of person, I might not be here today. He’s a model for me as I think about this next phase of my engagement in Minnesota and in public life. I’m a bit like a border collie. If I don’t have work to do, I’ll find work to do. If I’m not careful, before you know it, I’m power-washing the decks, or deciding that we need to cut down all the bushes in the backyard.

The bald eagle nest in Big Bear Valley has welcomed a new visitor. On Tuesday, cameras spotted an unknown adult spending time in the nest once shared by Jackie and Shadow.

A sub-adult eagle has also been spotted on FOBBV’s cameras in recent weeks. Shadow has not been seen by the cameras since October 23. Kaydee, a young adult bald eagle who has been seen arranging sticks and singing with Shadow in the past few months, was last seen on October 21.

It’s not unusual for bald eagles to spend less time in their nests in the fall. Fans often refer to this time as “bald eagle vacation,” where the birds will spend time exploring other parts of the area and sleeping in trees with more cover. Mating season for the Big Bear Valley bald eagles typically starts in December. It’s not yet known if Shadow will return to his nest or mate with a new partner following the death of Jackie on August 10.

Jackie and Shadow were not the original inhabitants of the nest seen on the FOBBV cams. Jackie’s parents, Lucy and Ricky, built the nest during the 2012-2013 nesting season. Shortly after the 2011-2012 nesting season, when Jackie was born and eventually fledged, Lucy and Ricky’s old nest blew away in a strong storm.

The old nest sat at the top of a dead tree. The new nest occupied a nearby, stronger tree about 50 feet away from where Jackie was raised. Lucy and Ricky abandoned the nest in the 2015-2016 season, opting instead to build a new nest nearby. At the beginning of the 2016-2017 nesting season, Lucy and Ricky disappeared from the area. It’s unknown where they went or what happened to them. But that same season, a juvenile female began hanging out at their old pad. Experts later guessed this was Jackie. She used this nest, first with original partner Mr. B and later with Shadow, until her death.

In summer 2026, Shadow continued to tend to the nest with the pair’s chicks, Sandy and Luna, until they fledged in mid-July. In the months that followed, he continued to visit the nest and eventually allowed Kaydee to also spend time there.

#4485. Why are bogs so good at preserving dead bodies?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 3:43 PM EDT

In August of 1984, commercial peat cutters working near Wilmslow, about a half-hour drive south of Manchester, England, discovered a dead body in the moss. The corpse was determined to be a 25-year-old man with a closely shorn beard and well-manicured nails. He had suffered two or three heavy blows to the head, and had sustained a cut throat and a fractured rib. But this wasn’t a fresh corpse: His body had been laying in that peat bog for nearly 2,000 years.

The “Lindow Man” is just one of at least 2,000 bog bodies recovered from Europe’s peatlands. They date from the Mesolithic period, which ended in Europe between 8,500 to 5,500 years ago, all the way up through World War II.

“Bog bodies” are essentially human corpses that peat bogs have naturally preserved and mummified. Quite often, their skin, hair, nails, and internal organs are remarkably intact for thousands of years. Their bones, however, are a whole other story.

To find out more about why bogs are so good at preserving bodies, Popular Science turned to author Anna North, a journalist whose fascination with peat bogs and preserved “bog bodies” inspired her national bestseller Bog Queen. This eco-historical mystery novel details what happens when a forensic anthropologist starts investigating a well-preserved Iron Age bog body, and required extensive research on North’s part. The author first got the idea for her book after coming across the Lindow Man at London’s British Museum, where he’s on permanent display.

“He doesn’t look great,” she says, “but he’s just so recognizably still a human.” The fact that North was sharing a room with this person who ate, drank, and walked the earth 2,000 years ago left an indelible mark on her. She wanted to learn more. “One of the first things I realized is that I didn’t have a good sense of what a peat bog would even look like.”

A bog or “peat bog” is an acidic, nutrient-poor wetland ecosystem. It’s made up of peat, which is ground that’s soft and spongy and consists of partially decayed plant material that builds up over hundreds or thousands of years.

“Peat is really any kind of organic matter that builds up faster than it decays,” says North. It can be grasses, reeds, roots, and shrubs. But peat mostly consists of Sphagnum moss, a large group of shaggy-looking, moisture-loving mosses.

“Sphagnum moss has the capacity to take in a lot of water and store it, so it becomes really spongy and waterlogged.” This creates an excellent environment for preservation, says North, “because there’s not much air.”

While bogs exist across the globe, you’ll find them mostly in cool, northern climates with lots of rain. For years, these mysterious landscapes have appeared as ominous settings in literature. They’re especially popular in Victorian detective fiction and gothic novels like Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s The Hounds of the Baskervilles and Daphne du Maurier’s Jamaica Inn. And for good reason. Their mist-shrouded surfaces (a result of the cool, damp air that sits above their spongy terrain), unstable footing, and stagnant waters make them natural backdrops for the unknown. Of course, there are also the bodies.

The preservation of bog bodies results from a highly-detailed balance of water source, chemistry, and vegetation.

Bogs get almost all of their water from rain and snow, which makes them low in minerals and nutrients. The water also doesn’t move thanks to the bog’s sponge-like moss.

Thick layers of decomposed organic material choke off the air supply to the decaying plant matter underneath. Without oxygen, the environment becomes highly acidic and aerobic bacteria and fungi are unable to thrive. This means they can’t break down that dead plant (and human) material the way they normally would.

Instead, the bog’s high acidity essentially “pickles” human corpses, many of which were left behind as part of a ritual sacrifice or the result of executions. The environment’s low pH (the result of its high acidity) acts like a pickle juice brine, stopping harmful bacteria from growing and preserving bodies almost like “living time capsules.” Sphagnum moss also releases a complex carbohydrate that binds to the proteins in human skin and tissue, called sphagnan. This carbohydrate turns the skin leathery and a dark-brown “tea” color while protecting it against decay. But while this unique environment slows a body’s decomposition, the bog’s intense acid leaches the calcium right out of human bones.

“If you look at X-rays of bog bodies, they’re not going to look like an X-ray of a living person,” says North. Mostly because the bones are softened, warped, or have dissolved completely.

Raised bogs, which are deep, dome-shaped peatlands that are both wetter and higher than their surrounding lands, are best at preserving bodies. That’s because they’re both highly acidic and entirely void of oxygen.

The shallower peat found in blanket bogs means that bodies decompose faster (though bog bodies are still found here). Fens, which are peat-accumulating wetlands that both  surface water and mineral-rich groundwater replenish, are less acidic. This means that a body’s soft-tissue will typically decay.

One way that forensic anthropologists can typically tell the difference between a recently decreased person and a bog body is through their teeth. While researching for her book, North discovered that people who lived and died before there was refined flour would have really different patterns of tooth decay than modern-day eaters.

Back then, she says, “They might actually grind wheat or corn with stone, and little bits of stone would end up in their flour. Those people would then chew on that stone and it would grind down their teeth.”

North also learned that identifying a bog body is a collaborative effort. A bioarchaeologist, who studies human biological remains at archaeological sites, might be brought in to determine the corpse’s age and sex, while a pathologist would examine the cause of death.

“It turns out that the same process that makes bogs really good at preserving a body also means that they’re storing this enormous amount of carbon,” says North.

Since there’s no oxygen to break down a bog’s organic plant matter, it just piles up layer by layer forming thick, carbon-rich peat. In fact, peatlands (including bogs) only cover roughly three-perfect of the planet’s land surface, though they store twice as much carbon as all the world’s forests combined. “They’re just a really powerful resource.”

Bogs are generally safe to walk across as well, despite their reputation for being “graveyards” of sorts. “There are cases of people appearing to have accidentally died on bogs,” says North. “Still, if you’re an average person visiting a bog today and you stay on the marked trail, I am not concerned for your safety.”

The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) has released the public’s best look yet at Shenlong, China’s ultra-secret space plane. Details remain scarce on the experimental reusable vehicle, but the unclassified photo offers an unprecedented glimpse of the craft from below, as well as clear views of its unique, rear-facing solar panel arrays.

The photo was taken in 2024 by the geospace intelligence company Maxar Technologies, and is wedged inside the DIA’s latest edition of its Challenges to Security in Space report. The 130-page threat assessment examines the biggest space-based issues currently facing the U.S. and its allies. Although the ever-growing cloud of orbital debris and junk above everyone’s heads remains an international problem, the report also focuses on the near- and long-term aims of countries like China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

Located on page 53 of the report, the photo’s caption simply describes it as an unnamed Chinese “spaceplane,” it is almost certainly China’s second reusable orbital vehicle, Shenlong. Very little is known about the project even nearly 20 years after China officially revealed its existence. A Shenlong prototype launched on an initial two-day orbital mission in 2020, followed by a 276-day mission in 2022. Maxar’s 2024 photo coincides with the country’s third known, 268-day orbital test of Shenlong that launched in December 2023. According to the DIA, Shenlong “released and interacted with an object,” but good luck learning much else about it.

The mysterious space plane is reportedly engaged in a fourth excursion after launching from China’s Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center on February 7, 2026. This places it alongside X-37B, the U.S. Space Force’s own secretive robot plane. Designed by Boeing, the reusable vehicle has spent 431 days and counting in low Earth orbit on its eighth classified mission. There is also a bit more available information on the solar-powered spaceplane. The vertical takeoff, horizontal-landing spaceplane measures over 29 feet long, features a nearly 15-foot-long wingspan, and reaches an orbital speed of 17,426 mph. Its full capabilities are unsurprisingly highly classified, although the U.S. Space Force did reveal its eighth mission includes testing cutting-edge laser communications technology as well as quantum measurement instruments.

#4512. Silly bear cub wins award for being terrible at hide-and-seek

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 12:57 PM EDT

Baby bears need to learn a lot during their first few summers—how to catch salmon, how to swim, how to climb trees, the importance of avoiding porcupine quills, etc. For one cub in Alaska’s Tongass National Forest, learning how to play hide & seek was added to that growing list of skills.

The United States Forest Service team at Tongass National Forest have awarded the official 2026 Worst at Hide-and-Seek Award to a baby bear who needs a bit more practice at this game. The baby bear belongs to Osso, who won the 2026 Mama Bear Award for her prowess.

“It [the cub] stole our Rangers’ hearts,” the team writes. “It looked like it wanted to play hide and seek but couldn’t fully grasp the concept. Our Rangers were glad the cub was bad at the game, because it meant they saw it more.”

Osso had three cubs this season. However, Rangers only spotted her with two cubs in August and believe that one died. Black bear cub survival rate can range from 51 to 88 percent depending on the location.

Tongass is the nation’s largest national forest and covers most of Southeast Alaska, including the capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles, and plenty of bears. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, which is home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska.

#390. Do spiders dream? Scientists just found a surprising clue

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Bonn have discovered that spiders experience distinct stages of sleep, including a REM-like state that shares surprising similarities with human sleep. During this stage, the spiders move their eyes and twitch their muscles, behaviors also associated with REM (rapid eye movement) sleep in humans and other vertebrates.

The findings provide the first comprehensive experimental confirmation that spiders experience genuine, regulated sleep rather than simply remaining inactive. They also raise intriguing questions about how sleep evolved and whether animals with very different nervous systems might share some of its fundamental features.

Researchers at the University of Bonn's Institute of Organismic Biology made the discovery while studying the Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata, a small but remarkably sophisticated predator.

A Tiny Spider With Remarkable Hunting Skills

The Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata is only about the size of a one-euro coin when fully grown, but its hunting abilities make it particularly interesting to scientists.

Unlike many spiders that primarily capture insects, members of the genus Portia specialize in hunting other spiders. They use a variety of sophisticated strategies to catch their prey, including imitating the vibrations made by struggling insects trapped in spiderwebs.

These deceptive signals can lure web-building spiders toward what appears to be an easy meal, only for them to encounter the hunting Portia instead.

Such complex behaviors suggest that these spiders have unusual learning abilities, making them valuable subjects for investigating the relationship between sleep, nervous system function, and cognition.

Scientists Discover Two Distinct Stages of Spider Sleep

Although sleep has been extensively studied in humans and other vertebrates, scientists still know relatively little about how it works in animals with very different nervous systems.

To investigate whether spiders experience genuine sleep and whether that sleep includes different stages, the researchers closely monitored Portia fimbriata over several days and nights.

They discovered that the spiders exhibit two clearly distinguishable sleep stages. One of these resembles REM sleep, the phase in humans associated with rapid eye movements, muscle activity, and vivid dreaming.

During this REM-like stage, the spiders displayed characteristic eye movements and muscle twitches. These observations suggest that some features of sleep previously studied mainly in vertebrates may also occur in spiders.

The researchers emphasize that identifying REM-like movements does not establish whether spiders dream. However, the discovery provides important evidence that sleep in these animals is more complex than previously understood.

How Researchers Confirmed That Spiders Really Sleep

To study the spiders' sleeping behavior, the team placed individual Portia fimbriata spiders in plastic dishes measuring 12 centimeters in diameter. They monitored the animals under different lighting conditions to examine their daily activity and rest cycles.

Infrared cameras allowed the researchers to observe the spiders throughout the night without relying on visible light. Detailed recordings captured their sleeping positions, periods of quiet rest, and episodes of REM-like movement.

The team also conducted experiments to determine whether the spiders were genuinely asleep rather than simply sitting motionless.

Using sound stimuli, the researchers measured how quickly the spiders responded to disturbances. These tests helped distinguish sleep from ordinary inactivity.

Another important part of the investigation involved sleep deprivation. The researchers examined whether the spiders displayed "sleep rebound," meaning they compensated for interrupted sleep by increasing their subsequent sleep.

Together, the behavioral observations and experimental tests provided strong evidence that Portia fimbriata experiences regulated sleep with distinct stages.

Studying Spider Sleep Presented Unusual Challenges

Determining whether a spider is truly asleep is considerably more complicated than observing a sleeping human.

Because spiders sense their surroundings differently from people, the researchers first had to identify a sound stimulus that would produce a consistent response during their experiments.

They also needed to avoid assuming that the observed eye movements and muscle twitches necessarily represented the same processes seen during human REM sleep.

One major limitation was that the researchers could not measure the spiders' brain activity. Instead, they relied entirely on carefully documented behavior to identify different sleep states.

This required extensive high-resolution video recordings collected over many days and nights, producing enormous amounts of data for analysis.

Despite these obstacles, the combination of behavioral monitoring, response testing, and sleep disruption experiments allowed the researchers to build a detailed picture of how these spiders sleep.

What Spider Sleep Could Reveal About Evolution

The discovery has implications that extend well beyond spiders.

Most scientific knowledge about sleep comes from studying vertebrates, particularly mammals and birds. Finding comparable sleep features in animals with radically different nervous systems could help researchers understand when and why different types of sleep evolved.

The results also open new opportunities in behavioral science, neuroscience, and chronobiology, the study of biological rhythms.

By investigating sleep in a wider variety of animals, scientists hope to identify features that are shared across species and uncover the fundamental biological functions that sleep may have served early in evolutionary history.

The researchers argue that future investigations should move beyond traditionally studied animals and include species with unusual adaptations or much simpler nervous systems.

Such comparisons could reveal whether certain sleep mechanisms are widespread throughout the animal kingdom or developed independently in different evolutionary lineages.

Even after decades of intensive research, many basic questions about sleep remain unanswered, including important mysteries surrounding human sleep.

Could Spiders Dream? Scientists Have More Questions

The University of Bonn team is now expanding its research to investigate how sleep, particularly REM-like sleep, varies across different spider species.

Scientists are also comparing spiders sleeping in natural environments with those observed in laboratories to determine how surroundings influence their behavior.

Another major goal is to investigate the relationship between sleep and cognitive abilities. Researchers want to understand what happens inside the brains of spiders and insects during sleep, and whether different sleep stages contribute to learning and other mental processes.

Environmental changes are also becoming an important focus. The team plans to examine how climate change and human activities, especially artificial light at night (ALAN), may interfere with the sleep patterns of spiders and other arthropods.

For now, the findings establish that even a tiny jumping spider can experience a surprisingly complex form of sleep. Whether spiders actually dream remains unknown, but their nighttime behavior is offering scientists new clues about one of biology's most enduring mysteries.

The Lumina Project spans 2 billion years and will allow scientists to test theories and predictions about the origins of the cosmos.

The origin of the universe is one of the greatest scientific mysteries of all time. Scientists have developed numerous theories and testable predictions about how the universe began, including the Big Bang model, cosmic inflation and quantum-cosmology proposals.

To test those theories, researchers from the Center for Astrophysics, a collaboration between Harvard and the Smithsonian, assembled the Lumina Project, a simulation of the early universe far larger than any prior one.

The details of the simulation, published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (PDF), describe how regular matter, dark matter and radiation likely interacted during the first 2 billion years of the universe, covering a region equivalent to a cube roughly 1.5 billion light-years on each side.

For reference, the prior champion for the largest early-universe simulation was the Thesan simulation in 2022, which simulated about a billion years of the early universe across a region roughly equivalent to a 300-million-light-year cube.

It does all of this in incredible detail, largely due to observations made by NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope and other telescopes that spend considerable time observing the oldest celestial objects. By looking at those ancient space objects, scientists have been able to piece together at least some ideas of what the early universe may have looked like.

“With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics,” said Oliver Zier, lead author of the study, in a statement. “To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions,” Zier said.

The simulation was made on the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee. Frontier has around 37,000 GPUs across its system, and the Lumina Project used about 15,000 of them.

That’s what helped the simulation become the largest and most detailed simulation ever made, giving researchers plenty of unique features to work with.

One example is helium reionization, a process that scientists believe took place in the early universe. Back then, the universe was so hot that virtually all matter was ionized, meaning it consisted of charged particles. When the universe cooled about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, atoms started combining together to make regular hydrogen and helium. Then, a short time later (comparatively speaking), the first generation of stars emerged and emitted enough ultraviolet light to reionize nearby hydrogen and helium atoms.

“Lumina follows both hydrogen and helium reionization,” Zier told CNET in an email. “This allows us to study these processes together in one self-consistent simulation, meaning it captures reionization as it occurred while allowing its effects to immediately influence the evolving universe within the model.”

Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas and an author on the paper, said the team is incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina. “For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation,” Smith said in a statement.

The main function of Lumina is to help scientists test and confirm theories about the early universe and gauge whether their understanding of physics aligns with the simulation’s results.

The team has already used the simulation to make some interesting predictions, including that there were “fewer massive galaxies” in the early universe than James Webb Space Telescope observations suggest. The simulation also houses the mysterious little red dots spotted by JWST, which some scientists now believe are black hole stars.

A side benefit is that the simulation has some truly gorgeous imagery, showing off a lot of interactions of the early universe, including gases at various temperatures mixing together, chemical reactions and the locations of early galaxies and stars. Since the simulation also fills in where dark matter and dark energy would be, the entire thing is a gorgeous litany of colorful clouds, threads and filaments.

#473. NASA SEES Interns Tackle Real-World Challenges in Lunar Exploration

The NASA STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) Enhancement in Earth Science (SEES) Internship Program at the University of Texas Center for Space Research places high school students on small, mission-focused research teams mentored by NASA, academic, and industry experts. During summer 2026, the SEES Mission Architecture Tool for Research, Integration, and eXploration (MATRIX) team focused on applying a method for measuring propellant mass in microgravity, where liquid fuel clings to tank walls instead of settling – a challenge central to NASA’s Artemis missions.

The MATRIX team was mentored by Dr. Kevin Crosby, who, along with colleagues at NASA, developed the Modal Propellant Gauging (MPG) approach to microgravity propellant mass gauging at Carthage College in Kenosha, WI. MPG uses the acoustic response of propellant tanks to estimate the mass of fuel inside a tank. The method has been used successfully in several spaceflight experiments and is scheduled for demonstration on the Intuitive Machines IM-3 mission. The IM-3 lander, Trinity, is scheduled to touch down on the Moon in the Reiner Gamma region in 2027. The team developed models for predicting when MPG can take reliable readings during the mission, giving the interns direct experience with a technology preparing for spaceflight and lunar surface operations.

Reconstructing a Lunar Mission from Public Data

The MATRIX team relied entirely on publicly available sources, including Intuitive Machines’ published mission data. The NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory’s Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility (NAIF) provided detailed trajectory records for the IM-1 and IM-2 missions. By comparing the IM-2 spacecraft’s reconstructed flight path with a model of its motion without engine thrust, the team identified engine burns and estimated the change in velocity associated with each burn.

Because a complete IM-3 trajectory is not public, the team wrote a reconstruction algorithm using published IM-1 and IM-2 flight data. To model fluid behavior, the team ran computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations to estimate slosh dynamics within the tanks and conducted Monte Carlo simulations to predict the range of possible propellant states.

The team’s analysis showed that MPG can take reliable readings during the two primary burns, Lunar Orbit Insertion (LOI) and Powered Descent, and in some cases during Descent Orbit Insertion. The spacecraft’s starting mass had the greatest effect on how long each gauging window lasts. The MATRIX team also identified candidate IM-3 launch opportunities for 2027 and produced a landing analysis for the Reiner Gamma site that showed the distribution of possible landing sites near the target.

The team briefed the Intuitive Machines propulsion group, walking the engineers through their methods and results and demonstrating that a rigorous analysis of an active lunar mission could be built from public data. The students presented their research, “Computational Models to Estimate Modal Propellant Gauging (MPG) Windows on the Intuitive Machines IM-3 Mission,” at the SEES Annual Symposium and the Wisconsin Space Grant Consortium Annual Conference. The team will present again at the American Geophysical Union (AGU) Annual Meeting in San Francisco in December 2026.

"This high school team, in a period of less than a month, developed a depth of understanding and facility with advanced methods that rivals advanced graduate students in the field," said Crosby. The SEES program has consistently provided a unique environment for students to demonstrate they can achieve far more than what is asked of them, even in the most advanced classes."

#4601. Earth’s first fully-marine amphibian stalked the seafloor with a chunky, bony body

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 12:00 PM EDT

Nearly 237 million years ago, a spiky and toothy bottom dweller skulked the Late Triassic oceans that once covered present-day China. What sets this newly discovered giant salamander-sized prehistoric amphibian apart is not its impressive body–it’s that it lived in the ocean. The newly described Gerrothorax maritimus is the first example of a fully marine amphibian in the fossil record, according to a study published today in the journal Nature Ecology & Evolution.

There are currently over 8,000 species of living amphibians, and none have been known to live in fully marine (salt water) environments. Less than two percent like crab-eating frogs (Fejervarya cancrivora) and some spotted salamandars have any degree of salt tolerance. Other four-limbed animals, including birds, reptiles, and mammals, all have species who can survive without any food or water from land. There was also no evidence of a fully marine amphibian in the fossil record. Until now.

In this study, a team from the University of California, Davis in the United States and Peking University, and the Geological Museum of Guizhou in China analyzed two nearly complete skeletons found in southern China’s Guizhou Province. The fossils were found in an area once covered by ocean and hundreds of miles from any large land mass.

“This area is part of an ecosystem that we’ve been looking at and trying to reconstruct,” Ryosuke Motani, a study co-author and paleontologist at UC Davis, said in a statement. “We’ve been mostly studying marine reptiles and the fish from this place, but now we also have this amphibian and it’s a sort of very unusual member of the lineage.”

The well-preserved skeletons helped the team reconstruct what Gerrothorax maritimus looked like and offer clues to its marine life. Notably, it had a flattened body covered in bony armor. This armor likely defended it against predators like the 10-foot-long ichthyosaur that stalked these seas millions of years ago. Gerrothorax maritimus itself was roughly three feet long with a disc-shaped body.

“If you think about today’s amphibians, this animal is unusual because it’s big,” Motani said. “It’s probably, body mass-wise, about the same as the giant salamander, but what’s different is that giant salamanders are elongated whereas this animal is really chunky.”

Based on Gerrothorax maritimus’  bony armor and the shape of its skull, the team believes that it was a bottom-dwelling suction feeder, similar to the wobbegong, a family of 12 bottom-dwelling carpet shark species alive today.

“They would probably just wait at the bottom and then when prey comes around, they would just suck it in,” said Motanti.

Modern amphibians can’t live in marine environments because their skin does not tolerate salt. The skin is permeable, and allows them to use it for multiple purposes like respiration. Amphibians don’t drink or breathe through the same mouth opening since they can do both just using their skin.

Due to this salt-intolerant biology, they must live in or near freshwater. While some modern species like the crab-eating frog have some degree of salt tolerance, they will lose weight in brackish waters and must recover in freshwater.

The researchers propose that bony armor and a more fish-like body helped Gerrothorax maritimus circumvent this limitation. The armor would have kept its skin from absorbing water, meaning that it needed to drink salt water with its mouth, and then absorb it through its digestive tract. The creature’s gills would have allowed it to filter out the salt. Other species in the Gerrothorax also have gills.

According to the team, the fossils’ location within deep sea sediments and the fact that they were nearly complete led them to believe that Gerrothorax maritimus was an ocean-dweller.

“Carcasses at sea, unless they sink to the bottom right away, tend to dry out and lose limbs prior to sinking to the bottom,” said Motani. “But these animals had their fingers preserved, so that means they probably died near where they lived.”

#4144. New 3D thermal invisibility cloak makes objects disappear from infrared cameras

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed and tested the first three-dimensional thermal invisibility cloak, a device that can conceal objects from infrared cameras while protecting them from extreme temperatures. The breakthrough could lead to new ways to cool microchips, safeguard delicate electronics, and prevent equipment from being detected through its thermal signature.

The cloak can conceal objects with a wide variety of shapes, regardless of the direction from which heat approaches. Earlier thermal cloaks were limited to two dimensions or worked only when heat traveled in a particular direction. The new design overcomes these restrictions by directing heat around a hidden object rather than simply blocking it. As a result, infrared imaging shows a temperature pattern that makes the object appear to be absent.

The research was conducted by University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign civil and environmental engineering professor Shelly Zhang, postdoctoral researcher Weichen Li, and graduate student Yibo Wang, in collaboration with professor Ole Sigmund of the Technical University of Denmark. Their findings were published in Nature Communications.

"A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from," Zhang said. "Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected."

A New Material Makes 3D Thermal Invisibility Possible

Creating a thermal cloak that functions in three dimensions has been a longstanding challenge. Previous experimental devices could redirect heat only across a flat surface or along one direction of heat flow, leaving them far short of the capabilities needed for a fully three-dimensional cloak.

To overcome these limitations, the researchers revisited the foundational principles of transformation thermotics, a field that explores how heat can be redirected through specially designed materials. They focused on a central question: "What kind of material structure could cover almost all the thermal properties needed for a perfect cloak?"

The solution was a new lattice-based material whose structure can be independently adjusted along three directions. These changes allow the researchers to control how efficiently heat passes through different parts of the cloak. Compared with previous designs, the material can achieve a much broader range of thermal conductivities, closely approaching the properties predicted to be necessary for ideal thermal cloaking.

Importantly, the researchers went beyond computer simulations and constructed a working device. They used 3D metal printing to produce an intricate aluminum lattice, which provides pathways for efficient heat conduction. They then used mold casting to fill the remaining spaces with a rubber-like material that conducts heat poorly. Together, these materials create a structure that can carefully control the movement of heat.

Infrared Camera Tests Confirm the Cloak Works

To evaluate the device, the team positioned it between hot and cold regions, establishing a temperature difference that caused heat to flow through the surrounding environment. They monitored the resulting temperature patterns with an infrared camera to determine whether the cloak could successfully redirect heat around the concealed area.

The experiments showed that the device could guide heat around the hidden region while preserving the surrounding temperature pattern. To an infrared camera, the area appeared much as it would if no object were present. At the same time, temperatures inside the cloak remained uniform, shielding the concealed space from the extreme conditions outside.

The researchers also tested whether the cloak could accommodate unusually complicated objects. They successfully concealed intricate three-dimensional geometries, including detailed shapes resembling human heads. According to the team, earlier experimental thermal cloaks have not demonstrated a comparable combination of geometric complexity and cloaking performance.

Potential Applications in Electronics and Stealth Technology

The ability to control heat in three dimensions could have important implications across several industries. In electronics, the technology could help engineers direct heat away from sensitive components, improve thermal management in microchips, and protect devices operating under demanding conditions.

The researchers also see potential applications in security and defense. Future versions of the technology might help conceal people or equipment from thermal detection systems or shield valuable assets from severe temperature conditions. These uses would build on the device's demonstrated ability to redirect external heat while keeping the protected region thermally stable.

"Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work," Zhang said. "But we also see it more broadly: it's about hiding and protecting information that is carried by heat."

The Next Challenge Is Hiding Objects That Generate Heat

Although the current cloak represents a major advance, the researchers want to develop more sophisticated versions capable of performing additional functions. One particularly important goal is to conceal objects that produce heat themselves, rather than only protecting them from heat originating outside the cloak.

Achieving this would require materials that can actively control heat within the concealed region. Instead of simply redirecting incoming heat, a future cloak would need to concentrate, distribute, or guide thermal energy as conditions change. Such capabilities could expand the technology's usefulness in electronics, thermal protection, and applications involving infrared detection.

"We've shown that a true 3D omnidirectional thermal cloak is possible," Zhang said. "The next step is to make cloaks that don't just hide and protect, but also actively manipulate heat in useful ways."

Zhang is also affiliated with mechanical science and engineering and the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

#4203. Scientists overcome a hidden obstacle to more powerful quantum computers

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Silicon spin qubits could play a major role in the future of quantum computing because they can be produced using the same semiconductor manufacturing methods that make today's computer chips. However, researchers have faced a significant challenge. When multiple silicon qubits are controlled at the same time, their operations tend to become less accurate. Building practical, large quantum computers will require scientists to control many qubits simultaneously while keeping errors to a minimum.

Researchers at the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and their collaborators have now made important progress toward solving this problem. In a study published in Nature Physics, the team demonstrated highly accurate control of several silicon spin qubits operating simultaneously. Their experiments used an array of five qubits connected to a shared microwave control line. By identifying a major cause of errors during simultaneous operation, the scientists developed a technique to reduce those errors and maintain precision.

Leon Camenzind, a RIKEN research scientist and co-first author who supervised the experiments, explains the significance of the results: "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Scientists Identify a Hidden Source of Quantum Computing Errors

Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, that can exist in combinations of both states. Silicon spin qubits store this information using the spin of individual electrons confined within silicon. Performing reliable quantum calculations requires extremely precise control over these electron spins.

Microwave signals provide that control, but operating multiple qubits simultaneously can create unwanted effects that interfere with their accuracy. The researchers traced much of this problem to a phenomenon called the AC Stark shift.

This effect occurs when microwave control signals alter a qubit's effective energy levels, producing changes in the phase of its quantum state. Those phase shifts can disrupt the intended operation of individual qubits, particularly when several are being controlled at once.

To counteract the problem, the team created a calibration method that compensates for these unwanted shifts. Rather than separately calibrating every possible combination of qubits, the technique relies on calibrations performed between pairs of qubits. This makes the approach more practical for systems containing larger numbers of quantum bits.

"Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his PhD research.

Quantum Qubits Achieve Accuracy Above 99.99%

The researchers combined their calibration technique with an improved device design and carefully adjusted microwave control pulses. Their results showed that exceptionally high accuracy could be maintained even when multiple qubits were operating simultaneously.

When the five qubits were tested individually, each achieved single-qubit gate fidelity well above 99.99%, with the highest approaching 99.999%. Fidelity describes how closely a quantum operation matches its intended result.

The team then tested how well the system performed when several qubits were active together. With as many as three qubits operating simultaneously, fidelity remained above 99.99%. Even when all five qubits were controlled at once, accuracy stayed at approximately 99.9%, approaching the levels needed for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Although the calibration method greatly reduced errors, it did not eliminate them entirely. The researchers found that the remaining loss of accuracy was linked to drive-induced decoherence, an effect caused by the greater microwave power required to operate multiple qubits simultaneously.

Unlike the unwanted phase shifts corrected by the new technique, decoherence involves the loss of the delicate quantum coherence needed for reliable operations.

According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

A New Path Toward Larger Quantum Processors

The findings show that controlling several silicon spin qubits at the same time does not necessarily require a major sacrifice in accuracy. By correcting a key source of errors, the researchers have demonstrated a promising approach for operating more qubits through shared microwave control lines.

This is especially important for the development of large quantum processors. Giving every qubit its own independent control system would make increasingly complex devices difficult to manage. Shared control lines could offer a more practical way to expand these systems while maintaining the precision needed for quantum calculations.

"By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind. "Our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

The broader objective is to develop quantum computers that can reliably perform calculations despite the errors and disturbances that affect quantum systems. Such machines, known as fault-tolerant quantum computers, will require precise, dependable control over large numbers of qubits.

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

#4648. Why do dead bodies move and make noise?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 25, 2026 7:54 AM EDT

Death is supposed to be the end of the road. The lights are out, the curtain has fallen. No breathing, no heartbeat—end of discussion, right? Not quite.

In some cases, corpses twitch, flex, shift, change positions, or even make noises that could easily be mistaken for messages from beyond the grave. But before you call the Ghostbusters, take a deep breath. The explanation is less zombie apocalypse and more basic biology.

Even after the heart stops beating, the body still has a few tricks left up its sleeve. The results can be creepy, sure, but experts say they’re perfectly natural. Here’s why corpses sometimes move and make noise.

Our bodies can continue moving long after death. When researchers in Australia used time-lapse cameras to keep tabs on a decomposing human body, they discovered that it continued to shift for at least 16 months, with some parts moving as much as 20 total inches over the course of the study period.

Researchers call this “post-mortem movement”—and it’s a lot less dramatic than you might imagine.

“A body can move, but not necessarily in the way that I think most people envision,” Derek Boyd, a biological anthropologist who co-directs the Forensic Anthropology and Bioarchaeology Laboratory at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, tells Popular Science.

“The movements are very subtle—you’re not going to see a body sit up or turn its head.”

After death, the human body moves through a pretty predictable series of decomposition stages. One of the earliest is rigor mortis, when the muscles stiffen and the body becomes rigid. As this process unfolds, a corpse may shift, creating the appearance of movement—but it’s easy to miss, Boyd says.

“You’re not necessarily going to see [the movement] unless you’re laser-focused on the body for the first 48 hours—and even then it’s just going to look like tension, just the body stiffening,” he says.

The next stages in which movement might occur are boat and post-bloat, says Boyd. When someone dies, the body’s own enzymes begin breaking down its cells, a process known as autolysis.

Meanwhile, bacteria and microbes start spreading throughout the body, digesting tissues and producing gases. This process, known as putrefaction, can “cause the body to become distended or start to bloat, and that bloating can result in a dramatic expansion,” he says. “The body inflates in various places. But, again, this is something that happens gradually.”

Eventually, those gases escape—and the body gradually deflates, sometimes causing small shifts in its position.

Finally, in the advanced stages of decomposition, the body’s soft tissues begin to dry out, a process known as desiccation. “We have ligaments that are attaching bones together, tendons connecting muscle to bone, and in these later stages of decomposition, those connective tissues can shrink and tighten—and, in doing so, they can pull on our skeleton in various ways,” he says.

Of course, not all postmortem movement comes from decomposition itself. Outside forces can also cause a body to shift or change position. Scavengers may drag or disturb remains while feeding, for example, while insects can move small body parts or contribute to the breakdown of tissues. Environmental factors, such as wind, flowing water, or shifting soil, can also displace remains over time.

While we’re alive, our bodies are constantly making noise—burps, farts, stomach gurgles, and growls, creaks, and cracks. Considering how noisy our living bodies can be, it shouldn’t come as much of a surprise that they can continue making noises after death.

As gases build up during putrefaction, for instance, the body can produce gurgles, bubbles, groans, or even noises that sound like sighs, Sarah Chavez, executive director of the Order of the Good Death, a nonprofit that aims to change how people think about death and make end-of-life care and funeral options more accessible and equitable, tells Popular Science. Fluids and gases can also get pushed through the mouth or nose, which can sometimes create surprising sounds, she says.

“It can be startling if you’re not expecting it, but there’s nothing supernatural happening,” Chavez adds. “The body is just continuing to do what bodies do—break things down, move fluids and gases around, and occasionally make unintentional noises.”

Ultimately, the fact that bodies can move or make noises after death is a reminder that the human body is “a beautiful ecosystem of its own,” says Boyd.

In life, our bodies are home to countless microorganisms that interact with one another and help keep us alive. After death, those same microorganisms continue to play a role in the body’s breakdown, creating the conditions that can cause a twitch, a shift in position, or an unexpected sound.

The body no longer has thoughts, feelings, or aspirations, but it remains a complex biological system in its own right. “We are walking ecosystems, and we remain ecosystems even after death,” says Boyd.

And scientists are still learning what these postmortem ecosystems can tell us. For example, Boyd says researchers are exploring whether changes to the body’s microbial communities could help forensic investigators estimate how much time has passed since someone died—a “microbial clock” of sorts. In other words, the very same processes that can make a corpse move or make noises may also offer valuable clues about the biology of death itself.

#568. Scientists discover a hidden flaw that challenges 40 years of superconductor science

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Warwick have uncovered a surprising secret inside a superconductor that researchers have studied for 40 years. Long believed to have a uniform crystal structure, the material actually contains a complex patchwork of different atomic arrangements. Using an advanced 3D imaging technique, the team was able to see deep inside the crystal for the first time, revealing hidden features that challenge decades of scientific assumptions.

High-temperature superconductivity ranks among the most important scientific breakthroughs of the past four decades. Superconductors can carry electrical current without any resistance, eliminating the energy losses that normally occur as electricity travels through conventional materials. They also have the potential to support extraordinarily powerful magnets, making them promising for future power grids, medical scanners, and quantum computers.

The new findings, published in Physical Review Letters, come from researchers in Warwick's Department of Chemistry working with the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in France. Their investigation focused on a class of copper-based superconductors known as cuprates, which can conduct electricity without resistance at unusually high (though still very cold) temperatures.

Advanced 3D Imaging Reveals a Hidden Crystal Structure

For four decades, scientists have generally built their theories of cuprate superconductivity around the assumption that these materials have the same crystal structure throughout their interiors. The new research shows that this fundamental assumption does not always hold.

To investigate, the team used scanning 3D X-ray diffraction, a sophisticated imaging method that reveals how atoms are arranged deep within a crystal. The approach is similar in principle to a medical CT scan, except that it maps atomic structures rather than tissues and organs.

This allowed the researchers to construct a detailed three-dimensional picture of the superconductor's interior, exposing structural variations that conventional techniques had not revealed.

Instead of finding a uniform crystal, they discovered a patchwork of regions containing two slightly different atomic arrangements. Even more surprising were the boundaries separating these regions, which were hundreds of times wider than expected.

These unusually broad transition zones were not simply narrow dividing lines between neighboring structures. They were extensive enough to behave almost like distinct structures themselves, potentially influencing how electricity moves through the material.

A 40-Year Assumption About Superconductors Overturned

Professor Mark Senn, who led the study at the University of Warwick's Department of Chemistry, explained why the findings could have major implications for superconductivity research.

"For forty years, the working assumption has been that these materials are the same all the way through, and nearly all the theory is built on that picture. We've shown it doesn't hold. The crystal is fundamentally patchworked and textured, with unusually wide boundary regions that likely work against superconductivity rather than just sitting alongside it.

"This might explain why some materials perform better than others and means some existing bulk measurements will need to be reinterpreted and future theoretical models built that incorporate this structural complexity."

The discovery suggests that the internal boundaries may actively interfere with superconductivity rather than simply coexist with it. If that is the case, the hidden structural differences could help explain why certain superconducting materials perform better than others.

It also means that some previous experiments, particularly those measuring the overall properties of these materials, may need to be reconsidered. Future theories will likely need to account for the complex structures inside real crystals rather than treating them as perfectly uniform.

What the Discovery Means for Future Superconductors

The researchers suspect that similar hidden structural patterns may be widespread among other cuprate superconductors. They could also occur in related materials being investigated for their ability to become superconducting under extreme pressure.

Beyond challenging existing theories, the study demonstrates the power of a new generation of imaging technology.

The advanced measurements were made possible by a recent 150 million euro upgrade to the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, which provides extremely intense X-ray beams for studying materials in extraordinary detail.

This technology now allows scientists to examine the three-dimensional interiors of many different materials with a level of precision that was previously unavailable. By revealing how hidden atomic structures affect a material's behavior, the technique could provide valuable insights across materials science.

For superconductivity research, the implications are particularly important. Understanding how internal crystal structures influence electrical performance could eventually help scientists identify ways to improve superconducting materials, bringing more advanced energy systems, powerful magnets, medical imaging devices, and quantum technologies closer to reality.

They survive exclusively on blood and can only go about two nights without a blood meal before they die. To get around this, they rely on their social networks.

“They have the equivalent of friends. Basically, if a bat comes back from a night of foraging and has not been successful, other members of that social group will share food,” Page explains. “They’ll regurgitate blood for the bat that came back hungry.”

If that donor bat has difficulty getting food later, the one recipient will often become the donor. “So there’s this very reciprocal relationship,” says Page.

According to Page, bats will have their distinctive personalities just like people. It’s present across species, but also individual bats as well.

“You have some species that are very feisty, and they’ll go out of their way to pick a fight, and then other ones that are much more passive, calm, and very gentle,” she says.

At the edge of Panama’s Soberanía National Park, tiny, fruit-eating bats will take over the roost of larger, carnivorous bats. The Seba’s short-tailed bat (Carollia perspicillata) weighs roughly the same as a strawberry. By comparison, the frog-eating, fringed-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) weighs roughly as much as an apricot and twice as much as the short-tailed bat.

Page and her colleagues in Panama have witnessed fights between the two species from roosts set up throughout the forest.

“Basically, it was the male Seba’s short-tailed bat who was instigating the fight. He was coming over and was kind of boxing and threatening much larger bats with wide open mouths and lots of teeth,” Page recalls. “And he just kept at it, and then finally, this group of fringed-lipped bats left.”

Even in the thick jungles of Panama, bats can find motionless insects on leaves. Initially, scientists believed that the thick tree cover would give small insects an “acoustic camouflage” that would confuse a bat’s echolocation system. But they were wrong.

The bats can see through this acoustic camouflage by changing the angle at which they approach the still insect.

“If you approach a flat leaf at just the right angle, you can actually get an echolocation signature,” Page explains. “You can get an echolocation signal that jumps back at you, but only if you direct your echolocation call at just the right angle.”

That’s exactly what the bats were doing in order to find insects who tried to hide in leaves.

When it’s time for the babies to wean or leave the roost, mother tent-making bats (Uroderma bilobatum) will physically prod their young with their forearms.

“There is this interesting tug that you see across so many different species. For birds, when they’re ready to fledge, there’s this sort of uncertainty.”

When sick, humans and even non-human primates change their behavior. We isolate so that the disease does not spread, and will look to plants and other sources of medicine for relief. Bats appear to change their diet to less nutritious leaves. While this may sound backwards, it’s in the hope that they have medicinal properties.

In an ongoing study with a team from the Ukrainian Bat Rehabilitation Center, the Smithsonian team is looking at the bats from Barro Colorado Island in Panama.

“We have fecal samples from these bats, so we were able to see exactly what they are eating,” says Page.

In addition to changing their diets, vampire bats will self-isolate when they are sick and others will act in a way familiar to any human parent—continuing to care for our young, even when sick.

“You don’t do as many of the normal behaviors unless it’s taking care of your babies,” says Page. “And again, it’s like a human relationship. If your little kid has a fever, you’re not going to shut them in a room and leave them. You’re not going to desert them.”

To find dinner, the fringe-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) will listen in on their prey’s mating calls. These frog-eating bats eavesdrop on the mating calls that the male frogs use to attract females. They then use the calls to assess if the frog is edible or poisonous before going in for the kill.

“They’ve really cracked this communication of another entire group,” says Page.

According to Page, it’s similar to being in a quiet room and hearing someone open up a bag of potato chips and is loudly eating. The noise makes everyone pay attention. “Bats attend to those acoustic signals of other bats, and that attention then helps them learn,” Page says. “They can learn those distinctions of palatable versus poisonous, and they can learn that socially as well from observing the other behaviors with bats.”

#552. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

When they conducted drone imagery to check in on their results this year, they found that the eelgrass meadow coverage had increased by 178 percent.

“It needs to survive for three years before we can really trumpet our horns and say it’s working, but the actual method of putting clams out and seeds attached to them really worked. The generation of the plants was incredible,” Lucey explains.

A single bat colony contains hundreds or even thousands of the winged mammals. But with less-than-ideal eyesight and so many neighbors, how do mothers identify their pups amid the extremely crowded conditions? Writing in a new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, researchers say it often comes down to the subtle, unique vocalizations emitted by their individual offspring. To assess their responses, the team relied on an even more delicate biological signal—the mother bat’s own heartbeat.

The investigation began when biologists at Japan’s Doshisha University noticed a surprising—but consistent—behavior among the Japanese house bats (Alionoctula abramus) in their laboratory colony. Whenever a pup wandered too far from its roosting area, only its mother raced to the rescue.

“We knew that pups emit isolation calls—acoustic distress signals—when separated. But seeing this strict behavioral selectivity made us wonder: what is happening inside the mother when she hears these calls?” recalled study co-author and Doshisha University biologist Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa.

To learn more, Yoshino-Hashizawa and his colleagues monitored four mother bats who each recently birthed a litter of three pups. Some of the litters only contained females, so they decided to exclude the young males from the study to avoid sex-based variations. At two days old, the team recorded clips of the female pups’ tiny isolation calls. These function like the cries from a human baby, and each vocalization has its own unique tones.

However, Japanese house bats grow quickly. The new generation started making echolocation calls at about eight days old, and they ceased isolation calls after around 12 days. After roughly one month, they began flying like adults and set off on their own within the colony. The researchers then recorded their adult echolocation calls, as well.

Around 40 days after the pups’ birth, the team isolated each mother and played them the various audio clips while monitoring their heart rates using an ECG machine. As a control, they also played the recording of a random adult male bat. The study’s authors quickly saw that a mother bat’s heart rate sped up when they heard cries of their own pups, but not the pups that belong to other bat moms. Surprisingly, the mother bats’ heart rates boosted the most from recordings of the older, independent pups. Researchers theorize this might relate to the bats’ memory of more recent calls, but that idea requires more study to confirm.

Still, don’t get overly sentimental. The team also recorded strong heart rate reactions when the mothers heard calls from the random adult male. It’s possible the bats interpreted the sounds as a threat, but the team stopped short of categorizing the reactions as good or bad.

“‘[Heart rate measurement] cannot distinguish between positive emotions, such as maternal motivation, and negative ones, such as stress or alarm,” Yoshino-Hashizawa added.

Instead, the study authors suggest heart rates may be a solid starting point when trying to analyze bat behaviors and responses to their everyday environment. At the same time, a mother bat’s heart certainly seems to patter a bit more for her pups.

“[This] suggests that these vocalizations instantly physiologically prime the mother to engage in maternal behavior,” explained Yoshino-Hashizawa.

#4766. Rattlesnake bite venom is based on their personality

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:42 PM EDT

Rattlesnake venom is a complex blend of necrotic toxins designed to quickly disrupt blood coagulation and destroy tissue cells. This effective biological weapon comes at a major premium, however. Snakes must regularly replenish their venom reserves, which means they need to dole out just the right amount with each bite. Injecting too little venom into prey may allow their next meal to escape, while delivering too much can leave a rattlesnake defenseless against its own predators.

But that doesn’t mean rattlesnakes adhere to a one-bite-fits-all policy. According to researchers at California’s Loma Linda University, the amount of venom routinely varies by individual reptile. Their recent findings published in Frontiers in Ethology indicate these dosage ranges aren’t randomly distributed, and that snakes and their venom are much more complicated than many people realize.

“This study gives us a better understanding that reptiles, such as rattlesnakes, are behaviorally complex and have personalities like humans do,” explained Loma Linda University biologist and study co-author Chelsea Martin.

Martin and her colleague, William Hayes, focused on the preferences of the Pacific rattlesnake (Crotalus oreganus helleri). On three occasions over a four-week window, the team offered 48 snakes the chance to sink their fangs into a membrane-covered beaker. Previous experiments mainly focused on the frequency of bites, but Martin and Hayes also measured the amount of injected venom in each instance. They then compared the data across snakes.

“The results were striking in their consistency,” the study’s authors wrote in an accompanying Phys.org rundown.

The team also reported measured behaviors like aggressiveness, bite willingness, and the amount of venom amount were statistically significant and repeatable across many of the snakes. Additionally, their study further debunks the lingering myth that larger snakes actually produce less venom.

The study’s authors emphasize that their findings shouldn’t be held against the rattlesnakes themselves. While often feared, the snakes generally do everything they can to avoid confrontations with larger animals like humans. Bites are almost always the result of an unintentional encounter in the wild rather than an overly aggressive serpent. If anything, it’s people who aren’t paying attention to their surroundings, and not the other way around.

“One practical lesson remains unchanged: No rattlesnake should be approached or handled on the assumption that it will behave predictably,” the authors said. “Consistent individual differences do not make any particular snake safe.”

#696. Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

#4795. What night is van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’? New analysis says it’s in July.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

The swirling bluish-white mistrals, towering cypress trees, and glittery stars in Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” (1889) have captivated our imaginations for generations. Despite never selling a painting during his lifetime, van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” has since inspired a hit song in 1972, draws visitors to the Museum of Modern Art in New York City, and adorns everything from journals to scarves and tote bags.

But what particular night above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France, did the troubled post-impressionist painter actually put on the canvas? A new analysis points to July 22, 1889. During the early morning hours at that time of year, a waning crescent moon, the planet Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in Taurus appeared close together—all of which are seen in the painting. A study published today in the journal Scientific Reports details the findings.

According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, van Gogh was inspired by the view from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in southern France. He spent twelve months there 1889–1890, seeking reprieve from his mental illnesses and was also diagnosed with a form of epilepsy.

Like many of his paintings that year, “The Starry Night” was created in several sessions during the day, under different weather conditions. The village nestled below the hills was also based on other views, and could not be seen from his window. Although van Gogh reconstructed some of the features in the night sky as he saw them, he also altered the celestial shapes and added a sense of glow.

“Van Gogh assigned an emotional language to night and nature that took them far from their actual appearances,” the museum writes. “Dominated by vivid blues and yellows applied with gestural verve and immediacy, ‘The Starry Night’ also demonstrates how inseparable van Gogh’s vision was from the new procedures of painting he had devised, in which color and paint describe a world outside the artwork even as they telegraph their own status as, merely, color and paint.”

Using astronomy to pinpoint which day inspired the painting began with UCLA art historian Albert Boime in 1984 and Harvard University astronomer and astrophysicist Charles Whitney in 1986. The two scholars reached conflicting conclusions, and neither of them displaced the prevailing religious and symbolic interpretations of the painting.

Historically, the painting has been dated based on a letter van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18, 1889. However, the waning gibbous moon from mid-June does not account for the slender crescent seen in the painting. By July 22, 1889, the Hyades star cluster rose in the eastern sky alongside Venus, and the moon’s shape looked closer to the crescent on the canvas. This new study uses observational geometry to find an answer.

“To an observational astronomer, the cluster of stars in ‘The Starry Night’ is the Hyades in Taurus,” said study co-author Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim.  “But the moment I read the earlier studies explaining it as Aries, I heard the painting calling to me.” Kim is the CEO at South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a PhD candidate at Kyung Hee University.

For this research, the team combined astronomical simulations and measurements of star positions, with historical weather records, and van Gogh’s own letters. They also used earlier sketches of the landscape to reconstruct the narrow field of view represented in “The Starry Night.”

According to the team, the crescent moon, Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus would have been visible around dawn on July 22, 1889. This configuration generally corresponds to what is portrayed in the painting. Weather records suggest that the night sky around July 22 would have been clear enough for this astronomical arrangement to be visible. Additionally, the frequent mentions of cicadas in van Gogh’s letters suggest that the region was seeing a period of hot weather, which matches the recorded temperatures in July 1889 better than in June.

The team also verified that the painter’s field of view in another painting titled “Starry Night over the Rhône” (1888) with photographs taken on site was painted when the sky was in Aries. Using the same method, the team also believes that the stars in “Café Terrace at Night” (1888) may be the Summer Triangle.

“I hope this work prompts a renewed, multidisciplinary look at the sequence and working methods of the Saint-Rémy paintings, and at Van Gogh’s movements that summer,” Kim said. “Our conversation with Van Gogh’s other skies is still ongoing. We plan to tell those stories one by one.”

#746. Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.

#5634. A Less-Is-More Strategy for America

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

BARRY R. POSEN is Ford International Professor of Political Science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

The Folly of Expanding the Exhausted U.S. Military

On October 8, the USS Abraham Lincoln and its accompanying strike group returned to the United States after 320 days at sea. The deployment—a near record for the modern American military—had become controversial. As the group’s ships went month after month without a port call, conditions deteriorated precipitously. Sailors reported supply shortages, plumbing issues, and water contamination. Morale dropped markedly; eight sailors even tried to commit suicide. By the time the ships finally left the Middle East, their struggles had made headlines across the country.

The travails of the Lincoln are hardly the only story about the U.S. military to generate alarm since the war in Iran began last February. Military analysts have expressed concern about other carrier groups’ lengthy deployments. They have fretted about the Trump administration’s decision to pull soldiers and weapons stationed in the Indo-Pacific out of the region and position them in the Middle East. Perhaps most of all, they have worried as the United States drains its stockpile of sophisticated munitions.

These problems all share the same root cause: the U.S. military is not built for the near-constant use it has experienced for decades. Ever since the Cold War, American defense planners have structured the U.S. armed forces to be ready to fight two major conflicts simultaneously. In practice, that means Washington has constructed a force that is designed to win short, sharp wars. For that reason, the military does not have enough units, weapons, or personnel to handle never-ending conflicts and simultaneously use its forces around the world to coerce enemies and reassure partners.

American officials are now trying to modestly increase the size of the military. More important, they are hoping that huge infusions of money will increase the capability of the existing armed forces. But making these changes will be exceptionally challenging—and they are unnecessary. The United States is a highly secure country with many capable and wealthy partners. It does not need a defense apparatus capable of fighting everywhere all at once. Instead, it would be much better off focusing its attention on a smaller set of issues and scaling down accordingly.

The U.S. nonnuclear forces, which consume more than 80 percent of the defense budget, are structured and sized for particular contingencies—currently, a clash with China, Russia, or both nearly simultaneously. The military’s weapons are therefore designed for maximum lethality and survivability in what military planners expect to be an unbelievably dangerous environment. To that end, the Pentagon has emphasized purchasing precise, long-range, and stealthy weapons. These are supposed to kick open the doors of enemy defenses and allow other expensive advanced platforms, such as F-35 fighter jets or B-2 or B-21 bombers, to deliver large quantities of slightly less sophisticated but still quite lethal precision weapons.

Washington has never expected these wars to last long. Instead, it imagines that under the fierce pressure such conflicts would generate, the adversary will cry uncle or the war will escalate to nuclear threats or—worse yet—nuclear use. During the Cold War, for example, the United States and its allies planned for an extremely intense, roughly 30-day conflict against the Soviet Union. Today’s military planners have probably made similar assumptions about a war with Beijing or Moscow. Highly capable conventional forces and the risk of nuclear escalation, American thinking goes, should deter an attack. If deterrence fails, U.S. planners hope to stop an enemy in its tracks and create escalation risks so great that the adversary will realize its errors and try to reverse course.

But such thinking means that Washington does not have enough resources to sustain long, great-power conflicts. At $100 million each, the F-35 fighter aircraft, a mainstay of the air force, navy, and Marine Corps, is simply not designed for mass production. Neither are the long-range stand-off missiles that would be the preferred munitions in the early days of such a war. The U.S. military has developed sophisticated theater ballistic missile defenses, but their main purpose is to defend U.S. forces and essential military infrastructure—not towns, cities, and civilian industries. They can be used for this purpose under certain conditions, but doing so quickly exhausts the supply of missiles. In a war with China or Russia, civilians in local, allied countries will need to look after themselves.

These problems have become readily apparent in Washington’s war against Iran. The United States likely thought it could rapidly overpower the Islamic Republic—at best a middle power, albeit one with great ambitions. After all, under the right circumstances, the U.S. military handily overmatches these kinds of enemies, as it did Iraq in the 1990–91 Gulf War. But Washington’s enemies have smartened up since then. They no longer simply produce smaller and less capable versions of the great-power militaries that American forces are designed to deter and combat. Instead, they have prepared themselves to take a beating and keep on fighting, even if at modest levels of effectiveness.

Overly ambitious war plans and faulty policies are not the only reason the U.S. military is struggling. Politics is also creating difficulties. Because the American people hesitate to support wars abroad, U.S. politicians will do anything to avoid casualties. That means they take all the high-end weaponry that the military stockpiles to break down great-power defenses and instead spend it profligately on regional conflicts. Similarly, they deploy scarce air defense weapons to protect allies’ towns and cities, lest those allies consider finding new partners or decide to settle a war by compromising with their enemies.

U.S. policymakers also generally overuse the military. Politicians like to accompany diplomatic messaging with action, in part because they do not believe in diplomacy anymore, which pushes them to parade or use the armed forces far more often than is warranted. When asked to act, commanders usually salute and say yes. Others simply cannot sit still. Commanders, after all, took on their roles because they wanted to command something.

The result is a worn-down military. The defense press is full of stories about ships and aircraft that are not up to standard because they have been out too long, resulting in deferred maintenance. This, in turn, creates repair problems for other units. When a ship must stay in dry dock longer than usual to receive adequate repairs, other ships must wait longer for servicing—which means their needs also grow. Ultimately, the navy has fewer fully ready combat ships available in the event of a great-power challenge. The army has similar problems, with combat and support vehicles arriving at maintenance depots in unexpectedly poor condition. In the air force, the F-35’s “full mission capable rate,” which was never great, has deteriorated from 38 percent to 25 percent since 2021. And the overall readiness of air force aircraft has declined in recent years, in part simply because of overwork.

All the while, U.S. soldiers, sailors, pilots, and marines are constantly on guard, coercing and deterring U.S. enemies and reassuring allies. This, too, creates high levels of activity that can negatively affect retention. Although the military has generally been successful at retaining senior enlisted personnel in recent years, it may soon run into difficulty. Since 2024, navy warships have repeatedly gone to sea undercrewed. The ultimate result of this overuse is that the sharp edge of the military is dulled, and the steel blade is weakened.

U.S. defense officials are aware that they have a problem. Currently, they are trying to solve it by building a somewhat bigger and far more technologically capable military. Congress, for example, appears poised to increase the Pentagon’s so-called base budget—which encompasses what the Congressional Budget Office defines as the “enduring costs of manning, training, and equipping the military”—by over 25 percent, or to $1.1 trillion for the 2027 fiscal year. The Pentagon hopes to secure an additional $350 billion over the base budget in a subsequent bill. Defense officials also want to add over 40,000 new service members. But this is, at best, a personnel increase of three percent, which is much too small to fill the additional units needed to solve extant readiness problems. And the fancier weapons Congress hopes defense officials will buy cannot overcome the strain of constant deployments and repeated wars. The defense industrial base is barely keeping up with current demands, and recruiting large numbers of new service members would likely require offering much higher wages, which would then need to be extended to everyone else. Personnel costs would start consuming an ever-greater share of the defense budget. U.S. officials could instead opt for conscription, but that would produce a backlash.

There is, however, an alternative: scaling down. Instead of pretending that it must be able to fight two great-power wars at once, Washington should cut its policy coat to fit the available cloth. It should continue to build forces for major contingencies, such as deterring adventurism by China. But policymakers should agree to slow down other projects. Congress should task the Pentagon with reviewing the armed forces and come up with an activity budget, which would be based on an assessment of how much real military activity—flying hours, ship days at sea, training days in the field—can be undertaken without harming the readiness of the total force. It would then allocate that activity appropriately. Based on what they ought to know about the virtual attrition the military has suffered thanks to 30 years of hyperactivity, commanders should determine what level of exercises, forward deployments, and regional wars the military can actually support without doing damage to the capabilities it wishes to have ready every day to deter a great-power war and, if need be, wage one effectively. The U.S. Navy already knows the activity level that its fleet can support, which it has detailed in its Optimized Fleet Response Plan. But American presidents and defense secretaries have simply ignored this plan whenever they wanted to deploy more forces.

The activity budget should not be classified but instead should be public information, listed along with the dollar budget that the Pentagon publishes annually, voluminous summaries of which can be found at the Defense Department’s comptroller’s website. When politicians want to exceed the activity levels on which they have previously agreed, they should have to explain why and how they plan to repair the force after intense use. Commanders should think of this as an ethical issue. Every now and then, one should resign when overuse causes serious problems.

To help lighten its load, Washington will need to hand off some responsibilities to others. This should be feasible despite the need to manage Moscow’s aggressive impulses, which are the presumed cause of one of the contingencies—a war with Russia—that drives U.S. defense planning. The principal European states have more than enough economic and technological resources to deal with Russia on their own, if they so choose. The transatlantic bargain should thus be more forthright about the limited liability that the United States is prepared to assume for the continent’s defense.

The National Defense Strategy of the Trump administration is honest about this fact, but U.S. military activity levels and deployments in Europe suggest that the United States has not truly reduced its role in Europe’s defense. The supreme allied commander of NATO is still an American, when the position should be filled by a European. Tens of thousands of U.S. troops are still based all over Europe; many should come home. Pentagon websites and those of American commands in Europe feature laundry lists of exercises and maneuvers across the continent that involve not only forward-based U.S. forces but also rotating deployments of units based in the United States. These numbers, too, should diminish.

American officials may want to be able to solve most of their global security challenges by simply buying more forces. But a much better solution is for the United States to change its grand strategy and act with greater restraint. Washington simply does not have the will or the resources to provide for both Asia’s and Europe’s defense while fighting protracted conflicts elsewhere, most recently in the Middle East. Nor, for that matter, does it need to. The United States is inherently a very secure country. It is rich, endowed with bountiful natural resources, insulated by vast oceans, and blessed with relatively weak and pliant neighbors. Only huge changes in the political constellation of power in Asia, Europe, or the greater Middle East could seriously affect U.S. security.

Washington should buy sufficient military power and stock adequate reserves of raw materials to ensure against such changes. But in general, it can afford to be less proactive. A change in grand strategy might even allow the United States to cut the defense budget. If concerns about the ballooning U.S. deficits are correct, then the Pentagon will need to help pay the bills to set things right.

#4812. Baby manta rays stick to the shallows to avoid sharks and orca

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

Before giant manta rays (Mobula birostris) can grow to up to 30 feet long and 4,409 pounds, they must start small like most living things. Giant manta ray babies (called pups) in Central America don’t have their size to protect them from orca whales (Orcinus orca) and the numerous sharks in the open ocean. Their solution—sticking to the coast, where there is plenty of food to help them grow and defend themselves. A new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Fish Science details the baby rays’ behavior.

“We show that manta rays’ lifestyle isn’t a one-size-fits-all behavior. Giant manta rays in Central America undergo a dramatic shift in space use as they mature,” Jeffry Madrigal-Mesén, a study co-author and project leader of Mantas Costa Rica, said in a statement. “Juveniles remained strictly within shallow nearshore waters that serve as coastal refuges, but most of the tracked adults executed long-distance transboundary migrations along the continental shelf break into Central America.”

In the study, the team used multiple methods to track and identify mantas at five locations between 2018 and 2025. Acoustic telemetry uses underwater sound pulses to monitor fish movements and allowed the team to track 59 individual rays. With satellite telemetry, transmitters track movement and behavior from a distance and helped the team track large-scale movements of 12 individuals. Lastly, photo identification helped to identify individual manta rays.

“If we hadn’t used all three technologies together, we simply wouldn’t have been able to piece this puzzle together,” explained Madrigal-Mesén. “Combining these tools turned fragmented data into a unified, undeniable story.”

The team ended up with a dataset of over 12,000 detections. The data showed that young mantas (those with a body about 10 feet or less) stick to shallow coastal refuges. In these more shallow spots, the pups have a safe place that doesn’t overlap with their predators’ hunting grounds. They have plenty of food and places to hide, and even get essential services like cleaning from reefs.

“Mature adults can rely on their massive body size and their ability to dive deep to evade threats. But juveniles don’t have that luxury,” said Madrigal-Mesén. “What is truly amazing is seeing how strictly young manta rays stick to coastal waters as long-term refuges. We see the same individuals more often than once a year. In fact, just recently, they were sighted again at the exact same tagging location. It is always a joy to see them again—they have truly become a part of the family.”

By comparison, nine mature mantas in the study went on expansive migrations along the continental shelf break. One manta ray nicknamed Mickey Mouse because of a spot pattern on his belly had movements that suggest the fish also continue to display loyalty to a particular site once they grow up. Unusually approachable for a manta, Mickey Mouse allowed the team tag him multiple times over several years, which provided a continuous dataset. In 2020, he logged 462 acoustic detections at a coastal hub called El Secreto. He returned to the same shelf in 2023, and stayed there for 38 days.

“Nearshore cleaning stations transcend basic hygienic function; they act as primary social hubs where courtship and reproductive interactions take place,” said Madrigal-Mesén. “Returning to a predictable coastal depot allows mature rays to prioritize social and cleaning needs before undertaking large-scale regional migrations.”

Once they leave coastal regions, adult manta rays face different dangers. Coastal areas are often legally protected marine reserves, but the open ocean is another story. They no longer have legal protection and are exposed to dangers from industrial fisheries and vessel strikes.

#890. Astronomers uncover new secrets of the Universe’s first five minutes

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have measured the amount of helium produced during the Universe's first five minutes with unprecedented precision, offering a clearer picture of the conditions that existed shortly after the Big Bang. The achievement, made by an international research team that includes scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, represents a major advance in precision cosmology.

The findings, presented in five papers published in The Astrophysical Journal, are based on 130 hours of observations using the Large Binocular Telescope. By analyzing distant galaxies, the researchers reduced the uncertainty in this fundamental measurement to just 0.5%, making it three times more precise than previous estimates.

The improved accuracy provides scientists with a powerful new tool for investigating the origins of the Universe and testing the Standard Model of physics. Such fundamental tests have a long history of advancing scientific understanding and contributing to technological breakthroughs.

"This is a physics experiment on a grand scale and one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career," said Evan Skillman, University of Minnesota College of Science and Engineering Distinguished Professor in the School of Physics and Astronomy. "It's a fundamental number that tells us specifically about the conditions of our Universe in its first five minutes. It has diagnostic power that speaks directly to the Standard Model of Physics."

According to the Big Bang theory, the Universe began expanding from an extraordinarily hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Three major lines of evidence support this explanation: the continuing expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the amounts of light elements such as helium and deuterium that formed during its earliest moments.

Although cosmic expansion and the microwave background have been studied extensively, measurements of primordial helium have historically received less attention. Determining its original abundance more accurately allows researchers to investigate the physical conditions that prevailed when the Universe was only minutes old.

To achieve their new level of precision, the team departed from the conventional method of estimating primordial helium. That approach involves observing large numbers of galaxies and extrapolating the results to determine how much helium existed before later generations of stars altered their chemical composition.

Instead, the researchers selected 15 exceptionally pristine, small, distant galaxies. These galaxies have undergone relatively little chemical evolution, making them valuable cosmic "time capsules" that preserve clues about the Universe's early composition.

Powerful Telescopes Deliver Record Precision

The team used sophisticated spectrographs developed at Ohio State University to examine more than 10 helium spectral lines and 15 hydrogen lines simultaneously. Studying these signals together made it possible to identify and correct subtle sources of measurement error that earlier studies could largely overlook.

Such small effects become especially important when researchers are trying to reduce uncertainty to less than 1%. Accounting for them was essential to achieving the new 0.5% precision.

"The MODS spectrographs took 12 years to build from conception to first light on sky," said Richard Pogge, College of Arts and Sciences Distinguished Professor of Astronomy at Ohio State University. "This is the kind of project we designed them to do, and to see them deliver is enormously satisfying. It's not every day you can help build instruments that measure something fundamental about our Universe."

Beyond refining estimates of the Universe's earliest chemical composition, the new measurement provides an important test of the Standard Model of physics. The amount of helium created shortly after the Big Bang depends on the physical conditions at that time, including the types of elementary particles present.

Using their precise helium measurement, the researchers were able to calculate the number of neutrino families in the early Universe. Neutrinos are extremely light subatomic particles, and their behavior plays an important role in scientists' understanding of fundamental physics.

The results therefore do more than strengthen existing evidence for the Big Bang. They also offer a more precise way to check whether established physical theories accurately describe the Universe during its first few minutes.

"We promised a half-percent uncertainty in our proposal, and we got there," added Skillman. "In the unpredictable world of science, that doesn't happen very often."

The international collaboration brought together scientists from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State University, Gonzaga University, Northwestern University, The University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, University of California Santa Cruz, University of Illinois, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF.

#909. Scientists discover a bizarre Jurassic “squid” unlike anything ever seen

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Two extraordinarily rare fossils found in Wyoming have revealed a strange new species of squid relative that lived approximately 160 million years ago, during the Jurassic Period.

Named Wyoteuthis linsterorum, the prehistoric creature belonged to an extinct group of marine animals known as belemnites, which were related to modern squid and octopuses. What makes this species particularly remarkable is its unusually thick, barrel-shaped internal skeleton, earning it the distinction of being the "chunkiest" or "fattest" belemnite ever identified.

An international research team from the United States, United Kingdom, and New Zealand, including Dr. Dean Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester, detailed the discovery in the scientific journal Papers in Palaeontology.

An Unusually Thick Jurassic Squid Relative

Belemnites are among the most frequently discovered fossils in the world. They are best known for their distinctive, bullet-shaped internal skeletal structures, called rostra or guards, which are often the only parts of the animals that survive in the fossil record.

Wyoming's Sundance Formation, which preserves evidence of an ancient inland sea that once stretched across much of the western United States, has yielded millions of these fossils. According to Wyoming paleontologist and study coauthor Jessica Lippincott, they are commonly nicknamed "squid butts" in the region.

Most belemnite fossils from Wyoming have a narrow, bullet-shaped appearance, typically measuring between 5 and 10 centimeters in length and just 1 to 2 centimeters across. The newly identified species is dramatically different. Its rostrum measures approximately 10 centimeters long but reaches an extraordinary 6 centimeters in width, making it the thickest known example of its kind.

Scientists estimate that the living animal, including its arms, could have reached a total length of approximately 60 centimeters, or about two feet.

Rare Fossils Reveal a Surprising Evolutionary Secret

Wyoming paleontologist Bill Wahl, who has devoted much of his career to searching for fossils across the state, recognized that the unusual specimen deserved closer examination. He said the belemnite "stuck out like a sore thumb" compared with the countless others found in the region.

Wahl assembled an international team to investigate the fossils, bringing in specialists including belemnite expert Alexey Ippolitov, a PhD student now based at Victoria University of Wellington in New Zealand.

The researchers used computed tomography (CT) scanning to investigate the fossils' internal structures without damaging the specimens. These detailed scans revealed an unexpected connection to an ancient belemnite family that scientists had believed disappeared millions of years before this animal lived.

Ippolitov said he was "deeply surprised" to encounter such an unusual specimen from a group that has already been studied extensively. Belemnites have been the subject of paleontological research for more than 200 years, making the identification of such a radically different species especially remarkable.

The creature's unusual proportions may also offer clues about how it lived and hunted.

Ippolitov explains: "A possible explanation is that their relatively large size gave them an advantage when hunting co-occurring smaller belemnites of the genus Pachyteuthis. Modern squid, after all, are hardly picky when it comes to prey: they readily hunt not only other species of cephalopods, but sometimes even their own kind."

A Fossil Discovery Nearly 30 Years in the Making

Although the species has only now been formally described, its discovery began almost three decades ago.

The first known specimen was found in the late 1990s by Dr. Burkhard Pohl, founder of the Wyoming Dinosaur Center. While examining fossils, Pohl noticed a belemnite that was considerably larger than any he had previously encountered.

Despite its unusual appearance, the specimen remained unstudied in a museum collection drawer for many years.

A breakthrough came when fossil collector Cliff Linster discovered a second specimen near Ten Sleep, Wyoming. He donated the fossil to the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in 2019, providing researchers with another example of the mysterious animal.

With two specimens available for comparison, scientists finally had enough evidence to recognize and formally describe an entirely new species.

The genus name Wyoteuthis means "a squid from Wyoming," while the species name linsterorum recognizes Cliff Linster and his family for their contributions. Linster discovered one of the only two known examples and ensured it was available for scientific research.

Lippincott emphasized how extraordinary the discovery was, even for researchers familiar with Wyoming's abundant belemnite fossils.

"After spending years fossil hunting in Wyoming and collecting countless fossils of the common Pachyteuthis belemnites here in Wyoming, this one is unlike anything I have ever seen! It goes to show that we are still finding new species of fossils all the time, and people who rockhound or collect invertebrate fossils can also contribute to science."

Honoring a Fossil Hunter's Remarkable Legacy

Sadly, Cliff Linster passed away in June before the research was published. He had been aware that scientists were investigating his discovery and was enthusiastic about seeing the unusual fossil receive formal scientific recognition.

The Linster family already had an impressive history of fossil discoveries, including their association with the famous dinosaur Bambiraptor.

For British paleontologist Dr. Dean Lomax, the discovery also carries considerable personal meaning. Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester and an 1851 Research Fellow at the University of Bristol, first worked alongside Cliff and his wife, Sandy, while excavating dinosaur fossils as a teenager.

He said: "I met Cliff and Sandy in 2009, when I spent a week digging up a dinosaur bonebed on their property in northern Montana. I have fond memories of that dig, listening to Cliff share his passion for fossils, including this belemnite. I'm honored to help name this fossil after Cliff. It's bittersweet that he didn't get to see it published, but it's wonderful that we can immortalize him and his family name in the history of paleontology. The find is incredibly rare, which might be due to some ecological adaptations to the environment at the time. Perhaps its rarity is a result of narrow specialization."

Lomax has extensive experience studying Wyoming's prehistoric animals, including collecting fossils and excavating plesiosaurs and ichthyosaurs from the Sundance Formation. He is also the author of the recently published book "The Secret Lives of Dinosaurs," which features fossils from Wyoming.

The discovery highlights how even well-studied fossil groups can still hold major surprises, particularly when unusual specimens are preserved and made available for scientific investigation.

Both known fossils of Wyoteuthis linsterorum are now on display at the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in Thermopolis, Wyoming, where visitors can see the extraordinary remains of one of the Jurassic seas' most unusual squid relatives.

#5286. Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Some people carry one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease yet never develop it, remaining mentally sharp well into their 80s. For years, scientists have wondered what protects these individuals despite their increased genetic vulnerability.

Now, researchers at Columbia University have uncovered a biological mechanism that could help explain this remarkable resistance. Their findings point to a rare genetic mutation that protects the brain's blood vessels from damage, potentially opening a new path toward Alzheimer's treatments.

The discovery builds on research the team published two years ago involving APOE ε4, a gene variant strongly associated with Alzheimer's risk. The scientists found that some people carrying APOE ε4 also possess a rare protective mutation in the fibronectin (FN1) gene.

Fibronectin is a protein that helps support the structure of tissues. However, in people with APOE ε4, excessive amounts can accumulate around blood vessels in the brain, potentially compromising a critical protective system known as the blood-brain barrier. The rare FN1 mutation appears to limit this buildup and the damage associated with it.

That earlier discovery suggested an intriguing possibility: What if scientists could develop a treatment that recreates the protection provided by this rare mutation?

A new study published in Nature Aging brings that possibility closer by identifying how excess fibronectin damages the brain's protective barrier and revealing potential ways to interrupt the process.

"This new study takes us from a genetic clue to a disease mechanism," says Caghan Kizil, the study's corresponding author and professor of neurological sciences in the Department of Neurology and the Taub Institute for Research on Alzheimer's Disease and the Aging Brain at Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons.

"We knew that changes in fibronectin could protect against Alzheimer's, but we didn't know why excess fibronectin was harmful in the first place. This study gives us that detailed mechanism -- and with it, ideas for how to reproduce that natural protection."

How an Alzheimer's Risk Gene Weakens the Blood-Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier serves as a protective boundary between the bloodstream and the brain. Made up of tightly connected cells surrounding the brain's blood vessels, it carefully regulates which substances can enter brain tissue while helping maintain conditions necessary for removing potentially harmful waste.

When this barrier becomes damaged or excessively permeable, substances that would normally be kept out can enter the brain more easily. Such disruptions may contribute to inflammation and other processes involved in neurological diseases, including Alzheimer's.

To investigate the role of fibronectin, the Columbia team combined several advanced research approaches. They examined human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, using genetic and epigenetic analyses to explore changes in DNA and gene activity.

Their investigation also included single-cell sequencing, which reveals the activity of individual cells, and spatial imaging to examine where biological changes occur within tissues. Additional experiments involved human stem-cell models, three-dimensional cultures of blood vessels, and studies in zebrafish and mice.

Despite the variety of methods, the findings consistently pointed to the same mechanism: APOE ε4 promotes the production and accumulation of fibronectin around the brain's blood vessels.

In mice engineered to carry human APOE ε4, researchers measured nearly twice as much fibronectin in the brain as in mice carrying APOE ε3, another common version of the gene. The greater accumulation was accompanied by evidence that the blood-brain barrier had become leaky.

Scientists Discover How Fibronectin Causes Brain Damage

One experiment provided especially important evidence that fibronectin does more than simply accumulate in damaged blood vessels.

The researchers increased human fibronectin specifically in astrocytes, specialized support cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier and keep the brain functioning properly.

Increasing fibronectin in these cells was enough to disrupt the barrier, allowing it to become more permeable. Conversely, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier's ability to function normally.

These results provided experimental evidence that excess fibronectin can directly contribute to blood-brain barrier damage rather than merely appearing alongside it.

"The animal experiments allowed us to move beyond association," says co-first author Prabesh Bhattarai, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "When we increased fibronectin in astroglia, the barrier became permeable. When we reduced fibronectin, barrier function improved."

The team then investigated the molecular events responsible for the damage.

They found that excessive fibronectin interferes with communication between cells by activating abnormal signals through integrins, proteins that help cells sense and respond to their surroundings, and an enzyme known as focal adhesion kinase.

These abnormal signals disrupt a network of growth factors called VEGF, HBEGF, and IGF1. Although growth factors are often associated with cell development and repair, these particular signaling molecules also help astrocytes and blood vessel cells coordinate their activities to maintain a healthy blood-brain barrier.

When their communication becomes disrupted, the barrier can lose its integrity, potentially leaving brain tissue more vulnerable to damage.

"Single-cell analysis allowed us to examine this pathway in the different cells that maintain the brain's blood vessels," says co-first author Elanur Yilmaz, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "We observed the same biological pattern across experimental models and human Alzheimer's tissue, making the mechanism especially compelling."

Evidence From Human Brains Strengthens the Alzheimer's Connection

Importantly, the researchers found signs of similar biological disturbances in human samples, suggesting that the process identified in laboratory experiments may also contribute to Alzheimer's disease in people carrying APOE ε4.

Analyses of brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid showed that elevated fibronectin levels were associated with inflammation in astrocytes.

Genetic and gene-regulation analyses also connected FN1 to blood vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA, a growth factor that plays an important role in maintaining healthy brain blood vessels.

Together, these findings provide multiple lines of evidence linking excess fibronectin to the vascular damage associated with APOE ε4.

"The convergence of these independent human datasets strengthens the case that fibronectin is part of the APOE ε4-related vascular disease process," says Badri Vardarajan, associate professor of neurological science in the Department of Neurology, Gertrude H. Sergievsky Center, and Taub Institute, who collaborated on this discovery of the FN1 mutation.

A Rare Genetic Defense Could Inspire New Alzheimer's Treatments

The potential implications are significant because APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. Approximately one in five people carries at least one copy of this gene variant.

Although carrying APOE ε4 does not guarantee that someone will develop Alzheimer's, it increases their likelihood of developing the disease. Understanding why certain carriers remain unaffected could reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

The latest findings suggest several possible approaches.

One strategy would be to prevent harmful amounts of fibronectin from accumulating around the brain's blood vessels. Another would involve blocking the abnormal cellular signals triggered by excess fibronectin. A third possibility would be to restore the growth factor signaling that allows astrocytes and blood vessel cells to maintain the blood-brain barrier.

Researchers are already investigating these possibilities through laboratory experiments.

However, translating the discovery into a treatment will require considerable care. Fibronectin performs essential functions throughout the body, including supporting tissue structure and helping damaged tissues heal. Any future therapy would therefore need to target its harmful accumulation or effects without disrupting these beneficial activities.

By identifying the molecular mechanisms involved, scientists now have a more precise framework for designing such treatments. The findings may also help researchers determine when interventions would be most effective and how to measure whether they are successfully protecting the brain.

"By showing how fibronectin contributes to early vascular damage, this study gives us new therapeutic targets and potential ways to measure whether treatments are working," says Richard Mayeux, chair of the Department of Neurology and a collaborator on the project. "Clinically, it connects a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's to an early disease process that may be possible to modify."

Although these potential therapies remain experimental, the discovery offers a promising new direction in Alzheimer's research. Rather than focusing only on the damage that develops as the disease progresses, scientists may eventually be able to reproduce a natural genetic defense that helps protect vulnerable brains before significant damage occurs.

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

When zoology professor Daniel Baldassarre traveled to the remote Galápagos Islands of Wolf and Darwin with two other researchers, getting there proved quite a challenge. With no beaches to land a boat on, they had to approach the islands’ cliffs in a small dinghy and wait for a lull in the waves before jumping onto sharp, black lava rocks.

But their efforts paid off. On that trip, they saw a bird that very few people have ever set eyes on in the wild: the vampire ground finch. These finches are only found on Wolf and Darwin, and feed on the blood of much larger birds called Nazca boobies that also live on the islands.

Up close, Baldassarre and his colleagues noted the vampire finches’ blood-stained bills and their throats visibly engorged with blood. They also saw them in action.

“We observed scores of vampire finches clamouring all over the backs, tails, and wings of boobies, opening up substantial wounds with their sharp beaks, and drinking their fill of blood,” they wrote in an article for The Conversation.

“Blood is a notoriously difficult food source to process and extract nutrients from, which probably partly explains why so few animals have evolved to feed on it,” Baldassarre tells Popular Science. He suspects that on these islands where freshwater is extremely rare, blood might serve as a water source for the finches.

It’s not the only component of the vampire finches’ diet, however. Based on unpublished data collected by Baldassarre and his colleagues, blood makes up about one-tenth of the vampire finches’ diet.

“These finches are quite opportunistic, so they will eat just about anything they can find,” Baldassarre says. “We observed them feeding on everything from seeds to insects to broken seabird eggs.”

One theory is that vampire finches rely more on blood feeding in periods of extreme drought, when other resources are scarce. But more research is needed to see if this idea holds up in practice.

Vampire finches weren’t always vampires, scientists think. The relationship between vampire finches and boobies probably started off with the finches eating parasites found on the skin and feathers of the boobies. This would have been mutually beneficial because it provided a cleaning service for the boobies and food for the finches.

But at some point in evolutionary history, in the process of picking off parasites, the finches started to create lesions on the boobies’ skin, and eventually began feeding on their blood.

Over time, their bills became more scalpel-like, perfect for making skin incisions. Their digestive system also changed. When Baldassarre and his colleagues studied the microbes found in vampire finches’ guts, they found that their gut microbiome was very different from that of other, non-blood-consuming finches on the Galápagos Islands, something they attribute to their unique diet.

Until recently, vampire finches were considered a subspecies of the sharp-beaked ground finch (Geospiza difficilis).

“But they have now been designated as their own species [Geospiza septentrionalis] because of the differences they have evolved because of blood feeding,” Baldassarre notes.

Baldassarre and his colleagues have seen vampire finches gang up on and kill nestling boobies,“but that was quite rare,” he points out.

“In most cases, the finches seem to inflict just enough damage to break the skin and extract some blood, but not too much to harm the boobies significantly.” This is similar to many parasite-host relationships, such as fleas, ticks, and mosquitoes on mammals. “You can kind of think of the vampire finches as giant mosquitoes,” Baldassarre says.

As seen in a segment on vampire finches in David Attenborough’s documentary A Perfect Planet, the boobies seem to tolerate the vampire finches rather than chasing them away. There are likely a few reasons for this, Baldassarre says.

“For one, as in large animals being bitten by mosquitoes, often the damage is minimal and not noticeable, so it doesn’t elicit a strong reaction. Also, the boobies sometimes have a vested interest in staying put and guarding their spot in the colony, or perhaps nest or nestlings, so may not want to fly away. Finally, the finches seem to target hard-to-reach areas on the boobies like the base of tail feathers, so it’s likely difficult for the boobies to deter them.”

#5955. What’s Your Airport Dwell Time? A Metric Businesses Think A Lot About

Dwell time, simply, is how long you spend in an airport, or airport space, before a flight. For most people, it’s not something they’ve ever actively really thought about.

For businesses in air travel and airports, like lounge operators, credit card companies or airlines, it’s one of the main things they think about. So what’s yours and what does it say about you?

Well, a lot. More importantly, the tracking of that dwell time has contributed to quite a lot of innovation in lounges and airports, with hopefully much more to come.

Build it and they will come. Who knew Kevin Costner knew so much about lounges?! In truth, if someone builds a lounge, everyone will come and ensuring it’s a good experience is an art form requiring math. Crowds can be hard to predict and most lounges are “too” full these days.

Knowing the average dwell time of a customer, and even a specific customer profile is one of the best tools lounges have to predict the flows of customers, including how much they’ll need to stock in food and drinks and everything else during peak times.

Even outside of lounges, knowing how long an air traveler spends in the airport is vital to understanding what kind of businesses, restaurants or concessions to offer. There’s a reason grab and go places do really, really well and get away with charging $12 for water.

Surprisingly to some, top tier frequent flyers typically have a really low dwell time compared to lower ranks or leisure travelers.

If an airline knows their lounge is full of top tiers that day, they can expect many people who won’t stay long. Whereas, if it’s a day with full flights and mostly leisure travel passengers, you can predict people will dwell far longer.

It makes sense. If you’re not used to the delights of a great airport lounge you come earlier, stay longer and take advantage of more. You also have less confidence in timings of the check in and security process, so you pad time in, in addition to the natural, “treat yourself” vibes.

If you do it all the time, every minute not in the airport is often seen as the win, so lounges are just a place to quickly reset and refresh before carrying on.

Don’t get me wrong, I still indulge from time to time, but I’ve seen a huge dip in my own dwell average.

As I strolled through T2 at Heathrow this week en route to the United Club, it occurred to me that most of my recent lounge visits averaged about 30 minutes in total. In that 30 minutes I’d say I have a maximum of one drink, often little or no food, and mostly take advantage of the wifi and nice calm facilities.

I’ve gotten my time from curb to security at Heathrow down to a decent science and I like it that way. At other airports with even more predictable security and check in patterns, I’ll shave even more time off, often leaving no time for lounges at all.

And this raises a fair question: if everyone could truly model out their time from curb to airside, would they spend as long? Things like TSA PreCheck and CLEAR have been monumental in adding a layer of certainty to this process and I’d expect we’ll see more of it. Will more passengers change habits as they have better confidence in making their flight while cutting it fine?

Now of course, all bets are off when I travel with my kids. And that’s kind of the point. We think we’re very unique in our travel habits, but there are many variables of our travels which drop us into a fairly predictable pattern … and dwell time.

When lounge access is a marquee benefit of being a frequent flyer, but most top frequent flyers hardly leave any time for lounge dwelling, is that a real selling point?

In response, we’ve seen really clever pivots lately, including from Air Canada and Capital One. Each brand has created curated “grab and go” offerings in their lounges or lounge-like areas to offer passengers complimentary things which have tangible value — a nitro cold brew latte, or a nice pastry — for “free”, which they’re totally able to take on the road with them.

That’s a huge departure from the days of lounges with signs everywhere saying no outside food and drinks and no removing food or drinks from the lounge. People never paid attention anyway, and this now just creates a formalized and better thought out approach to catering to all traveler needs.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. #404. Erdogan's political fate may be determined by Turkey's Kurds

📅 Apr 03, 2023  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Turkey’s persecuted pro-Kurdish party has emerged as a kingmaker in the country’s upcoming election, playing a decisive role that may just tip the balance enough to unseat two-decade ruler Recep Tayyip Erdogan.

In a key setback to the Turkish president and leader of the Justice and Development Party (AK Party), the pro-Kurdish Peoples’ Democratic Party (HDP) last month announced that it would not put forward its own presidential candidate, a move analysts say allows its supporters to vote for Erdogan’s main rival.

“We are facing a turning point that will shape the future of Turkey and (its) society,” said the HDP in a statement on March 23. “To fulfill our historical responsibility against the one-man rule, we will not field a presidential candidate in (the) May 14 elections.”

It is a twist of irony for the Turkish strongman, who spent the better half of the past decade cracking down on the party after it began chipping away at his voter base. Its former leader Selahattin Demirtas has been in prison for nearly seven years and the party faces possible closure by a court for suspected collusion with the militant Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) and affiliated groups. But its influence may nonetheless determine the course of Turkey’s politics.

The HDP’s decision not to field a candidate came just three days after head of the Republican People’s Party (CHP) Kemal Kilicdaroglu, Erdogan’s main rival, visited the party’s co-chairs. He told reporters that the solution to Turkey’s problems, “including the Kurdish problem” lies in parliament,” according to Turkish media.

Kilicdaroglu, who represents the six-party Nation Alliance opposition bloc, is the strongest contender to run against Erdogan in years. And while the HDP hasn’t yet announced whether it will put its weight behind him, analysts say it is the kingmaker in the elections.

“It was a carefully crafted political discourse,” Hisyar Ozsoy, deputy co-chair of the HDP and a member of parliament from the predominantly Kurdish province of Diyarbakir, told CNN. “We are not going to have our own candidate, and we will leave it to the international community to interpret it the way they wish.”

Experts say the crackdown on the HDP is rooted in the threat it poses to Erdogan politically, as well as its position as one of the main parties representing Turkey’s Kurds, an ethnic minority from which a separatist militant movement has emerged.

The party and the Kurdish people have had a complicated relationship with Erdogan. The leader courted the Kurds in earlier years by granting them more rights and reversing restrictions on the use of their language. Relations with the HDP were also cordial once, as Erdogan worked with the party on a brief peace process with the PKK.

But ties between Erdogan and the HDP later turned sour, and the HDP fell under a sweeping crackdown aimed at the PKK and their affiliates.

Kurds are the biggest minority in Turkey, making up between 15% and 20% of the population, according to Minority Rights Group International.

It is unclear if the HDP will endorse Kilicdaroglu, but analysts say that the deliberate distance may be beneficial for the opposition candidate.

The accusations against the HDP place it in a precarious position during the elections. It currently faces a case in Turkey’s Constitutional Court over suspected ties to the PKK, which is designated as a terrorist group by Turkey, the United States and the European Union. Knowing it may be banned at any moment, its candidates are running under the Green Left Party in parliament.

The HDP’s threat to Erdogan’s hold on power became apparent after the June 2015 election, the first general election it participated in. It won 13% of the seats, denying the ruling AK Party its majority for the first time since 2002. Erdogan, however, called a snap election five months later, which led to a drop in the HDP’s support to 10.7%, as well as the restoration of the AK Party’s overall majority.

“They are a kingmaker in these elections because the HDP gets about half of the votes of the Kurdish population in Turkey,” said Somer, adding that the other, more conservative Kurdish voters have traditionally voted for Erdogan’s AK Party. And last month, the Free Cause Party (HUDA-PAR), a tiny Kurdish-Islamist party announced support for Erdogan in the elections. The party has never won seats in parliament.

“We want to play the game wisely, and we need to be very careful,” said Ozsoy, adding that the party wants to avoid a “contaminated political climate” where the elections are polarized “between a very ugly ultra-nationalist discourse against Kilicdaroglu and others.”

The party was founded in 2012 with a number of aims, said Ozsoy, one of which was “peaceful and democratic resolution of the Kurdish conflict.”

Somer said that the party was seen to be “an initiative” of the PKK, which later led to a heavy government crackdown on it in the name of counterterrorism.

Its former leader Demirtas remains an influential figure.

The Turkish government has been trying to link the HDP to the PKK but has so far failed to prove “a real connection,” said Asli Aydintasbas, a visiting fellow at the Brookings Institution in Washington, DC.

A post-Erdogan Turkey may give some breathing space to the Kurds and Kurdish-dominated parties in Turkey, Aydintasbas told CNN, noting that many Kurdish voters have recently left Erdogan’s camp. “For HDP, this is more than just an ideological choice,” she said. “It’s a matter of survival.”

Ozsoy says his party understands what’s at stake, not only for Turkey’s Kurds but for all its minorities.

“We are aware of our responsibility here. We are aware of our role. We know we are in a kingmaker position,” the HDP lawmaker said.

#5938. Here are 10 Full-Sized Liquid Items That You Can Actually Carry On Board a Flight

📅 Dec 09, 2022  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

For years, travelers have become accustomed to meticulously packing liquids ranging from shampoo to sunscreen in their carry-on bags in order to meet the TSA liquid limit—and avoid getting that expensive perfume confiscated.

Internationally, this rule is known as “3-1-1." Liquids, gels, and creams packed in a carry-on must be in containers no larger than 3.4 ounces (100 mL). All containers must fit in a single, clear, quart-sized bag. (Larger liquids can still be brought along on your travels but must be packed in checked luggage.)

While commonly referred to as “liquids,” these regulated substances are technically known as LAGs, an abbreviation for liquids, aerosols, and gels. This category also encompasses creams or pastes—essentially anything that can be cut through or spread with a butter knife—from common travel necessities like shampoo, face wash, and sunscreen, to toothpaste, hairspray, and even peanut butter.

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) first introduced the rule in August 2006, after British police foiled a terrorist plot to detonate explosive liquid in soda cans on flights from London to the US and Canada—prompting countries around the world to adopt the new airport security measure.

Today, most countries abide by the rule, though it has loosened in recent years, as more airports install advanced CT scanners with the capability to identify hazardous liquids. Some UK airports, including London Heathrow (LHR), now allow liquids of up to 2 liters (about 67.6 ounces).

In the US, a TSA spokesperson tells Condé Nast Traveler they “anticipate the limit on liquids will be lifted in the coming years." However, with the roll-out of the new scanners varying by terminal, the limit still remains in effect nationwide.

In this transitional period, the decisions are ultimately up to the individual officers, but it turns out there are certain exceptions to the 3-1-1 rule. The TSA currently allows travelers to take 10 common full-size liquids through airport security in the US.

To bring approved oversized liquids through security, travelers need to declare these items to a security officer prior to screening and place them in a separate bin. Anything that triggers the alarm while going through security is subject to further inspection. Here's what you need to know.

The only exception is medically-necessary gel ice packs—for items such as breast milk, IV bags, pumps, and syringes—which are allowed in “reasonable qualities,” even if they are melted or slushy.
