#1759. Three Man United and Bournemouth stars and Leeds striker Dominic Calvert-Lewin join those refusing to take the knee in Premier League - after Chelsea fans booed the gesture before Tottenham clash

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 14:15 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 16:55 GMT, 25 October 2026

Four more Premier League footballers decided against taking the knee during Sunday's games.

Manchester United centre back Ayden Heaven and Bournemouth duo Rayan and Tyler Adams stayed standing before the two teams faced off at Old Trafford.

All Liverpool and Brighton players took the knee at Anfield, as did every Crystal Palace and Newcastle player at Selhurst Park.

At the late kick-off at the Stadium of Light between Sunderland and Leeds, England striker Dominic Calvert-Lewin rejected the stance for the second week running.

The stance of Heaven, Rayan, Adams and Calvert-Lewin comes after both Issa Diop and Marc Guehi also didn't take the knee before their games earlier this weekend.

Rayan and Adams both kept their stance from last Sunday ahead of Bournemouth's victory over Sunderland, while Heaven didn't feature in United's draw at Leeds.

Three more Premier League stars decided against taking the knee during Sunday's games

The majority of players observed the gesture but Rayan, Tyler Adams and Ayden Heaven didn't

Last week, the Bournemouth duo were joined by Man City defender Guehi, Ipswich Town's Diop, Everton's Ainsley Maitland-Niles, and Leeds' Calvert-Lewin in not taking the knee.

Footballers in England's top flight have been given the opportunity to take the knee across two weekends this month in an effort to challenge discrimination. This is part of the Premier League's 'No Room for Racism' initiative.

After last week's snub, Guehi, who this week suggested taking the knee had 'lost its meaning', refused to kneel before kick-off at Villa Park as league leaders City took on Aston Villa, instead he remained standing and clapped.

When asked whether he had spoken to his players about taking the knee, City boss Enzo Maresca said: 'No, the players are already aware of the information.'

The England star's stance also followed Diop, who doubled down when Ipswich hosted Nottingham Forest at Portman Road.

Diop was the only player among the 22 on the pitch to remain standing as Forest's starters and his own team-mates all opted to take part.

Diop has not spoken publicly about his stance but the move has divided players, some of whom view it as a hollow gesture.

Maitland-Niles, meanwhile, was named on the bench for Everton's trip to Arsenal, so was not on the field of play when the players observed the anti-racism initiative.

The gesture was also met by a smattering of boos ahead of Tottenham's clash with Chelsea at Stamford Bridge later on Saturday.

Man City defender Marc Guehi refused to kneel for the second week running on Saturday

Issa Diop (right) also stood while other players took the knee prior to Ipswich Town's clash with Nottingham Forest on Friday night

When asked about Calvert-Lewin's viewpoint last week, Leeds manager Daniel Farke explained his striker's thinking, saying: 'Dom is more or less like, "No, I don't go down. I show my respect by standing up."

'Every individual is different and shows respect for this campaign in a different way. It's definitely a good campaign and a good topic that football and sports, in general, support situations like this because a dressing room is a perfect example of how we should all live together.

'Different passports, different languages, different religions, but they are all together as human beings and friends who work together toward one target. This is how the world should be.'

The campaign was previously enacted before every game until it stopped in August 2022 having been started in the UK in 2020 following the death of George Floyd in US police custody, which sparked the Black Lives Matter movement.

Subsequently, a decision was made to limit the gesture to match rounds falling during the No Room For Racism initiative.

It was agreed ahead of the season among Premier League captains that they would continue to perform the gesture twice over 38 rounds.

While the vast majority of players have taken part, some strong opposition has been voiced, notably by Wilfried Zaha in 2021, who said: 'I feel like taking the knee is degrading, because growing up my parents just let me know that I should be proud to be black no matter what and I feel like we should just stand tall.'

And last year, the Lionesses chose not to kneel before their Euro 2025 semi-final against Italy following racist abuse aimed at defender Jess Carter on social media.

Captain Lucy Bronze said the anti-racism message is not 'as strong as it used to be' following the abuse of her team-mate.

'It was driven by the group – obviously certain individuals more than others,' Bronze explained. 'I think it was just the fact that we feel as a collective, is the message as strong as it used to be? Is the message really hitting hard? Because to us it feels like it's not if these things are still happening to our players in the biggest tournaments of their lives.

'It's about putting another statement out there to say, it's something that still is a problem, it's something that still needs to be put right. More needs to be done in football, more needs to be done in society. What that is right now as an individual, I don't exactly know.'

#3015. How Sal Frelick became Brewers' unlikely World Series hero with Game 2 blast: 'He has this edge about him'

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

MILWAUKEE -- Ian Seymour kept testing Sal Frelick late Saturday night, almost dismissive of what the Brewers outfielder might be capable of producing.

But Frelick has never been one to shrink when his mettle is challenged. His competitive fire only burns hotter.

In the bottom of the sixth inning in Game 2 of the World Series, with the Brewers on the brink of erasing a four-run deficit for the second time this postseason, the lefthanded Rays reliever kept feeding Frelick fastballs.

All nine pitches of the at-bat, in fact. Alternating between two variations of the pitch for much of it.

Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam. Sinker. Four-seam.

"I'm an eight-hole hitter," Frelick said, detailing the number of fastballs he saw. "I'm a lefty. You have to assume a guy like that is coming at you."

Frelick kept fighting, just as he had his whole career. He fouled off half of the first eight pitches he saw and ultimately worked the count full.

Seymour became predictable, a creature of habit, not doubling up on the same pitch twice to that point.

That was until the ninth pitch of the at-bat.

Seymour tested Frelick again, throwing a four-seamer for the second consecutive pitch, this one hanging over the inner third of the plate. Frelick didn't miss it, crushing a solo shot to tie the score at four apiece.

SAL FRELICK. TIE GAME. pic.twitter.com/aSkT0NcTRU

Bedlam ensued at American Family Field. Yellow towels twirled in unison throughout the stands. And as the stadium erupted, so did Frelick, rounding first base. He pointed toward his bullpen. They pointed back. Because for these Brewers, deficits have become invitations. Adversity only fuels the congregation.

Winning feels inevitable, as Milwaukee's 5-4 victory in Game 2 demonstrated.

Frelick, who'd hit just four regular-season home runs, didn't deliver the final blow. Cooper Pratt did. Yet he made certain the Brewers would have the chance to land it.

"He hit four lasers tonight," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said. "He hit four balls right on the screws. We talked about it in pregame, he's got the 'it factor.'"

The "it factor" that Murphy sees in Frelick was forged on the high school football field, where he earned Massachusetts Gatorade Player of the Year honors as a quarterback in 2017.

The "it factor" was cultivated on ice skates, in a state where hockey borders on religion.

The "it factor" was instilled by his father, Jeff, who played football at the University of Pittsburgh before injuries cut his career short.

The "it factor" was fortified through doubt. For all his accolades, Frelick still had to fight to be seen. At just 5-foot-8, he was accustomed to being overlooked.

"Everybody's built differently," Murphy said. "Everybody expresses it differently. I think that Sal was a young man that had very good physical skills, but he wasn't big in stature like a lot of these guys are. So, as a smaller guy, I think he always was having to prove how great he was. He has this edge about him that he believes in himself."

It's an edge Murphy likened to longtime Red Sox second baseman Dustin Pedroia, whom he coached at Arizona State. Pedroia possessed physical gifts Frelick doesn't, but both share an insatiable desire to dominate, regardless of what their natural abilities suggest.

"Murph will tell you firsthand that it's never pretty watching me take a swing or run or field or whatever it might be," Frelick said. "I just always felt like that was where I was able to get my edge was just kind of turn everything into a competition. Put myself in the box and iso me and the pitcher. Or on defense, just iso me and the ball. How can I beat it?"

Maybe not the prettiest swing, but the prettiest of outcomes. A reminder of how October has a way of washing away the tribulations endured just to get there. How an entire regular season can be reduced to a footnote when a player delivers in the postseason.

Because Frelick's regular season was anything but memorable. He hit just .230 with a .614 OPS.

A right shoulder injury sidelined him for six weeks, costing him half of July and most of August. And when he returned, his role had diminished. Though the Brewers have long embraced platoons, Frelick found himself largely relegated to facing right-handed pitching.

Luis Lara, the 21-year-old prospect called up in July, had taken some of Frelick's playing time down the stretch. But Frelick didn't sulk. His focus only sharpened. His emotions, rarely on display, are encased in steel. He can take tough coaching.

In May, Murphy acknowledged he didn't tell Frelick he loved him enough. So he scribbled himself a reminder: Tell Sal, "I love you, Sal."

Then he thought better of it. Crossed it out.

"You know what? He doesn't need me to tell him that," Murphy said.

Beneath the humor was a truth: Frelick doesn't need constant reassurance. His competitiveness, his confidence, his unwavering sense of self -- all nurtured over time.

"It was great," Frelick said of his home run. "Like, sort of this blackout feeling. Then it kind of ends. We were tied. We weren't winning or anything."

The Brewers now have a chance to capture the first World Series championship in franchise history as the series shifts to Tropicana Field, where the Rays have been dominant, going 59-27 this season, including the postseason.

It will be another test for Milwaukee in a series decided by one run in the first two games. The Rays won't go quietly.

Yet at every turn, Milwaukee has answered the challenge.

And on Saturday night, Frelick answered his.

Because as leaves fall and flowers wilt, Frelick has bloomed.

Chinese Cars are a no-go for the U.S., effectively. Any car that uses software or hardware made by a Chinese company is subject to the connected cars ban and can’t be sold in the U.S. Just ask Polestar. Still, the U.S. car market is huge, the second largest behind China. Some Chinese automakers still want to find a way into such a large market, especially one in dire need of inexpensive options. Chery, for one, isn't giving up.

According to reporting from Reuters, the company is open to trying different things to get access to the U.S. market. Vice President ⁠Charlie Zhang told Reuters that Chery is confident that it can reach the U.S. market and build a car that meets the data security laws.

"Definitely from a technical point of view, we will be able to find a China-free solution in terms of hardware ​and software,” Zhang told Reuters.

One way that could possibly be accomplished is through a partnership with American automakers. However, Zhang didn’t explain to Reuters what exactly that kind of partnership would look like for Chery.

Still, this is markedly different than what other Chinese brands have said with respect to the U.S. market. Some brands, like BYD and Geely, have been open about their desire to come to the U.S., even going as far as hosting American journalists to drive their product. Yet, these companies have been clear that right now isn’t the right time to sell here; the geopolitical tensions between the U.S. and China aren’t great. Car manufacturers that already operate in the U.S. are very much against Chinese cars coming here, and there’s more legislation proposed to stop any of them from trying at all. But this isn’t stopping Chery.

Chery has an incentive to sell outside of China, though. While Chery’s sales performance in China has historically been mediocre, it has been one of the strongest-selling Chinese brands in export markets. Chery and its sister brands like Jaecoo, Lepas, Jetour, and iCar have shown surprising results. The Jaecoo 7 PHEV has been the UK’s best-selling new car for two different months this year. The consensus is that Jaecoo’s products are reasonably priced, feature-packed vehicles, something that resonates with buyers on a budget. Given that the U.S. market is starved for budget-friendly choices, Chery could do well if it figured out a way to sell here.

Although right now Chery’s plan for U.S. sales is vague, Zhang did tell Reuters that Chery plans to sell in Canada next year. They're getting closer to the U.S. market, but a true entry seems to be a ways off.
