#3758. Decades Before Landman, Billy Bob Thornton Was In A Sitcom Featuring A TV Legend

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Billy Bob Thornton might be 71 years old, but as his Paramount+ series "Landman" proves, he hasn't missed a step. Taylor Sheridan's oil drama has been the perfect showcase for Thornton's considerable talents, and despite the fact "Landman" has yet to earn any Emmy nominations, the show has been consistently well received, with Thornton garnering Actor Award and Critics Choice Award nominations. Long before he reminded us all of his acting prowess, however, Thornton appeared on a very different TV series when he supported sitcom royalty and the future co-star of his film "Sling Blade," John Ritter, on the early 1990s CBS sitcom "Hearts Afire."

The show was created by Linda Bloodworth-Thomason, who'd previously written for such well-known shows as "M*A*S*H" and had contributed an episode to "Rhonda" (one of many Emmy-winning sitcoms that we don't really talk about anymore). Of course, she remains best known for her 1980s/'90s sitcoms "Designing Women" and the Burt Reynolds-led "Evening Shade." In the case of "Hearts Afire," Bloodworth-Thomason teamed up with another established star in Ritter, who famously played Jack Tripper on the legendary ABC sitcom "Three's Company" between 1977 and 1984. She also drafted in Markie Post of "The Fall Guy" and "Night Court" fame.

The two stars play politically opposed individuals who develop an attraction to one another over the course of the show's first season. Thornton, meanwhile, plays Ritter's best friend, who divorces his wife and manages to stick around for the entire three seasons of "Hearts Afire," despite a major cast shakeup mid-way through.

In 1996, Billy Bob Thornton cast John Ritter as Vaughan Cunningham in "Sling Blade," the Oscar-winning movie that Thornton claims changed his life. By that point, the pair had worked together for three years on "Hearts Afire," and according to a 2003 Herald Times article, Thornton took the opportunity between takes on the sitcom to "crack Ritter up with the mentally challenged hillbilly character he'd use four years later as the centerpiece of 'Sling Blade.'" During their time on "Hearts Afire," then, Thornton and Ritter developed a close relationship that lasted until Ritter's passing in 2003.

At the time Thornton was cast on "Hearts Afire," he was still an up-and-coming actor. He'd made his screen debut in 1986's "Hunter's Blood" and racked up some small roles in TV shows including "Matlock" and Linda Bloodworth-Thomason's "Evening Shade." Thus, when Bloodworth-Thomason set to work on "Hearts Afire," she thought of Thornton.

The show stars Ritter as John Hartman, a father of two whose wife has recently left him for her therapist. John is also a conservative senator's (George Gaynes) aide whose political views aren't exactly aligned with those of Georgie Anne Lahti (Markie Post), a reporter who hopes to become the senator's new press secretary. Nonetheless, the two are drawn to each other.

Thornton, meanwhile, played Ritter's friend Billy Bob Davis. (He'd previously used his real name in "Hunter's Blood" and the pilot for a show called "Circus" as well.) In the series premiere, Billy Bob is kicked out of his house by his wife, Mavis (Wendie Jo Sperber). Meanwhile, Georgie moves in with John, and the first season follows their developing romance and experiences in Washington D.C. But when Season 2 arrived, the show underwent a major retooling.

"Hearts Afire" Season 1 debuted in September 1992 and ran for 23 episodes, ending in March 1993. When it returned for a second season in October of the same year, it was a different show. According to a contemporaneous report from Deseret News, the series' first season was "both too political and often of questionable taste" due to multiple jokes that were "completely inappropriate for an early time slot." Whatever the case, CBS decided the show needed reworking, and in the Season 2 premiere, the now-married John Hartman and Georgie Anne Lahti decide to move back to John's home town in the midwest with John's two children. There, they buy the local newspaper and attempt to create a wholesome life for their family in a town that John often doesn't recognize from his childhood.

While the political aspect of the show was abandoned along with several characters, Billy Bob Thornton's Billy Bob Davis made it to the second season, moving with the couple alongside his mother, Velma Davis (Maxine Stuart). It was a good thing the producers kept him on, too. As the Deseret News put it, Thornton's "deadpan observations and dry wit steal almost every scene he's in."

"Hearts Afire" ran for two more seasons, wrapping up after its third season in February 1995. During that time, the show benefited from the presence of another towering TV figure, Ed Asner, who played Georgie's father, George Lahti, in 12 episodes of the series. While the show didn't continue past Season 3, Thornton and Ritter's relationship very much did, with the pair re-teaming for "Sling Blade" and reuniting once again when Ritter was cast alongside Thornton in "Bad Santa."

#5758. Backer of hemp regulation looks to reconciliation process

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Rep. Morgan Griffith said Monday that a GOP reconciliation package in the upcoming lame-duck session could become a possible vehicle for his legislation aimed at regulating hemp-derived products.

The Virginia Republican said he was considering coupling his bill with tax legislation to meet the strict restrictions of the filibuster-proof reconciliation process, which can be used only for measures that have a significant budgetary impact to comply with Senate rules.

“I think we need to get my THC regulatory bill passed, and if we could get that into a reconciliation bill before the 11th of December, that’d be fabulous,” he told reporters after presiding over a pro forma House session. THC, or tetrahydrocannabinol, is the main psychoactive ingredient in the cannabis plant.

Griffith said in a later statement that his bill could ride in a reconciliation package if “combined with a measure related to taxes on hemp-derived products, such as Rep. Van Duyne’s Beverage Regulatory Parity Act.”

The legislation offered by Rep. Beth Van Duyne, R-Texas, and Rep. Greg Landsman, D-Ohio, seeks to establish a regulatory system for hemp-derived beverages similar to how alcohol is treated, and would impose a federal excise tax based on the amount of intoxicating THC in those beverages.

Griffith’s bill would charge the Food and Drug Administration with developing regulations within three years to set limits on intoxicating substances in hemp-derived and CBD products. If the FDA can’t complete the process within that time, the bill would set default intoxication limits of 5 milligrams per serving and 30 milligrams per package.

As part of the short-term government funding patch passed in September, lawmakers agreed to temporarily delay a ban on sales of certain intoxicating hemp-derived products through Dec. 11.

The ban’s delay reignited GOP tensions over loosening cannabis regulations. Griffith, who has called for Congress to approve a regulatory framework for hemp-derived products, acknowledged internal rifts over the delay.

“I get it. Andy Harris, Ted Budd, some of the others, they just want to stop it,” Griffith, a senior member of the Energy and Commerce Committee, said Monday, referring to the delay. But he also warned, “I truly believe if we don’t do something before that Dec.11th deadline, that it’s going to create all kinds of issues.”

“So, now the question becomes: Do you want to have good products that are third-party tested, that [have] a restriction on the age of purchase, that has all this other stuff? Some of the states are doing that,” he said. “But if you’re going to look at it from a federal level, I don’t think we can stop it.”

Some Republicans in both chambers have expressed hopes for passing a third reconciliation bill before the new Congress convenes in January. But others remain doubtful, saying Republicans have yet to agree on what such a package should contain with little time to take up the measure before the end of the year.

The House adopted a budget resolution in July laying the framework for a reconciliation bill, but the Senate took no action on its own budget blueprint. Both measures would provide roughly $100 billion for Iran war and other military funding, agriculture aid and some voter ID measures.

Sen. Cynthia Lummis, R-Wyo., told reporters Monday that she would like to see a reconciliation bill passed after the elections. But she added, “It will be very challenging to sync up with the House and the Senate on what should be included.”

She also noted she would like to see parts of a key regulatory bill for digital assets included in the reconciliation bill, along with some tax provisions.

“But opening the tax code is challenging,” she said, while also pointing to the strict rules governing the reconciliation process in the Senate that require the bill to have some kind of budgetary impact.

#5238. OpenAI launches GPT-6.1 Terra for faster, better-performing AI agents at half the price

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI Group PBC today released GPT-6.1 Terra, an upgrade to the middle tier model in its lineup of frontier artificial intelligence systems.

The new model, the successor to GTP-5.6 Terra, costs around 50% less while handling computer use tasks faster and with greater accuracy.

Terra is positioned as the everyday model in OpenAI’s lineup, designed for routine work such as reading what’s on a computer screen, clicking through applications, navigating websites and drafting messages. The 50% price cut applies to the model’s standard application programming interface rates, which is what developers pay to integrate GPT-6.1 Terra into their own applications.

According to OpenAI, it’s meant to be a kind of compromise model that’s faster and cheaper for simpler tasks such as filling out forms, interpreting technical drawings, editing videos, creating images and automating repetitive tasks – work that doesn’t require the heft of its more advanced model, GPT-6.1 Sol.

“We’re introducing GPT-6.1 Terra, a fast and efficient model that combines detailed visual reasoning and precise computer use alongside clearer, more natural writing,” OpenAI wrote in a blog post introducing the model. “A significant step up from GPT-5.6 Terra, it excels at the communication and computer-based tasks that fill the workday, from drafting proposals and interpreting technical drawings to filling out forms and editing videos.”

GPT-6.1 Terra: delivering fast and capable computer use at a lower price.

GPT-6.1 Terra understands what’s on screen, takes action across apps, writes naturally, and is a more capable model for your whole workday with the efficiency to take on more of it. pic.twitter.com/8JTN1yonmV

OpenAI said the model is available now for all ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex. ChatGPT Work is the agent mode feature within ChatGPT, designed for completing work on behalf of users in third-party applications and browsers, while Codex is OpenAI’s software coding tool.

One concern with GPT-6.1 Terra is that its more advanced capabilities appear to come at a slight cost in terms of safety. In its blog post, OpenAI said that in nearly 30% of simulated runs, Terra went against restrictions to complete tasks using methods it had no authorization to use.

In addition, OpenAI revealed that GPT-6.1 Terra took unauthorized actions in 13.75% of use cases after coming across messages that came from other AI agents. The company’s system card also shows that researchers found evidence that the model understood it was being tested.

Nonetheless, OpenAI said GPT-6.1 Terra did better in some metrics. For instance, on an internal test built using prompts designed to trick models into making mistakes, its rate of factual errors at low reasoning effort fell to just 16.7%, down from 53.6% with GPT-5.6 Terra. When making maximum effort, its rate of factual errors dropped to just 4.3%.

On the Terminal-Bench 4.0 test, which measures coding agents on tasks done through a computer’s command-line terminal, it solved 50.3% of all tasks when using maximum reasoning effort, up from 21.5% with GPT-5.6 Terra. OpenAI said the model completed these tasks around three-times faster on average than more powerful variants such as GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra.

The release of Terra follows the debut of GPT-6.1 Sol last month. The lineup also includes a lightweight model variant called GPT-6.1 Luna.

The base EV6 Light standard-range RWD model starts at $37,900, not including the $1,545 destination fee, which is the same as last year.

Powered by a 63 kWh battery, the standard-range variant offers up to 237 miles of range. Upgrading to the EV6 Light long range RWD model costs an extra $3,300, starting at $41,200, but delivers up to 319 miles of range using a larger 84 kWh battery.

Kia said that with the EV6 MSRP unchanged and the launch of the new EV3, it’s “making EVs more attainable for those who embrace the fully electric lifestyle.”

The 2027 Kia EV3 starts at just $29,890, or about $8,000 less than the EV6. Kia’s entry-level electric SUV is available in five trims: Light, Wind, Land, GT-Line, and GT.

The lowest-priced EV3 Light FWD model uses a 58.3 kWh battery and delivers an EPA-estimated range of up to 221 miles.

Higher-priced trims, including the Wind, Land, GT-Line, and GT, use a larger 81.4 kWh battery that delivers up to 321 miles of range on a single charge. Like the EV6, the EV3 is also available with front-wheel-drive and all-wheel-drive powertrain options.

Both also include a native NACS port so that you can access Tesla Superchargers without a separate adapter.

However, the EV6 uses an 800V architecture, enabling faster charging than the EV3, which uses a 400V system.

#5745. DHS suspends visa-free travel program for Chinese nationals amid birth tourism, security concerns

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

State Department spokesperson Tommy Pigott told Fox News Digital that the Trump administration is ramping up efforts to curb birth tourism.

The Trump administration is suspending a visa-free travel program for Chinese nationals visiting a U.S. island territory after Republican lawmakers warned it could facilitate birth tourism and pose national security risks.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s decision targets a Biden-era program that allowed prescreened Chinese nationals to visit the Northern Mariana Islands for up to 14 days without a visa.

In a notice announcing the decision, the Department of Homeland Security cited concerns that the program could facilitate birth tourism, espionage and illegal onward travel to nearby Guam, a key hub for the U.S. military.

The program at issue was created in 2024 and took effect the following year. More than 3,000 Chinese nationals used it to enter the Northern Mariana Islands in fiscal year 2025, according to DHS.

Those travelers were screened by Customs and Border Protection, but did not have to obtain a visa through a U.S. consulate, the department said.

The suspension is set to take effect at 2 p.m. EDT on Oct. 30 and will apply to both new applicants and travelers who have already been approved. Those already in the territory under the program may finish their authorized stays but will need a visa for future visits unless another lawful exception applies.

DHS said the suspension will remain in place while the department evaluates the program with the secretaries of state and the interior.

The department warned that the program could make it easier for Chinese nationals to travel to the territory to give birth.

"While birth tourism has long been an issue in the CNMI, the availability of visa-exempt travel and reduced vetting under EVS-TAP may lower barriers to this practice," the notice says.

DHS also said birth tourism can strain the islands’ medical system. The notice did not provide a number of births linked to travelers who entered through the program, formally known as the Economic Vitality & Security Travel Authorization Program.

The department warned that visa-free travel could be exploited for intelligence gathering near U.S. military facilities. The Northern Mariana Islands are home to Tinian Air Base, while Guam hosts several military installations.

Although the program did not permit visitors to travel to Guam or the U.S. mainland, DHS cited previous cases involving Chinese nationals smuggled by boat from the Northern Mariana Islands to Guam. Those cases involved earlier travel policies or other streamlined arrangements, but DHS said they showed a vulnerability that the newer program had not eliminated.

The suspension comes as the Trump administration pursues a broader crackdown on birth tourism. A State Department task force launched Aug. 12 had revoked more than 750 visas tied to suspected birth tourism activity as of late August, the department previously told Fox News Digital.

State Department spokesman Tommy Pigott told Fox News Digital that officials were working with international partners and law enforcement to identify travel patterns that could point to birth tourism networks.

Pigott said some networks help visitors conceal the purpose of their trips and then coach them on how to avoid paying medical bills.

The Northern Mariana Islands program is separate from the visitor visa process targeted by those revocations.

WASHINGTON (AP) — The Trump administration is attempting to prosecute James Comey for what it calls a decade-old leak of classified information, an effort the former FBI director's lawyers immediately dismissed as invalid and outside the legal time limit for when the Justice Department can file a criminal indictment.

The allegations from the Justice Department were revealed Monday in newly unsealed court documents that shed light on a closed-door fight that represents an unmistakable escalation in the administration's targeting of Comey, a longtime critic and foe of President Donald Trump who has faced two other criminal cases since the Republican returned to the White House last year. The effort to prosecute Comey is also part of a series of politically charged investigative actions the department has undertaken in the run-up to next week's midterm elections.

"Well, they're back — again," Comey said in a video statement Monday. "It would almost be funny if it weren't such an appalling abuse of the Department of Justice – one that we cannot become numb to. Nothing's changed with me; I'm still innocent, I'm still not afraid of these people — and I still believe in an independent federal judiciary."

The Justice Department said it had filed what's known as a "criminal information" to extend the statute of limitations for when they can permissibly bring an indictment.

"Filing a criminal information to toll the statute of limitations is a widely recognized practice in federal court and has been approved by multiple Circuit Courts of Appeals. This is an ongoing criminal investigation, and we will not comment further," a department spokesperson said.

An information can be filed without the approval of a judge or a grand jury's finding of probable cause to a court. Comey's lawyers made clear that they intend to fight the charges as invalid and barred by a 10-year statute of limitations.

"For the third time in little more than one year, the Department of Justice has attempted to charge Mr. Comey with a federal crime. Mr. Comey steadfastly maintains his innocence," his attorney, Patrick Fitzgerald, said in a statement.

The latest allegation concerns an October 2016 New York Times story related to a secret court order that required Yahoo to scan emails for messages potentially linked to a foreign terrorist organization. The story cited two government officials who spoke on the condition of anonymity about the order from a judge on the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court that Yahoo was barred from publicly disclosing.

Federal authorities, including prosecutor John Durham, began investigating the disclosure in early 2017. Durham, who later investigated the FBI's conduct as it scrutinized potential ties between Russia and Trump's 2016 presidential campaign, wrote a classified memo in December 2018 recommending no criminal charges against anyone in connection with the disclosure, Comey's attorneys said in a recent court filing.

In January 2019, the U.S. Attorney's office for the District of Columbia opened a second investigation of the same matter under the code name "Tropical Vortex." But that investigation was closed without any charges filed in February 2020.

"Both times the Government determined that no charges should be brought against anyone. Now, ten years after the article was published, the Government filed an "Information" against Mr. Comey that was not approved by a judge nor issued by a grand jury. It purports to charge Mr. Comey with crimes that he did not commit," Fitzgerald said.

Prosecutors in a newly unsealed filing said investigators found evidence that Comey "sought to correct misinformation which had appeared in the press by way of Reuters the day before" that New York Times story was published, directed his staff to speak to a journalist at the newspaper related to accuracy concerns "and thereby illegally transferred classified information."

The criminal information was filed under seal on Oct. 6, 2016, 10 years after the story was published. "But," Comey's lawyers said, "the applicable statute of limitations explicitly demands an indictment within 10 years of the offense; an information does not suffice."

They have asked a judge to dismiss the information, a request that is still pending.

The first criminal case against Comey, filed in federal court in Virginia, accused him of having lied to Congress in 2020 but was dismissed by a judge who concluded that the prosecutor who brought the charges was illegally appointed. A separate pending indictment charges the former FBI director with threatening Trump through an Instagram photo of seashells in the numerical arrangement of "86 47," a case he has sought to dismiss and some legal experts have greeted with skepticism.

The cases are part of a Trump retribution campaign that has even extended to Comey's daughter, Maurene, who was fired from her job as a Justice Department prosecutor in New York last year in a termination that a federal judge on Monday ruled was illegal.

Comey was the FBI director when Trump took office in 2017, having been appointed by then-President Barack Obama, a Democrat, and serving before that as a senior Justice Department official in President George W. Bush's Republican administration.

But the relationship was strained from the start, including after Comey resisted a request by Trump at a private dinner to pledge his personal loyalty to the president -- an overture that so unnerved the FBI director that he documented it in a contemporaneous memorandum.

Trump fired Comey in May 2017 amid an FBI investigation into potential ties between Russia and Trump's campaign. That inquiry, later taken over by special counsel Robert Mueller, found that while Russia interfered in the 2016 election and the Trump team welcomed the help, there was insufficient evidence to prove a criminal collaboration.

The two men have been bitterly at odds ever since Comey's firing. Comey in 2018 published a memoir, "A Higher Loyalty," that painted Trump in deeply unflattering ways, likening him to a mafia don and characterizing him "untethered to truth." Trump, for his part, has repeatedly claimed that Comey should face charges for "treason" — an accusation Comey dismissed as "dumb lies" — and called him an "untruthful slime ball."

#5749. The Hitchhiker's Guide to why removing Ilhan Omar from Congress could be harder than Trump expects

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fox News chief congressional correspondent Chad Pergram reports on President Donald Trump's calls for Rep. Ilhan Omar to resign amid questions about her name and legal status.

Republicans hope there are lots of Democrats who won’t win re-election next week and return to Capitol Hill. Republicans are imploring voters to kick them out.

But President Trump is focused on bouncing one Democrat from Congress who likely wins re-election in a solidly blue district. That’s Rep. Ilhan Omar (D-Minn.). She carried her district two years ago with nearly three-quarters of the vote. But President Trump questions whether Omar is in the country legally and, therefore, qualified to sit in Congress.

"She used a fake name to be sworn in," said the President. "She's a little troublemaker."

The Department of Justice is investigating Omar for potential immigration fraud. Federal authorities raided her Washington, DC home recently and removed 17 items. That included phones, notebooks, a camera and a Somali phrase book for her kids.

An image of Ilhan Omar overlaid over her neighborhood in DC. (Getty Images)

President Trump suggests that her name is not "Ilhan Omar" and that it’s "an entirely different name name. Ihan Nur Sayeed Elmi. It's close."

A federal filing in that inquiry lists the Congresswoman’s name as "Ilhan Nur Said Elmi, a/k/a Ilhan Abdullahi Omar."

That’s why the President says "she must immediately resign from Congress."

A reporter for Fox Digital confronted the Congresswoman about her name.

"Is your name Ilhan Sayed, Ilhan Nur Sayed Alami, or Ilhan Abdullahi Omar?" asked Rashid Umar Abbasi, working for Fox.

"Ilhan Abdullahi Omar. You know it," replied Omar.

"It's Ilhan Abdullahi Omar," she confirmed.

The Clerk of the House of Representatives officially lists the Congresswoman as solely "Ilhan Omar" on 15 different financial disclosure reports dating back to 2018. That’s even before she entered Congress. The same with the Minnesota state legislature where Omar served prior to coming to Washington.

The discrepancy is why the President wants the House to banish Omar.

"She will not be legally allowed to be a member of that august body that sits in Washington," said President Trump.

However, Article I, Section 5 of the Constitution grants both bodies of Congress final authority about who it admits to its membership.

At least one House Republican is willing to explore that. But only if the facts merit it.

"The House has the prerogative to seat or not seat and to throw Members out if it so chooses," said Rep. Morgan Griffith (R-Va.). "There ought to be good sound reasons for it."

It’s a different set of rules, but Griffith had experience with this as the Virginia House of Delegates grappled with a similar issue about seating lawmakers in the Commonwealth in 1988.

"It would be extraordinary. And it should be extraordinary and it should not be used except in extraordinary circumstances," said Griffith.

Yours truly asked what would meet that bar to block someone from serving in the House.

"It's like the Supreme Court said about pornography. I'll know it when I see it," said Griffith, paraphrasing late Justice Potter Stewart from a key 1964 obscenity case.

House Republicans have two options to deal with Omar if they are truly determined to take action – regardless of what is proven or not. Qualifications for the House are pretty simple. You must be a citizen and a resident of the state you are representing. And, you must be 25 years old. You could even be convicted of a crime and serving time in jail. The Constitution is silent on all other credentials. You could also have a resume which is without a blemish. It doesn’t matter. The House could still take action against you.

The first conceivable option could come now. The Constitution grants the House and Senate the right to expel Members – provided there’s a two-thirds vote. Any scenario to expel Omar seems remarkable. Especially since she hasn’t been convicted of a crime. And even if she is charged, there are lots of other issues which could emerge before a trial.

It would be strange to have the executive branch of government trying to remove a Member of the legislative branch of government. Moreover, it’s likely that the House counsel would contest any effort by federal prosecutors to procure Omar’s official House communication devices as well as documents. There’s a firewall between the legislative branch and executive branch known as the "Speech or Debate Clause." Article I, Section 6 of the Constitution grants lawmakers privileges tied to their official duties. So getting those papers — let alone potentially deporting her as some have suggested – is a far cry. Especially since she’s a Member and this all hinges on expulsion.

Moreover, two-thirds of the House will never vote to expel a Member under these circumstances unless there’s ironclad proof of wrongdoing. That’s why the House has only expelled six Members in its history. The last was former Rep. George Santos (R-N.Y.) in 2023.

However, what may be an issue is whether or not there could be an objection to seating Omar when the new Congress convenes in January.

In 1968, then Rep. Adam Clayton Powell (D-NY) faced a criminal investigation. House Speaker John McCormack (D-Mass.) refused to seat Powell – even though he met all other requirements. The Supreme Court ruled in Powell v. McCormack that the House can’t exclude a Member if they meet the basic standards to serve in the House.

That said, it’s possible there could be motions not to seat Omar – or even other progressive Members-elect who have said controversial things about the U.S. and Constitution. Such a motion would come right after the House chooses a Speaker. But the Speaker has not sworn-in the rest of the Members. The House would then swear-in everyone – except the Member or Members-elect who may face questions about serving. Once the House swears in the rest of the body, it can then vote on a motion not to expel those persons, table the motion, or refer the motion to a committee for investigation.

In 1985, the House did not swear-in Rep. Frank McCloskey (D-Ind.) and decided to investigate whether or not he won his seat in the Hoosier State. The House didn’t swear-in McCloskey for several months.

Based on Powell v. McCormack, it’s hard to envision a scenario where the House declines to seat someone. But it might not do it immediately based on the McCloskey case.

Of course, it’s always probable that the House could seat someone – and then expel them. That doesn’t collide headlong with the Powell decision. But considering that the House has only expelled six members, those circumstances are doubtful, too.

What’s in a name? Especially for Ilhan Omar. Her adversaries may raise questions about her name. But it’s doubtful they can do anything about her position.
