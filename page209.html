#589. Astronomers uncover a mysterious magnetic web above the Sun

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a surprisingly complex network of magnetic structures hidden above regions of the Sun that appear relatively calm. Using the Sunrise-III solar observatory, which traveled high into Earth's stratosphere aboard a balloon in 2024, an international research team led by the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) discovered intricate, thread-like magnetic features that had previously been difficult to observe.

The findings reveal that even the Sun's seemingly quiet regions contain elaborate magnetic structures. They also offer new clues to how energy travels upward through the solar atmosphere and contributes to its heating.

A Hidden Web of Magnetic Threads Above the Sun

Sunrise-III detected numerous thin, elongated magnetic structures woven into a larger network of magnetic fields above a quiet region of the Sun. This network, known as the magnetic canopy, extends through the chromosphere, a layer of the solar atmosphere located above the Sun's visible surface.

The canopy develops as magnetic fields concentrated at the solar surface (photosphere) spread outward and arch upward into the chromosphere (chromosphere). Rather than forming a relatively smooth, uniformly expanding structure, however, the new observations reveal that the canopy contains many narrow, thread-like features.

To investigate how these structures form, researchers used computer simulations that successfully reproduced the patterns recorded by Sunrise-III. The results suggest that movements on the solar surface twist magnetic field lines, creating the intricate structures observed higher in the atmosphere.

The observatory also examined another quiet region near the solar limb (visible edge of the Sun). There, it measured magnetic fields within spicules, narrow jets of solar material that extend upward from the surface. These observations revealed how the distribution of magnetic fields changes with increasing height above the Sun's visible edge.

Why the Sun's Quiet Regions Are Difficult to Study

Although the Sun is constantly active, much of its surface lacks prominent features such as sunspots. Scientists describe these comparatively inactive areas as "quiet." They cover most of the Sun, making them especially important for understanding how our star behaves.

However, quiet does not mean magnetically inactive. These regions contain magnetic fields that are generally weaker than those found in more active areas of the Sun. Their subtle structures are difficult to measure, especially with telescopes on the ground, where turbulence in Earth's atmosphere can blur fine details.

Sunrise-III was designed to overcome this obstacle by carrying a powerful solar telescope far above most of the atmosphere.

A Balloon-Borne Telescope Reveals New Solar Details

Sunrise-III is an international research project built around a 1-meter telescope carried by a high-altitude balloon. During its 2024 mission, the observatory floated approximately 35 kilometers (about 22 miles) above Earth in the stratosphere, where atmospheric interference is greatly reduced.

This elevated vantage point allowed the telescope to capture exceptionally detailed measurements of the Sun's magnetic fields.

The balloon's gondola carried three scientific instruments developed through contributions from different countries. One of these was SCIP (Sunrise Chromospheric Infrared spectroPolarimeter), a near-infrared instrument developed under NAOJ's leadership.

SCIP is designed to measure magnetic fields continuously across different layers of the solar atmosphere, from the photosphere to the chromosphere. This capability allowed researchers to examine how magnetic structures extend and change with altitude rather than studying only a single layer.

New Clues to How the Sun's Atmosphere Is Heated

By observing from the stratosphere, Sunrise-III provided researchers with an unusually detailed picture of magnetic fields above the Sun's quiet regions. Its measurements revealed that the chromosphere contains far more intricate magnetic structures than a simple expanding canopy would suggest.

Understanding these structures could help scientists investigate how energy generated by motions near the solar surface is transferred into higher layers of the atmosphere.

The discovery offers an important step toward explaining how magnetic activity contributes to heating the Sun's atmosphere, including in regions that appear calm when viewed from afar.

#599. These tiny snakes roll four times faster than they slither

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Tiny snakes found in Southeast Asia have developed an extraordinary way to escape danger. Instead of relying on the familiar side-to-side motion of slithering, dwarf reedsnakes can rapidly roll across the ground, reaching speeds about four times faster than their usual movement.

Scientists have now uncovered how these small reptiles accomplish such an unusual feat. By studying video recordings captured from multiple angles, researchers discovered that the snakes coordinate their movements with remarkable precision. While the rear portions of their bodies rotate rapidly, their heads remain relatively steady, facing forward and staying at a nearly constant height.

[See link to video after the end of the article.]

The findings reveal how an animal can achieve an unexpected form of movement through specialized behavior and body control, even without the physical structures normally needed for rolling.

Rolling might seem like an effective way to travel quickly, but it presents a major challenge for living creatures.

A wheel can spin continuously around a fixed axle, allowing it to rotate without disturbing whatever it supports. Animals, however, have no comparable mechanism separating their rotating bodies from their heads.

If an animal's entire body spins together, its head and sensory organs must also rotate. This can make it difficult to maintain balance, adjust body position, and steer in the intended direction. As rolling speed increases, these problems become even harder to manage.

Yet dwarf reedsnakes belonging to the genus Pseudorabdion have found a way around these limitations.

These small Southeast Asian snakes are among the few animals known to deliberately roll as a means of escaping potential predators. Their unusual behavior has appeared in field guides and was previously documented during a field observation, but scientists had not fully understood the movements that made it possible.

Tiny Snakes Roll Four Times Faster Than They Slither

To investigate this remarkable escape strategy, researchers filmed dwarf reedsnakes from several angles and carefully examined how different parts of their bodies moved during rolling.

The results revealed impressive speeds. While rolling, the snakes reached peak forward velocities ranging from 1.72 to 3.69 meters per second, approximately four times their maximum speed when moving through lateral undulation, the familiar side-to-side motion used by most snakes.

Despite traveling so quickly, the snakes were able to follow a narrow racetrack measuring just 20 centimeters across, demonstrating that their rolling movements are not simply uncontrolled tumbling.

Closer examination of the recordings revealed a surprisingly intricate sequence of movements.

Rather than spinning their entire bodies together, the snakes alternate between rolling on one side and swinging a curved section of their bodies downward beside their heads. The researchers called this downward sweeping movement a "stroke."

During each successive cycle, the side touching the ground switches, and the stroke changes direction from left to right or right to left. By repeating this carefully timed pattern, the snakes generate continuous forward rolling motion.

The Remarkable Secret Behind Their High-Speed Escape

One of the most surprising discoveries involved what happens to the snakes' heads during these rapid movements.

Although the rear portions of their bodies undergo substantial rotation, the snakes keep their heads pointed in the direction they are traveling. Rather than spinning along with the rest of the body, the head remains comparatively stable.

The researchers also found that the snakes maintain a relatively consistent head height above the ground. This stabilization becomes especially noticeable at higher speeds, when the snakes tend to hold their heads elevated and minimize upward and downward movements.

Keeping the head steady while the rest of the body rotates may help the animals maintain control and navigate effectively during their rapid escapes.

This ability is particularly remarkable because the snakes accomplish it without a mechanical separation between the rotating and stabilized portions of their bodies.

What Rolling Snakes Reveal About Evolution

The discovery demonstrates how sophisticated coordination can allow animals to overcome physical limitations that might otherwise make certain types of movement impractical.

Although dwarf reedsnakes lack anything resembling a wheel's axle, they effectively divide their bodies into regions with different functions. Much of the body performs the powerful rotational movements needed for propulsion, while the head remains stabilized to support controlled travel.

This unusual strategy allows the snakes to combine speed, directional control, and rapid body rotation in a way that would be difficult to predict from their anatomy alone.

More broadly, the findings highlight an important principle of animal evolution: physical structure does not necessarily determine the full range of movements an animal can perform. Innovative behaviors and precise muscular coordination can open up entirely new possibilities, allowing creatures to accomplish feats that their body shapes might initially suggest are impossible.

#312. Why popular airplanes are getting scrapped years early

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

GOODYEAR, Ariz. — An Airbus A320neo, one of the world's most popular planes, rolled off the Mobile, Alabama, factory line in July 2022, painted bright yellow for its next destination: Spirit Airlines.

It was built to fly for decades. But this May, less than four years later, it was stripped for parts worth millions and crushed, the steel teeth of an excavator tearing through its fuselage at a facility on the outskirts of Phoenix.

More aircraft are getting dismantled years, if not decades, earlier than expected, industry executives told CNBC. It is the result of a shortage of parts, none more precious and expensive than aircraft engines, that has exasperated airlines and driven up costs for years.

Even after the engines are removed, firms are looking for parts as varied as landing gear and antennae to sell.

"It's the first time in my career that involved purchasing an aircraft that's less than five years since date of manufacture," said Bill Thompson, vice president who leads aircraft and engine acquisition for the Americas at aviation asset management and trading firm EirTrade Aviation, which bought the less than 4-year-old Airbus A320neo earlier this year.

He said the company paid "single-digit" millions each for that airplane and a similarly aged one, and it expects to make a profit from selling the more than 1,000 parts it took off them before they were crushed.

"I don't know if you're going to sell the coffee makers but you never know. Sometimes you do," he said.

Twenty-one newest-generation aircraft were torn down from June 2025 through this past August, according to aviation data and advisory firm IBA Group. The scrapped planes were 6.4 years old on average, and included mostly Airbus but also some Boeing planes. That compares with an average retirement age for narrow-body airplanes of 21.7 years, IBA said.

"Anytime that you end up with any period of market softness in aircraft availability, engine availability, it's going to cause investors to look at other options," said Simon Goodson, CEO of AerFin, a company that buys, sells, repairs and leases aircraft and parts. "We always say to our aircraft and engine traders, 'Please don't fall in love with the metal.'"

Hundreds of aircraft are retired each year as manufacturers ramp up production for airlines, so older planes are swapped out for newer, more fuel-efficient models.

But aside from retiring aging planes, some owners don't want to wait around for replacement parts or repairs on younger jets.

"A year might not sound that long, but does three or four years?" said Dave Querio, CEO of Ascent Aviation Services, a company that repairs, modifies, stores and disassembles aircraft, based in Marana, Arizona, outside of Tucson. "That's the issue for the aircraft that came in in '23, '24 and '25."

In some cases, owners can reap more for the parts than they can selling the plane or paying storage costs and waiting for it to get back in the air again.

Querio said he hasn't seen "the volume of younger aircraft being torn down as I'm seeing today."

A major reason for the young retirements is the long wait and rising prices for jet engines.

One problem is that cutting-edge engines that burn hotter and use less fuel are coming up for maintenance far earlier than airlines expected.

The maintenance requests have kept engine repair and overhaul facilities backed up, from those owned by manufacturing giants GE Aerospace and RTX unit Pratt & Whitney to smaller, independent ones. Airlines and leasing firms have been hunting for spare parts and engines anywhere they can find them, like here in Goodyear, Arizona.

The trend has added more fuel to the already hot secondhand parts market. An engine fan blade could cost $50,000 or even more than $100,000 for bigger jets, Goodson said. Engines that power some Airbus A320neo aircraft have 20 of those blades. A disc for the engine, meanwhile, could cost between $1 million and $2 million.

An engine recall because of a manufacturing defect has backed up repair shops and grounded hundreds of Airbus planes around the world. Spirit Airlines bore the brunt of that problem in the United States, years before the airline collapsed.

The engine is made by Pratt & Whitney, which issued the recall in 2023 after discovering contaminated powdered metal used in making some parts. Those engines are worth close to $17 million this year at the high end, up 60% from the upper end of the range in 2019, according to IBA.

Airlines are removing some engines off retired planes to keep current aircraft flying while they wait for engines to come back from the shop.

"So you're just playing Legos and moving engines on and off aircraft," said Mike Stengel, a partner at AeroDynamic Advisory, an aerospace consulting firm.

The problems are driving up the cost to buy or rent one of the popular engines to record levels.

Renting one is ranging from about $160,000 to $180,000 a month, according to IBA data. A competing engine for the A320neo family made by CFM International, a joint venture of GE Aerospace and France's Safran, is far from a bargain, renting in a similar range of $130,000 to $180,000 a month.

The problems with young retirements predate Spirit's collapse.

The 4-year-old Airbus plane that was torn down outside Phoenix in May is a key example. The aircraft was put in storage because of the engine recall, and it stayed until it was torn apart.

But the company's demise has brought more of its Airbus fleet to the market, forcing owners to figure out if it's worth the wait for engines or other parts.

CNBC visited AerSale's facility in Goodyear on a scorching day in late June, less than two months after Spirit collapsed, where dozens of the idle yellow planes were stored. It was so soon after the airline's demise that a former Detroit-based Spirit captain, Glenn Zecchin, popped in and asked about opportunities to ferry Spirit's aircraft to the desert, and to peek in on his former airline's planes.

"I knew the airplanes were there, and I wanted to see what happened," said Zecchin, who is now flying corporate jets.

A jet parked at the storage facility and maintenance hangar still had Spirit in-flight menus and safety cards in the bungee cord seatbacks. Pilot manuals were still in the cockpit.

Not all of these aircraft will be destroyed. Some companies that owned Spirit's planes before bankruptcy will just sell their parts, but some may keep the planes in storage. Other aircraft may go on to fly for other airlines, with potential carriers ripping out Spirit's tight 28-inch seat pitch for something roomier.

But as long as they're not flying, it means paying storage fees, which vary by aircraft type. Some planes in desert facilities — selected because the arid climate can preserve the aircraft better and avoid corrosion — can sit for decades.

Others won't make it back into the air if there's enough demand for the parts and too long of a wait to get the plane flying again.

"After you get rid of all the valuable equipment and sell it back into the aftermarket, what's really left is just a metal carcass that you will literally take a crane and crush," said AeroDynamic's Stengel. Then the owner can "sell it to recyclers that will make of it what they will, maybe into beer cans or something outside of aerospace."

Ascent's Querio said some owners opt to take that route.

"It's a little melancholy to watch it at times," he said. "But sometimes, it's kind of a thrill to ... see how fast an aircraft can be crunched up and sent off to the smelter."

— CNBC's Erin Black contributed to this report.

#491. The PHP Meltdown Shows How AI Makes Bugs More Exploitable

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Welcome to Kernel Panic, a weekly newsletter by Lily Hay Newman and Matt Burgess from inside the new world of privacy and digital security. To receive this newsletter in your inbox each week, sign up here.

As security teams and web admins scrambled this week to patch newly public PHP bugs, criminal hackers quickly attempted to exploit the flaws in the wild. Millions of exploitation attempts hit within hours of the vulnerabilities being published, according to analysis from Cloudflare—unsurprising given broad access to AI tools that can make it easier for attackers to quickly exploit flaws.

So far, the nightmare scenario of mass victim takeovers and breaches hasn’t come to pass. The PHP Foundation says it hasn’t seen any successful exploitation of the bugs, collectively dubbed PHP2Shell, and many publicly circulating potential exploits appear to be ineffective. However, perhaps the most interesting role of AI amid the chaos this week is how advanced models helped researchers uncover practical exploitations for the vulnerabilities, which were known about, at least in part, for years. In other words, AI helped turn historic vulnerability into a current crisis.

So are we all about to be walloped by a wave of critical vulnerabilities that have been kicking around for decades?

Researchers at security company Calif were the first to identify the PHP2Shell vulnerabilities and create exploits in early August—partially using frontier AI tools as part of OpenAI’s Patch the Planet program, but also using expert human knowledge of PHP, its dynamics, and its weaknesses. Some of the underlying issues were identified in 2024 but were deemed too complex to exploit at the time. Following Calif’s discovery, researchers at OpenAI and the Google-owned security firm Wiz independently discovered PHP2Shell flaws using AI tools.

“The vulnerability had been known to the PHP team for years, but because it could not be leveraged by an attacker—at least, not a human one—it was not addressed,” says Sagi Tzadik, a security researcher at Wiz.

No software is perfect. There are inevitably flaws in even the most critical, foundational digital tools, but the expanding capabilities of AI models have made it faster to analyze and potentially exploit vulnerabilities. This puts even more pressure on under-resourced IT teams, developers, and admins to keep up. Microsoft’s security updates included a record 1,453 security vulnerabilities this month alone. Meanwhile, Bank of America recently hired 1,000 consultants to fix flaws discovered by Anthropic’s Mythos. Security researchers emphasize, though, that AI is ultimately not doing anything new, but rather magnifying existing dynamics of vulnerability discovery and coordinated disclosure.

“We'll definitely see more of these long-life bugs that are in every version of the software for the last decade or whatever,” says Jake Williams, vice president of research and development at Hunter Strategy and a former NSA hacker. “But I think there’s only so much of this low-hanging fruit. There isn’t much else that has the specific reach of PHP. It’s a pretty wild attack surface—most other tools aren’t exposed to the internet by design.”

Researchers note, too, that even with frontier models, most examples of sophisticated AI-aided hacking also involve human expertise. As Ryan Austin, the Calif researcher who first discovered the PHP flaws, wrote this week, “No model is simply capable of unearthing ‘all bugs’ with simple prompts. At least for now.”

In short, security engineer and researcher Niels Provos says, everyone can dial back their freak-outs, at least a bit. “To be honest, I am not really worried,” he says, about AI finding an overwhelming number of historic bugs that are newly exploitable—or new vulnerabilities that couldn’t have been practically exploited without AI. “People have now been looking with AI for over a year, and it required a highly skilled team to find this one. I guess we will see whether I am completely wrong, but I doubt it.”

#4227. Your blood could reveal inflammatory bowel disease 10 years before diagnosis

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified hidden changes in the immune system that can appear up to 10 years before a person is diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The findings, presented at UEG Week 2026, suggest that certain antibody patterns in the blood could eventually help doctors identify people at risk of developing the condition and even predict how severe their disease might become.

Inflammatory bowel disease refers to a group of long-term disorders, including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), that cause persistent inflammation in the digestive tract. These conditions develop when the immune system responds abnormally, contributing to inflammation and damage in the intestines.

Scientists increasingly believe that IBD begins developing years before it is officially diagnosed. However, the immune system changes that take place during this early, largely hidden period have remained poorly understood.

Tracking Immune Changes a Decade Before Diagnosis

To investigate what happens before IBD is diagnosed, researchers examined 2,000 blood serum samples collected from 500 people. The participants included 200 who eventually developed Crohn's disease, 200 who developed ulcerative colitis, and 100 healthy individuals of comparable age and sex.

Blood samples were available from the time of diagnosis and from approximately two, four, and 10 years earlier. This allowed the researchers to look backward through a decade of immune activity and identify changes that emerged before the diseases were recognized.

The team analyzed antibody reactions to approximately 357,000 antigens associated with bacteria, viruses, food, and the immune system itself. Antigens are substances that the immune system can recognize and respond to, while antibodies are proteins produced by the immune system that help identify specific targets.

Across all 2,000 samples, the researchers detected 123,980 unique antibodies.

A striking pattern emerged. People who later developed Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis had significantly less antibody diversity than healthy participants, meaning their immune systems showed a narrower range of antibody responses. Distinctive changes could be detected in both diseases as far back as 10 years before diagnosis.

Different Blood Signals Reveal Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis

The researchers also found that Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis were associated with different immune signatures.

Among people who later developed Crohn's disease, antibody responses to herpesviruses and bacterial flagellin were elevated. Flagellin is a protein that forms part of the tail-like structures some bacteria use to move.

These elevated responses were especially noticeable in individuals who eventually developed complicated Crohn's disease or disease affecting the ileum, the final section of the small intestine.

At the same time, antibody responses to encapsulated bacteria gradually decreased as diagnosis approached. These bacteria are surrounded by protective outer coatings that can help them evade immune defenses.

People who later developed ulcerative colitis showed a somewhat different pattern. Their antibody responses to herpesviruses and autoantigens increased, while responses to encapsulated pathogens declined. Autoantigens are components of the body's own tissues that can mistakenly become targets of an immune response.

Together, these findings suggest that the immune system undergoes measurable changes many years before inflammatory bowel disease is diagnosed.

AI Identifies Hidden Disease Risk Years in Advance

The researchers then used machine learning, a form of artificial intelligence that identifies patterns in complex data, to determine whether these antibody signatures could distinguish people who would eventually develop IBD from healthy individuals.

Using blood antibody patterns from up to 10 years before diagnosis, the models distinguished future Crohn's disease cases from healthy controls with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90. For ulcerative colitis, the corresponding value was 0.84.

AUC is a measure of how well a model distinguishes between two groups. A score of 0.5 indicates performance equivalent to chance, while 1.0 represents perfect discrimination.

The researchers also discovered that antibody patterns could help distinguish people who would develop complicated Crohn's disease from those whose disease would remain less complicated. This distinction was possible approximately 10 years before diagnosis, with an AUC of 0.85.

These findings raise the possibility that blood antibody testing could eventually help identify not only who is at increased risk of IBD, but also which patients may need more intensive treatment.

Commenting on the findings, lead author Dr. Arno Bourgonje said: "It is fascinating to see that complicated CD at diagnosis is associated with a distinct antibody profile years before the first clinical symptoms and is not simply the consequence of disease progression after diagnosis. Finding these patterns so early points to the potential of antibody profiling to support earlier risk detection, distinguish between disease subtypes and help identify patients who may benefit from more intensive therapy as early as possible."

Could a Blood Test Detect Gut Disease 10 Years Early?

Although the results offer compelling evidence that IBD has a detectable early immune signature, the researchers emphasize that the findings are not yet ready for use in routine medical screening.

The study establishes an important proof of principle, but additional research is necessary to determine whether these antibody patterns can reliably predict disease in broader populations.

Researchers must also establish whether antibody testing provides meaningful benefits beyond existing genetic information, clinical assessments, and other blood-based measurements. Any potential screening test would need to be accurate, affordable, reproducible, and ultimately shown to improve patient outcomes.

"The next priority is to validate these signatures in independent, more diverse and prospectively followed populations," concluded Dr. Bourgonje. "We also need to determine their added value alongside genetic, clinical and other blood-based markers, translate the findings into a reproducible and affordable targeted assay and, ultimately, demonstrate that using such a test actually leads to better clinical outcomes."

If future studies confirm these results, blood-based immune profiling could offer doctors a new way to recognize inflammatory bowel disease risk years before diagnosis, distinguish between disease subtypes, and identify patients who might benefit from earlier intervention.

#496. New mRNA Vaccine Reduces Risk of Melanoma Recurrence by Over 40 Percent

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

An experimental vaccine offers new hope to patients with melanoma, one of the most aggressive forms of skin cancer. The pharmaceutical companies Merck and Moderna released the results of a Phase 3 clinical trial that evaluated the efficacy of a personalized mRNA-based vaccine that, when combined with immunotherapy, reduced the risk of disease recurrence or death by 42 percent compared to treatment with the immunotherapy drug alone.

Georgina Long, medical director of the Australian Melanoma Institute and the study’s principal investigator, said in a statement that these results are “transformative” and “have the potential to make a foundational shift in how we treat patients with earlier-stage melanoma.”

The trial, known as INTerpath-001, included 1,137 people with high-risk melanoma who had undergone surgery to completely remove their tumors. The participants were divided into two groups. Those in the first group received only Pembrolizumab, a drug that helps the immune system detect and fight cancer cells, while those in the second group were treated with the same drug in combination with the mRNA vaccine, called Intismeran Autogene.

After monitoring the participants for an average of 24.6 months, the researchers determined that addition of the vaccine reduced the risk of cancer recurrence or death by 42 percent compared to Pembrolizumab alone.

The results also showed that the experimental treatment reduced the risk of the disease spreading to other parts of the body by 38 percent. At the end of the follow-up period, 82.2 percent of patients who received both treatments remained recurrence-free, compared with 70 percent of those who received Pembrolizumab alone.

“The results of the INTerpath-001 study represent a major breakthrough in the treatment of melanoma and, in particular, in the development of personalized medicine,” Eva Muñoz Couselo—who leads a melanoma research group at the Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology, in Barcelona and who was not involved in the study—told SMC Spain. “For the first time, a Phase III trial has demonstrated that an individualized therapeutic vaccine, designed on the basis of the molecular characteristics of each patient’s tumor, can significantly improve the outcomes of conventional immunotherapy.”

The laboratories explained that the vaccine was developed from a tumor sample taken from each patient, which was used to identify specific mutations that would create distinct proteins the immune system could learn to recognize in order to attack cancer cells. The scientists selected up to three of these molecular signatures per patient.

During the trial, some participants experienced mild side effects such as fever, fatigue, and pain at the injection site. These side effects were seen in earlier trials as well, and the Phase 3 findings demonstrate similar safety when the vaccine is used in the combination with Pembrolizumab.

“These results reinforce our conviction that mRNA has the potential to fundamentally change how we treat cancer,” Stéphane Bancel, CEO of Moderna, said in a statement.

The pharmaceutical companies emphasized that the vaccine is not designed to prevent melanoma but rather to reduce the risk of recurrence and death following prior medical treatment. They also noted that the therapy remains under investigation to validate its efficacy and obtain approval from health authorities before widespread use.

Most people diagnosed with melanoma have a high chance of survival, especially if detected early, but as one of the most common cancers, melanoma kills over 50,000 people globally each year. In theory, similar vaccines could be developed for other kinds of tumors in the future. Earlier this year, another trial demonstrated that personalized mRNA vaccines can greatly improve survival among patients with pancreatic cancer, one of the deadliest kinds of tumor.

“This is the major breakthrough, because conceptually it would make it possible to treat not only melanoma but also other tumors in which the immune system can be reactivated,” Marisol Soengas—a leading melanoma researcher at Spain’s National Cancer Research Center also not involved in the trial—told SMC Spain.

That doesn’t mean a cure for cancer is right around the corner. “The challenge is to ensure that this personalization can be scaled up to a large number of patients and that the cost is affordable for health care systems,” Soengas said. “It is logistically complex. It is literally a bespoke vaccine.”
