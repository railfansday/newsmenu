#5634. A Less-Is-More Strategy for America

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

BARRY R. POSEN is Ford International Professor of Political Science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

The Folly of Expanding the Exhausted U.S. Military

On October 8, the USS Abraham Lincoln and its accompanying strike group returned to the United States after 320 days at sea. The deployment—a near record for the modern American military—had become controversial. As the group’s ships went month after month without a port call, conditions deteriorated precipitously. Sailors reported supply shortages, plumbing issues, and water contamination. Morale dropped markedly; eight sailors even tried to commit suicide. By the time the ships finally left the Middle East, their struggles had made headlines across the country.

The travails of the Lincoln are hardly the only story about the U.S. military to generate alarm since the war in Iran began last February. Military analysts have expressed concern about other carrier groups’ lengthy deployments. They have fretted about the Trump administration’s decision to pull soldiers and weapons stationed in the Indo-Pacific out of the region and position them in the Middle East. Perhaps most of all, they have worried as the United States drains its stockpile of sophisticated munitions.

These problems all share the same root cause: the U.S. military is not built for the near-constant use it has experienced for decades. Ever since the Cold War, American defense planners have structured the U.S. armed forces to be ready to fight two major conflicts simultaneously. In practice, that means Washington has constructed a force that is designed to win short, sharp wars. For that reason, the military does not have enough units, weapons, or personnel to handle never-ending conflicts and simultaneously use its forces around the world to coerce enemies and reassure partners.

American officials are now trying to modestly increase the size of the military. More important, they are hoping that huge infusions of money will increase the capability of the existing armed forces. But making these changes will be exceptionally challenging—and they are unnecessary. The United States is a highly secure country with many capable and wealthy partners. It does not need a defense apparatus capable of fighting everywhere all at once. Instead, it would be much better off focusing its attention on a smaller set of issues and scaling down accordingly.

The U.S. nonnuclear forces, which consume more than 80 percent of the defense budget, are structured and sized for particular contingencies—currently, a clash with China, Russia, or both nearly simultaneously. The military’s weapons are therefore designed for maximum lethality and survivability in what military planners expect to be an unbelievably dangerous environment. To that end, the Pentagon has emphasized purchasing precise, long-range, and stealthy weapons. These are supposed to kick open the doors of enemy defenses and allow other expensive advanced platforms, such as F-35 fighter jets or B-2 or B-21 bombers, to deliver large quantities of slightly less sophisticated but still quite lethal precision weapons.

Washington has never expected these wars to last long. Instead, it imagines that under the fierce pressure such conflicts would generate, the adversary will cry uncle or the war will escalate to nuclear threats or—worse yet—nuclear use. During the Cold War, for example, the United States and its allies planned for an extremely intense, roughly 30-day conflict against the Soviet Union. Today’s military planners have probably made similar assumptions about a war with Beijing or Moscow. Highly capable conventional forces and the risk of nuclear escalation, American thinking goes, should deter an attack. If deterrence fails, U.S. planners hope to stop an enemy in its tracks and create escalation risks so great that the adversary will realize its errors and try to reverse course.

But such thinking means that Washington does not have enough resources to sustain long, great-power conflicts. At $100 million each, the F-35 fighter aircraft, a mainstay of the air force, navy, and Marine Corps, is simply not designed for mass production. Neither are the long-range stand-off missiles that would be the preferred munitions in the early days of such a war. The U.S. military has developed sophisticated theater ballistic missile defenses, but their main purpose is to defend U.S. forces and essential military infrastructure—not towns, cities, and civilian industries. They can be used for this purpose under certain conditions, but doing so quickly exhausts the supply of missiles. In a war with China or Russia, civilians in local, allied countries will need to look after themselves.

These problems have become readily apparent in Washington’s war against Iran. The United States likely thought it could rapidly overpower the Islamic Republic—at best a middle power, albeit one with great ambitions. After all, under the right circumstances, the U.S. military handily overmatches these kinds of enemies, as it did Iraq in the 1990–91 Gulf War. But Washington’s enemies have smartened up since then. They no longer simply produce smaller and less capable versions of the great-power militaries that American forces are designed to deter and combat. Instead, they have prepared themselves to take a beating and keep on fighting, even if at modest levels of effectiveness.

Overly ambitious war plans and faulty policies are not the only reason the U.S. military is struggling. Politics is also creating difficulties. Because the American people hesitate to support wars abroad, U.S. politicians will do anything to avoid casualties. That means they take all the high-end weaponry that the military stockpiles to break down great-power defenses and instead spend it profligately on regional conflicts. Similarly, they deploy scarce air defense weapons to protect allies’ towns and cities, lest those allies consider finding new partners or decide to settle a war by compromising with their enemies.

U.S. policymakers also generally overuse the military. Politicians like to accompany diplomatic messaging with action, in part because they do not believe in diplomacy anymore, which pushes them to parade or use the armed forces far more often than is warranted. When asked to act, commanders usually salute and say yes. Others simply cannot sit still. Commanders, after all, took on their roles because they wanted to command something.

The result is a worn-down military. The defense press is full of stories about ships and aircraft that are not up to standard because they have been out too long, resulting in deferred maintenance. This, in turn, creates repair problems for other units. When a ship must stay in dry dock longer than usual to receive adequate repairs, other ships must wait longer for servicing—which means their needs also grow. Ultimately, the navy has fewer fully ready combat ships available in the event of a great-power challenge. The army has similar problems, with combat and support vehicles arriving at maintenance depots in unexpectedly poor condition. In the air force, the F-35’s “full mission capable rate,” which was never great, has deteriorated from 38 percent to 25 percent since 2021. And the overall readiness of air force aircraft has declined in recent years, in part simply because of overwork.

All the while, U.S. soldiers, sailors, pilots, and marines are constantly on guard, coercing and deterring U.S. enemies and reassuring allies. This, too, creates high levels of activity that can negatively affect retention. Although the military has generally been successful at retaining senior enlisted personnel in recent years, it may soon run into difficulty. Since 2024, navy warships have repeatedly gone to sea undercrewed. The ultimate result of this overuse is that the sharp edge of the military is dulled, and the steel blade is weakened.

U.S. defense officials are aware that they have a problem. Currently, they are trying to solve it by building a somewhat bigger and far more technologically capable military. Congress, for example, appears poised to increase the Pentagon’s so-called base budget—which encompasses what the Congressional Budget Office defines as the “enduring costs of manning, training, and equipping the military”—by over 25 percent, or to $1.1 trillion for the 2027 fiscal year. The Pentagon hopes to secure an additional $350 billion over the base budget in a subsequent bill. Defense officials also want to add over 40,000 new service members. But this is, at best, a personnel increase of three percent, which is much too small to fill the additional units needed to solve extant readiness problems. And the fancier weapons Congress hopes defense officials will buy cannot overcome the strain of constant deployments and repeated wars. The defense industrial base is barely keeping up with current demands, and recruiting large numbers of new service members would likely require offering much higher wages, which would then need to be extended to everyone else. Personnel costs would start consuming an ever-greater share of the defense budget. U.S. officials could instead opt for conscription, but that would produce a backlash.

There is, however, an alternative: scaling down. Instead of pretending that it must be able to fight two great-power wars at once, Washington should cut its policy coat to fit the available cloth. It should continue to build forces for major contingencies, such as deterring adventurism by China. But policymakers should agree to slow down other projects. Congress should task the Pentagon with reviewing the armed forces and come up with an activity budget, which would be based on an assessment of how much real military activity—flying hours, ship days at sea, training days in the field—can be undertaken without harming the readiness of the total force. It would then allocate that activity appropriately. Based on what they ought to know about the virtual attrition the military has suffered thanks to 30 years of hyperactivity, commanders should determine what level of exercises, forward deployments, and regional wars the military can actually support without doing damage to the capabilities it wishes to have ready every day to deter a great-power war and, if need be, wage one effectively. The U.S. Navy already knows the activity level that its fleet can support, which it has detailed in its Optimized Fleet Response Plan. But American presidents and defense secretaries have simply ignored this plan whenever they wanted to deploy more forces.

The activity budget should not be classified but instead should be public information, listed along with the dollar budget that the Pentagon publishes annually, voluminous summaries of which can be found at the Defense Department’s comptroller’s website. When politicians want to exceed the activity levels on which they have previously agreed, they should have to explain why and how they plan to repair the force after intense use. Commanders should think of this as an ethical issue. Every now and then, one should resign when overuse causes serious problems.

To help lighten its load, Washington will need to hand off some responsibilities to others. This should be feasible despite the need to manage Moscow’s aggressive impulses, which are the presumed cause of one of the contingencies—a war with Russia—that drives U.S. defense planning. The principal European states have more than enough economic and technological resources to deal with Russia on their own, if they so choose. The transatlantic bargain should thus be more forthright about the limited liability that the United States is prepared to assume for the continent’s defense.

The National Defense Strategy of the Trump administration is honest about this fact, but U.S. military activity levels and deployments in Europe suggest that the United States has not truly reduced its role in Europe’s defense. The supreme allied commander of NATO is still an American, when the position should be filled by a European. Tens of thousands of U.S. troops are still based all over Europe; many should come home. Pentagon websites and those of American commands in Europe feature laundry lists of exercises and maneuvers across the continent that involve not only forward-based U.S. forces but also rotating deployments of units based in the United States. These numbers, too, should diminish.

American officials may want to be able to solve most of their global security challenges by simply buying more forces. But a much better solution is for the United States to change its grand strategy and act with greater restraint. Washington simply does not have the will or the resources to provide for both Asia’s and Europe’s defense while fighting protracted conflicts elsewhere, most recently in the Middle East. Nor, for that matter, does it need to. The United States is inherently a very secure country. It is rich, endowed with bountiful natural resources, insulated by vast oceans, and blessed with relatively weak and pliant neighbors. Only huge changes in the political constellation of power in Asia, Europe, or the greater Middle East could seriously affect U.S. security.

Washington should buy sufficient military power and stock adequate reserves of raw materials to ensure against such changes. But in general, it can afford to be less proactive. A change in grand strategy might even allow the United States to cut the defense budget. If concerns about the ballooning U.S. deficits are correct, then the Pentagon will need to help pay the bills to set things right.

#5630. Brazil’s Economic Impasse

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

MONICA DE BOLLE is Senior Fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, an Adjunct Professor at Georgetown University, and a co-author of The New Economic Nationalism.

Neither Lula nor Bolsonaro Can Solve the Country’s Problems

On October 5, the day after the first round of Brazil’s general elections, the country’s financial markets were euphoric. Brazil is struggling with high debt and low growth, and investors are eager for leaders to reduce budget deficits, deregulate, cut taxes, and perhaps even privatize state assets. After nearly four years under the left-wing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, known as Lula, who has been slow to implement such measures, investors hope a right-wing government will create opportunities for bold reform.

Rule by the right might be exactly what Brazil gets. The far-right Liberal Party made substantial gains in the legislature, becoming the single largest party in both the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies. Its presidential candidate, Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of the notorious former President Jair Bolsonaro, led the aging incumbent, Lula, by nearly two percentage points in the first round of voting. Lula has shown himself capable of impressive political comebacks, but Flávio Bolsonaro is still the favorite in the runoff vote scheduled for October 25.

Even if Bolsonaro wins, however, the prospects for the Brazilian economy are dim. The policies he has put forward are nothing close to the substantial fiscal consolidation program that will be necessary to bring down debt and stimulate private investment, and his determination to weaken Brazil’s political institutions creates the kind of uncertainty that scares investors away. Economic growth has been sluggish under Lula, whose administration has struggled to relieve the high debt burdens afflicting the public sector and households. What would truly doom the country to economic crisis, however, is the kind of authoritarian-inspired politics that a Bolsonaro government could usher in.

Brazil is in serious economic trouble. The country’s sovereign debt ratio has ballooned to 83 percent of GDP, fiscal deficits leave no room for additional government spending, and high interest rates are hurting consumers and investors. Meanwhile, the Iran war has raised the price of fuel and other goods, weighing on household balance sheets already strained by high debt and leaving many Brazilians desperate for leaders who can alleviate their daily hardships.

This predicament is years in the making. Before Jair Bolsonaro was elected in 2018, his predecessor, President Michel Temer, enacted a cap on public spending to contain Brazil’s runaway fiscal problems. Temer introduced the cap as a constitutional amendment to prevent future governments from changing the spending ceiling to suit their needs. Yet Jair Bolsonaro raised the cap several times anyway, which required convincing Congress to amend the constitution. In return, the executive branch effectively ceded control of the budgetary process to the legislature.

Lula therefore inherited a system in which he had little room for fiscal adjustment, and he made few attempts to fix the problem during his term. The result was high budget deficits and persistent inflation, which forced the central bank to raise interest rates substantially. In turn, high interest rates damaged household balance sheets and lowered overall investment and growth.

If Lula were reelected, he would find it incredibly difficult to introduce new fiscal reforms and other measures to support growth. He promised to reduce spending and has proposed a comprehensive overhaul of Brazil’s tax system, which would help increase the budget surplus and reduce public debt. Yet Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party and its allies now hold a majority of seats in the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies, allowing them to block any legislation Lula might pursue.

Yet it is overly optimistic, if not completely unrealistic, to expect that Bolsonaro would fare better. Neither candidate has provided much detail during the campaign about what he would do to fix the economy. Bolsonaro has instead spoken often about “institutional reforms,” which he has said would include amending Brazil’s 38-year-old constitution to make existing labor and environmental regulations more flexible, among other changes; meddling with the Supreme Court; and granting amnesty to his father, who is serving a prison sentence for leading a 2023 coup attempt. Those steps are likely to drive away investors because stable institutions are necessary for an economy to function well. A government that undermines the judicial system and the constitution will compromise the integrity of its economic policies, no matter what those policies are or how good the reputations of the officials making them may be.

Gathering support for such an overhaul of the country’s political institutions, moreover, would require compromises that could worsen Brazil’s economic troubles. To secure enough votes in Congress to advance Bolsonaro’s priorities—for example, two-thirds of the Senate would need to approve the removal of a Supreme Court justice, and three-fifths of both houses must approve a constitutional amendment—the government would have to bring legislators outside its coalition on board. Those politicians would almost certainly demand substantial funds for their constituencies in exchange for their support, adding to the budget deficit.

Bolsonaro’s far-right movement will also be focused on holding together its recently formed coalition of governors, mayors, and voters. Much of its constituency is lower-middle-class Brazilians who rose out of poverty during the boom years two decades ago, while Lula was serving his first two terms in office. After seeing their income growth stagnate in the years since, these voters have turned to the Liberal Party, but their loyalty is new. Most are self-employed and face economic precarity. Both Bolsonaro and Lula are now trying to appeal to these voters by promising initiatives that boost income and offer household debt relief, but delivering such programs—if it is even possible—would only exacerbate Brazil’s economic woes.

What Bolsonaro has shared about his economic policies does not inspire much confidence, either. His party has said it would reduce government spending by $40 billion, or 1.5 percent of GDP, and deliver primary budget surpluses—a measure of the budget balance that does not include interest payments. But it has not explained how it would accomplish this, nor has Bolsonaro elaborated on his vague plans for controlling the rise in public debt. Ultimately, it is unlikely that Bolsonaro can deliver on any of these promises, especially if he keeps his pledge not to cut pensions and key social programs.

Part of the problem is that the Liberal Party’s economic program lacks a viable plan for reducing costs. It proposes eliminating at least ten government ministries but has no estimate of the resulting cost savings. When Jair Bolsonaro took similar steps after he assumed office in 2019, removing cabinet positions and laying off public servants, those moves had little effect on fiscal outcomes. Other promised measures include reducing Brazil’s value-added tax, phasing out a tax on financial transactions, lowering taxes on electricity and fuel, spending $180 billion on infrastructure over four years, and building 2.5 million new homes. None of them would fix Brazil’s fiscal problems; they would only make those problems worse.

The same goes for the rest of Flávio Bolsonaro’s tax plan. His economic advisers have said they intend to reverse every tax created or raised under Lula’s finance minister, Fernando Haddad. Haddad’s tax increases were intended to shift the tax burden to high-income brackets, raise government revenue, and reduce inequalities in the tax system. These taxes have contributed to a modest increase in revenue but fell short of delivering budgetary surpluses. Bolsonaro has argued that the measures are impeding investment because they tax higher income brackets more heavily, but there is no evidence to support this idea. Instead, repealing all these tax increases would worsen Brazil’s fiscal situation. A simple calculation shows that Brazil needs to increase its primary budget balance by about six percent of GDP, either through increased revenue or decreased spending (or both), to keep its debt from rising further. That adjustment would have to be spread over at least three years, giving indebted households and firms time to change their consumption and investment patterns, to avoid a recession. Slashing taxes, however, would reduce government revenues, raising the debt.

Brazil’s high interest rates are another constraint. The central bank’s current rate is 13.75 percent, astronomical by Latin American standards, dictating interest rates everywhere in the economy. Public debt service payments alone consume some 8.7 percent of GDP. Bolsonaro would not be able to lower interest rates significantly without sparking a recession, so he would face the same problem that has plagued Lula during his latest term—few good options for containing a rapidly growing debt.

Neither Lula nor Bolsonaro is well positioned to solve Brazil’s economic problems. A decisive turn toward illiberalism under Bolsonaro, however, would all but assure economic disaster.

Flávio Bolsonaro has never disavowed his father’s championing of Brazil’s dictatorship, which lasted from 1964 to 1985, and his Liberal Party traces its lineage to the party that sustained the military regime. Moreover, he has pledged to release his father and his father’s associates who are in prison for staging an insurrection and a coup attempt in 2023. Flávio’s brother Eduardo Bolsonaro, a former member of Brazil’s Congress who would likely play a significant role in his brother’s government, has openly expressed his admiration for Viktor Orban’s illiberal regime in Hungary, claiming a desire to learn the “best practices” of a movement that rewrote Hungary’s constitution, reshaped its courts, silenced its press, and deployed other overtly authoritarian tactics. And the substantial far-right bloc in Brazil’s Congress supports policies to undermine institutions, including by impeaching members of the Supreme Court—a step these parties advocated even before corruption allegations emerged against several of the court’s justices. (Flávio Bolsonaro is also under investigation for his involvement in the same corruption scandal.)

Although Brazil is not at risk of returning to military rule, the former authoritarian regime serves as a warning of what could happen if a Bolsonaro administration follows its illiberal inclinations to their logical conclusion. During those two decades of military dictatorship, Brazil spent at unsustainable levels, mostly funding state enterprises, to preserve the regime’s hold on power. The country defaulted on its public debt, endured corrosive hyperinflation, and saw income inequality skyrocket. Six economic stabilization plans failed and growth stagnated. Brazil is not alone in this experience. No Latin American country that swung from democracy to authoritarian rule in the past century has benefited economically from the shift. Countries elsewhere in the world that prospered after such a transition, including some in East Asia, became less authoritarian during their economic ascent.

Brazil’s rightward shift fits a regional pattern. The common thread that unites Brazil with Argentina, Chile, and Colombia is the crude libertarian worldview that each of their far-right movements espouse. All of them talk about citizens becoming entrepreneurs free of the state. They tell voters who have unstable employment and little economic security that their policies will bring prosperity. But their populism rarely wins the confidence of investors. Like its peers, when the Brazilian far right says it can overcome the country’s substantial problems with institutional demolition and a threadbare economic agenda, what it really offers is empty promises.

#1254. First trophy, old tradition: St. Louis City SC brings U.S. Open Cup back to soccer's oldest American hotbed

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It took over two hours of play and a penalty shootout for St. Louis City SC to win their first trophy in club history, defeating the Columbus Crew 5-3 on Wednesday in the Lamar Hunt U.S. Open Cup final. For the first time in 11 years, an Open Cup final was decided via a penalty shootout, and it came after St. Louis' starting keeper, Roman Burki, had to be taken to the hospital after being kneed in the head in the 78th minute of play.

Benjamin Lundt came in for Burki, making his fourth appearance of the season and his second in relief of Burki in an Open Cup penalty shootout. He only saved the first penalty by Santi Rodriguez, but that would be more than enough for St. Louis to get the job done. Their takers were clinical under pressure, as midfielder Daniel Edelman went down the middle to seal the win after playing all 120-plus minutes.

"It's just the next guy up. Roman was a hero. He had another save in the first half, and he's throwing his body on the line there. He's done it in Europe, and he's playing here now in MLS, and he's still doing what he did in Europe," Edelman said after the game to CBS Sports, praising his keepers following the match. "It's phenomenal for him and Ben. He started it for us in the Open Cup and had a big penalty save against Houston, and he did it here again today."

During the trophy lift, the team had Berki's jersey on display since he was at the hospital to be checked out. He was conscious and able to move while leaving the stadium, but it's never a good moment when a player has to be stretchered off, especially in a final, but his team managed to pull it off for him, adding to the Gateway to the West's rich history.

The city of St. Louis is no stranger to winning this competition, so it's only fitting that the first trophy in the history of the MLS club, which began play in 2023, is the U.S. Open Cup. Between 1920 and 1988, the city of St. Louis won the Open Cup 10 times, with the Busch Seniors being the most recent to win in 1988 before St. Louis City SC ended the drought. Edelman summed up what it meant to everyone.

"It's the first trophy for me and for a lot of guys in this city because you look up there and you see that [the support from away fans] and they completely deserve to have some silverware and for us as a team, what we've been through this year, it's phenomenal for us to have this night and have this trophy," he said.

Speaking to members of that '88 team, there were times that they didn't think that St. Louis would see another U.S. Open Cup title, but under Yoann Damet, this team has come together in a big way, and being able to overcome injuries to secure the Open Cup title with their backup keeper is another way that they've showcased their resilience. Heading into the weekend, St. Louis had a 21-game unbeaten run snapped, and there was no issue being able to regain focus in this critical moment.

Columbus dominated the chances for much of the game, taking 16 shots, but only three tested the St. Louis keepers as their defense bent but didn't break. This is preparation at its finest, but this team also isn't done. Damet wants to make sure to enjoy this trophy, but doing the double is the next goal as they've risen to second in the Western Conference. This is a team that will want to win the MLS Cup and secure the league title, and Damet made sure to acknowledge that.

"You always want to fill the trophy cabinet, and it takes time. Success is not built overnight, so we started the team thinking, let's implement a clear vision and a clear identity of how we want to do things, and see how far we can go in year one," Damet told CBS Sports. "This is still phase one of what we're gonna be, so I'm very happy with what we've been able to do. Our consistency in the league and winning a trophy is an amazing testament to the work of the guys. At the same time, it means so much for the city and for the community, and I'm so happy."

If this is only phase one with a 36-year-old coach at the helm who is also being backed by the front office, MLS may be hearing more from this club before long.

#5232. Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.

#5244. A simple 15-minute treatment could help patients avoid carpal tunnel surgery

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

A simple procedure that takes about 15 minutes could offer lasting relief to people with carpal tunnel syndrome, a painful condition that can cause numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hands. A new study suggests that the treatment, which uses ultrasound to guide an injection of ordinary saline around a compressed nerve, provides longer-lasting benefits than traditional steroid injections, without the risks associated with steroids.

The findings, published in Radiology, a journal of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), point to a promising alternative for patients whose symptoms have not improved with conventional treatments. Researchers say the minimally invasive technique could also help some patients delay or avoid surgery.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

The study, "Efficacy of US-guided Hydrodissection for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial," was conducted by Anupama Tandon, M.D., Anindita Bose, M.D., Gopesh Mehrotra, M.D., Siddharth Maheshwari, M.D., and Ayush Khandelwal, M.D.

#809. People are taking an unapproved weight-loss drug. AI found something surprising

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An experimental weight-loss drug that has not yet received regulatory approval is already attracting users willing to obtain unapproved versions online. Now, scientists are using artificial intelligence to investigate their experiences, uncovering unexpected symptom reports that differ from those documented in clinical trials.

The drug, retatrutide, belongs to a new generation of GLP-1 medications designed to help regulate appetite and body weight. Although it remains under investigation and is not approved for use, people are purchasing unapproved formulations that are not intended for human consumption and discussing their experiences on internet forums.

Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania analyzed nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments from approximately 40,000 users to investigate these accounts. Their study, published in JAMA Network Open, provides the first systematic examination of potential side effects reported by people claiming to use unapproved retatrutide.

One finding stood out. Among users whose reported symptoms could be classified, increased appetite was the most common complaint. That was particularly unexpected because retatrutide is designed to suppress hunger, and its Phase 2 clinical trials primarily identified digestive problems as the most frequent side effects.

AI Uncovers Unexpected Weight-Loss Drug Reports

"Our goal is to identify hypotheses in online conversations that we could test. AI helps us find recurring patterns in people's self-reports, including experiences that may be missed by conventional drug-safety monitoring," says Sharath Chandra Guntuku, Research Associate Professor in Computer and Information Science (CIS), Director of Penn's Computational Social Listening Lab and senior author of the study.

However, the researchers emphasize that the findings should not be interpreted as proof of previously unknown side effects. Reddit users represent a very different population from clinical trial participants, and their accounts cannot establish whether the drug caused the symptoms they described.

There is also considerable uncertainty about what these individuals are actually taking. Products sold outside regulated medical channels may differ from the genuine experimental medication in their ingredients, purity, and dosage.

"We can't say that retatrutide is causing these symptoms, or even that Reddit users are actually taking genuine retatrutide, since the drug is not legally available, and gray market formulations vary," says Lyle Ungar, Professor in CIS and a co-author of the study.

Rather than confirming new risks, the research offers scientists an opportunity to identify questions that warrant more rigorous investigation.

"This is the first glimpse into the experiences of a population that is otherwise outside conventional drug safety monitoring because they are taking unapproved versions of a drug that has yet to finish clinical trials," says Neil Sehgal, the study's first author and a doctoral student in CIS.

How Researchers Analyzed 150,000 Reddit Posts

The project builds on previous research by the University of Pennsylvania team. In that earlier investigation, scientists used large language models (LLMs), the type of artificial intelligence that powers many modern chatbots, to examine more than 400,000 Reddit posts discussing semaglutide and tirzepatide.

That analysis revealed reports of possible side effects, including menstrual irregularities, that may not have been fully documented in clinical trials or official drug safety records.

"We wanted to apply the same methodology to gray-market retatrutide," says Sehgal.

Retatrutide is particularly interesting because it works differently from existing GLP-1 medications. Semaglutide acts on one hormone receptor involved in blood sugar control and appetite regulation, while tirzepatide targets two. Retatrutide activates three receptors, potentially producing a different combination of effects throughout the body.

To investigate what people were experiencing, the researchers used AI to examine nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments. They identified approximately 14,000 users who claimed to have personally taken an unapproved version of retatrutide.

The AI systems then extracted descriptions of symptoms that users associated with the medication and translated those descriptions into standardized medical terminology. This allowed researchers to compare reports even when different people used different words to describe similar experiences.

After excluding expected effects such as weight loss and reduced appetite, the team identified 7,823 users who reported at least one symptom that could be medically classified.

"A researcher might be able to examine a few thousand Reddit posts by hand, but looking at nearly 150,000 would be an enormous undertaking," says Sehgal. "AI makes it possible to analyze these conversations at scale, identifying patterns in what people are sharing that would otherwise be difficult to recognize."

The most striking difference involved hunger.

In retatrutide's Phase 2 clinical trials (the drug is currently in Phase 3 trials, the final step before FDA approval), participants most commonly experienced gastrointestinal complaints, including nausea. These effects are familiar concerns with medications that influence appetite and digestion.

But among Reddit users who reported taking unapproved retatrutide, the pattern looked different. Increased appetite emerged as the most frequently described classifiable symptom, despite the substantial weight-loss effects demonstrated in clinical testing.

The researchers also compared the new findings with their earlier analysis of semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Compared with those earlier Reddit reports, discussions involving unapproved retatrutide contained proportionally more mentions of appetite-related changes, sleep disturbances, increased heart rate, and muscle-related symptoms. Reports of nausea and other digestive complaints were proportionally less common.

These differences could reflect how retatrutide interacts with the body. However, they could also result from differences between the groups of people discussing the medications, their other drug use, or the contents of the unapproved products.

"Retatrutide works differently from existing GLP-1 drugs, so it's possible that its effects on the body are different, too," says Jena Shaw Tronieri, Senior Research Investigator at Penn's Center for Weight and Eating Disorders and a co-author of the study.

"But the people discussing it on Reddit also may be different," she notes. "Users willing to obtain an unapproved medication on the gray market likely differ from other clinical populations. Some may be taking multiple experimental peptides, and we can't verify exactly what they're taking. We need more research to understand what, if anything, these differences in self-reported symptoms mean."

Can AI Detect Drug Safety Concerns Earlier?

Beyond the findings about retatrutide, the study demonstrates how AI could potentially improve the early detection of drug safety concerns.

Traditional monitoring systems depend heavily on clinical trials, reports submitted to regulators, and information collected by healthcare professionals. People obtaining experimental medications through unapproved channels may not be represented in those systems.

Social media offers another source of information, allowing researchers to identify recurring experiences that might otherwise escape attention. However, these reports must be independently investigated before they can be considered reliable evidence of a drug-related problem.

The Penn team has already seen an example of how this approach can help guide further research.

In their previous investigation of semaglutide and tirzepatide, researchers identified online reports of menstrual irregularities. Those findings subsequently encouraged another scientific team to examine similar symptoms using the FDA's database of reported adverse drug events.

The follow-up study, published in Obstetrics & Gynecology, also detected disproportionate reports of certain menstrual changes. Although those findings did not establish causation, they provided additional reasons to investigate the potential connection.

The researchers hope their retatrutide findings will lead to similar independent studies, particularly as the drug advances through clinical development.

"That's what we hope will happen with this study," says Sehgal. "Other researchers can take what we find on Reddit and look for the same patterns in other datasets, using entirely different methods. Whether those signals hold up or not, the goal is to build a better understanding of what people are actually experiencing."

#850. A rock plunged 90 kilometers into Earth and revealed something unexpected

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A remarkable rock discovered in Papua New Guinea has revealed an unexpected temperature pattern deep beneath Earth's surface, challenging scientists' understanding of how tectonic plates move into the planet's interior.

An international research team led by Göttingen University found that the rock traveled more than 90 kilometers (56 miles) underground about four million years ago. Along the way, it experienced unusually high temperatures at shallower depths before entering conditions associated with a relatively cooler type of subduction.

The discovery raises new questions about how heat is distributed inside Earth and how tectonic plates behave as they descend. The findings were published in Nature Geoscience.

A Strange Discovery Deep Beneath Earth's Surface

Earth's outer shell is divided into enormous tectonic plates that slowly move across the planet. When two plates converge, one can be forced beneath the other and sink into Earth's interior. This process is called "subduction."

These regions play a major role in shaping our planet. They are associated with powerful earthquakes and volcanic activity, but they also help regulate Earth's climate over millions of years by recycling materials such as carbon dioxide through the planet's interior.

The immense pressure found in subduction zones can transform rocks into a distinctive red and green material known as "eclogite." Researchers examined one of these rocks from Papua New Guinea using several advanced analytical techniques.

Inside the rock, they discovered tiny crystals of coesite, a mineral that can form only under extremely high pressure. These crystals had become trapped within another mineral as the rock formed, preserving valuable evidence of the conditions it experienced deep underground.

By studying these microscopic clues, the scientists determined that the rock had descended at least 90 kilometers beneath Earth's surface, where temperatures reached approximately 800°C (1,472°F).

Even more surprising was what they uncovered about its earlier journey.

An Unexpected Shift From Hot to Cold Subduction

The researchers reconstructed the rock's temperature history and found that at a depth of approximately 45 kilometers (28 miles), it had already reached temperatures of around 700°C (1,292°F).

That meant the rock was only about 100°C cooler at half its maximum depth, even though it still had another 45 kilometers to descend.

This was unexpected because existing models predict a different distribution of heat within subduction zones. The rock appears to have experienced unusually hot conditions at shallower depths before entering an environment with a relatively cooler temperature pattern farther down. Although its actual temperature increased with depth, the rate of warming was much smaller than scientists would normally expect.

Dr. Jan Schönig at Göttingen University's Geoscience Center explains: "Finding this change from hot to cold subduction was unexpected as this contradicts our current thermal models of subduction zones."

Scientists have previously discovered rocks containing evidence of unexpectedly high temperatures. However, this is the first time researchers have been able to reconstruct such a clear transition between different thermal conditions as a rock descended from shallower to much greater depths.

What Could Explain Earth's Hidden Heat Mystery?

The researchers have identified several possible explanations for the unusual temperature pattern.

One possibility is that tectonic plates generate substantially more heat at relatively shallow depths than scientists previously believed. As the plates move against each other, intense shearing forces could produce additional heating along their boundaries.

Another explanation is that the subduction zone had not yet existed long enough to develop the cooler temperatures predicted by conventional models.

Both processes may also have contributed to the rock's unusual thermal history.

Understanding these temperature differences is important because subduction zones influence some of Earth's most powerful geological processes. More accurate models could help scientists better evaluate the conditions associated with earthquakes and volcanic activity.

The findings could also improve our understanding of major geochemical cycles, particularly the long-term carbon cycle, which helps regulate Earth's climate by moving carbon between the surface and the planet's interior.

By revealing how conditions change as tectonic plates descend, the discovery offers scientists a new way to investigate how subduction has operated over millions to billions of years and how these processes continue shaping Earth today.

Like decades’ worth of high-winding Ferrari V-8s and the LT6 in the range-topping C8 Corvettes, Mercedes’s M177 Evo uses a flat-plane crank.

When they massaged the M177 V-8 for the last-generation AMG GT Black Series, Mercedes engineers were surprised to find it developed more torque more quickly with a flat-plane crankshaft than the cross-plane piece it was originally designed with. Not only that, but alternating power strokes evenly between the cylinder banks—rather than the necessary sequencing of adjacent cylinders in a cross-plane V-8—improved combustion efficiency and emissions. With more stringent tailpipe regulations coming into effect for Europe and China in 2027, the engineering team took note.

Flat-plane cranks are notorious for two things: shrieking exhaust notes and intense vibration. Neither would do in a luxury SUV. (It’s not just the AMG that gets the new V-8; a detuned version of the M177 Evo also powers the statelier GLS 580.) Quelling the vibrations necessitated the new engine block. A pair of balance shafts nestled low alongside the crank cancel out indecent tremors.

Balance shafts are far from novel. Mercedes’s solution for preserving the deep exhaust rumble its V-8s are known for, however, is rather innovative—innovative, but elegantly simple and refreshingly analog. Rather than cherry-picking frequencies to amplify and suppress or just playing the sound they want through the standard Burmester sound system’s 15 speakers, they turned metal. The lobes on the exhaust cams are not oriented identically for every cylinder. On the left bank, the front two cylinders open their exhaust valves 15 degrees of crank rotation earlier than the back two cylinders. On the right bank, the difference is 20 degrees of crank rotation over the front two cylinders. Powertrain development engineer Sebastian Schlegel says, “Maybe we lose half a horsepower, but that does not matter.”

This development was followed by a sort of Great German Bake-Off of different exhaust-pipe crossovers, X- and H-pipes in different locations and diameters, until the team had a flat-crank engine that sounds an awful lot like its cross-plane predecessor. This sounds like a lot of work for no gain, but perhaps landing precisely back on the M177’s output figures while futureproofing the engine against stricter emissions regulations is a point of pride for the development team. The new V-8’s heads have reoriented intake ports to facilitate what the engineers dubbed the “super high-tumble Miller cycle.” Engine nerds will note that the Miller combustion cycle’s late intake-valve closing improves thermal efficiency at the expense of horsepower. #4812. Baby manta rays stick to the shallows to avoid sharks and orca

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

Before giant manta rays (Mobula birostris) can grow to up to 30 feet long and 4,409 pounds, they must start small like most living things. Giant manta ray babies (called pups) in Central America don’t have their size to protect them from orca whales (Orcinus orca) and the numerous sharks in the open ocean. Their solution—sticking to the coast, where there is plenty of food to help them grow and defend themselves. A new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Fish Science details the baby rays’ behavior.

“We show that manta rays’ lifestyle isn’t a one-size-fits-all behavior. Giant manta rays in Central America undergo a dramatic shift in space use as they mature,” Jeffry Madrigal-Mesén, a study co-author and project leader of Mantas Costa Rica, said in a statement. “Juveniles remained strictly within shallow nearshore waters that serve as coastal refuges, but most of the tracked adults executed long-distance transboundary migrations along the continental shelf break into Central America.”

In the study, the team used multiple methods to track and identify mantas at five locations between 2018 and 2025. Acoustic telemetry uses underwater sound pulses to monitor fish movements and allowed the team to track 59 individual rays. With satellite telemetry, transmitters track movement and behavior from a distance and helped the team track large-scale movements of 12 individuals. Lastly, photo identification helped to identify individual manta rays.

“If we hadn’t used all three technologies together, we simply wouldn’t have been able to piece this puzzle together,” explained Madrigal-Mesén. “Combining these tools turned fragmented data into a unified, undeniable story.”

The team ended up with a dataset of over 12,000 detections. The data showed that young mantas (those with a body about 10 feet or less) stick to shallow coastal refuges. In these more shallow spots, the pups have a safe place that doesn’t overlap with their predators’ hunting grounds. They have plenty of food and places to hide, and even get essential services like cleaning from reefs.

“Mature adults can rely on their massive body size and their ability to dive deep to evade threats. But juveniles don’t have that luxury,” said Madrigal-Mesén. “What is truly amazing is seeing how strictly young manta rays stick to coastal waters as long-term refuges. We see the same individuals more often than once a year. In fact, just recently, they were sighted again at the exact same tagging location. It is always a joy to see them again—they have truly become a part of the family.”

By comparison, nine mature mantas in the study went on expansive migrations along the continental shelf break. One manta ray nicknamed Mickey Mouse because of a spot pattern on his belly had movements that suggest the fish also continue to display loyalty to a particular site once they grow up. Unusually approachable for a manta, Mickey Mouse allowed the team tag him multiple times over several years, which provided a continuous dataset. In 2020, he logged 462 acoustic detections at a coastal hub called El Secreto. He returned to the same shelf in 2023, and stayed there for 38 days.

“Nearshore cleaning stations transcend basic hygienic function; they act as primary social hubs where courtship and reproductive interactions take place,” said Madrigal-Mesén. “Returning to a predictable coastal depot allows mature rays to prioritize social and cleaning needs before undertaking large-scale regional migrations.”

Once they leave coastal regions, adult manta rays face different dangers. Coastal areas are often legally protected marine reserves, but the open ocean is another story. They no longer have legal protection and are exposed to dangers from industrial fisheries and vessel strikes.

According to the team, this shows that marine protected areas alone are not enough to protect this highly mobile species as it swims across Central America. They say that multi-national “conservation swimways”  that link the domestic juvenile coastal refuges with adult migratory corridors could help keep the endangered rays safe.

“This study opens a new chapter for manta ray research in Central America,” Madrigal-Mesén concluded. “We hope it sparks fresh enthusiasm and encourages future collaborators to join us in executing the new ideas and research projects we have planned for the conservation of this species.”

#5654. Rio Tinto eyes a giant hot-brick battery to cut fossil fuel use

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Rio Tinto wants to put Australia’s cheap midday electricity to work at its Yarwun alumina refinery – by storing it in hot bricks and using that heat battery to make steam after the sun sets.

The global mining giant and California-based Rondo Energy have started engineering and design work on a huge heat battery near Gladstone, Queensland. Rondo calls it Australia’s largest planned thermal energy storage project and says it would be a first for the alumina industry.

Alumina is a hard white powder made of aluminum and oxygen that’s used to make aluminum metal, sandpaper, and strong ceramics. Globally, aluminum sector refining accounts for roughly 175 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent annually.

It’s still at the study stage, but the potential payoff is substantial. Rio Tinto estimates the battery could supply around 5% of Yarwun’s steam needs and cut the refinery’s direct emissions by about 67,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide annually. It would replace some of the coal and natural gas used to generate steam.

Rondo’s heat battery uses electricity to heat refractory bricks, which can withstand high temperatures. The bricks hold that energy as heat, which is then used to produce steam for the refinery’s existing steam network.

That matters because industrial heat demand doesn’t stop when solar production drops. Rondo’s heat battery charges during hours when electricity is cheap and plentiful, then delivers heat continuously so factories can keep running.

The proposed Yarwun system would have 360 megawatt-hours (MWh) of thermal storage and deliver up to 30 megawatts MW of heat. The Australian Renewable Energy Agency (ARENA) lists its electrical charging capacity at 78 MW, allowing it to take in electricity faster than it releases the stored energy as heat.

For the refinery, another attraction is that the battery would feed into equipment it already uses. The study will assess whether that integration can deliver reliable high-pressure steam at a cost that makes sense.

“This project shows how Australia’s abundant energy can become a competitive advantage for industry,” said Dr. Kira Rundel, Rondo’s head of Australia. “Heat batteries allow manufacturers to use low-cost electricity to produce the reliable, round-the-clock heat their operations need.”

ARENA is providing an initial A$3.6 million for the engineering and design study. If the project moves ahead, the agency will provide up to A$38 million for deployment. There’s also a further A$33.3 million in contingency support.

Rio Tinto will match ARENA’s A$3.6 million study contribution. The company says its total investment over the project’s life is expected to exceed ARENA’s funding if deployment goes ahead.

“Reducing emissions from alumina refining is complex, particularly when it comes to generating the high-pressure steam needed for our operations,” said Rio Tinto Pacific Operations managing director Armando Torres.

“This study will help us determine whether thermal energy storage can provide a technically and economically viable alternative to fossil fuels for part of Yarwun’s steam requirements.”

The feasibility study is expected to take about 12 months.

On Oct. 21, 2008, the Indian Space Research Organization launched its first mission to the moon. The mission was named Chandrayaan-1, and it consisted of both an orbiter and an impactor.

Chandrayaan-1 launched from the Satish Dhawan Space Center on an Indian rocket called the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle, or PSLV-XL. (Launch occurred at 8:52 p.m. EDT, though it was about 6:22 a.m. local time at the launch site on Oct. 22.)

It arrived in lunar orbit about three weeks later and dropped off the Moon Impact Probe, which crashed into the moon on Nov. 14. The rest of the Chandrayaan-1 spacecraft stayed in orbit, where it collected and transmitted data from the moon to Earth for about a year. The mission ended abruptly 14 months ahead of its planned end date when scientists lost contact with the probe.

The Indian Space Research Organisation, or ISRO, had a lot riding on the success of Chandrayaan-1 moon mission, which marked the first-ever deep space mission by the Indian space agency. It was also the first mission to the moon for India, a country that hopes to launch its own crewed spacecraft and build a space station.

Chandrayaan-1 did more than just demonstrate that India's space program was capable of launching missions to the moon; it also returned some amazing science results, like evidence of water ice on the moon.

"In September 2009, scientists published results of data collected by the American M3 instrument which had detected absorption features on the polar regions of the surface of the Moon usually linked to hydroxyl- and/ or water-bearing molecules," NASA said in a mission overview.

The spacecraft carried 11 instruments to study the moon, five of them built by ISRO. The rest were experiments for the European Space Agnecy and NASA.

"In addition to the five Indian instruments, the spacecraft carried scientific equipment from the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, and Bulgaria," NASA said.

It took until Nov. 8, 2008 for Chandrayaan-1 to enter lunar orbit, where it stayed until contact was lost in August 2009, most likely due to a power supply failure caused by overheating.

"This was short of the spacecraft’s planned two-year lifetime, although ISRO noted that at least 95% of its mission objectives had been accomplished by then," NASA wrote.

India did not stop at the moon with Chandrayaan-1. In 2019, the country launched the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter, which carried the Vikram lander and Pragyan rover. Chandrayaan-2 reached lunar orbit successfully, but the Vikram landing failed.

The Chandrayaan-3 misson followed in 2023, which successfully landed a new Vikram lander and Pragyan rover near the moon's south pole. India plans to launch the Chandryaan-4 mission to return moon samples to Earth in 2028.
