#3723. Sacha Baron Cohen and Nathan Fielder Reveal Just How Shameless America Can Be

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I was sitting at Sunset Tower in West Hollywood, telling a publicist how Sacha Baron Cohen duped Newt Gingrich. In the British comedian’s latest movie, Ali G: Who Iz I?, the former House speaker was tricked into recording a public service announcement warning Americans about an imaginary cartel leader who was trying to naturalize 143,000 of his own children, I recounted to her.

Much to my surprise, when I reached out to Gingrich’s team for comment, they didn’t bother to explain away his appearance. On the contrary, they seemed thrilled by the attention it was getting. “Our hits blew up” after it was reported he would be in the movie, a Gingrich representative told Vanity Fair.

“The shameless can always make a living in America,” my publicist acquaintance responded with an air of amusement.

Her sentiment was so right on that it actually stung a bit. I arrived at a similar eerie conclusion after watching Nathan Fielder’s You Can See Everything, in which potential investors indulge Elizabeth Holmes’s idea to create a new version of her defunct fraudulent company, Theranos. Holmes was willing to put unreliable blood-test results in the hands of patients and, even after that fraud was exposed, Fielder showed us investors still willing to support her efforts to get back into the biotech game.

Taken together, the two films, which fittingly arrived in theaters a week apart, offer a rather bleak diagnosis of the American condition.

From social media to politics, we increasingly incentivize the brand of brazen behavior that these filmmakers exposed. Meanwhile, the institutions that once policed this behavior are crumbling, and the new ones emerging in their place seem like they’re engineered to reward it.

In many ways, Fielder is Baron Cohen’s disciple. Baron Cohen pioneered a genre he calls “undercover character documentaries,” where he inhabits fictional personas and enlists unsuspecting regular people to advance the narrative. It’s a high-wire act—an apt analogy considering the comedian is constantly putting himself in legitimately dangerous situations in order to stumble upon a coherent plotline and find the funny.

The Canadian comic, who worked with Baron Cohen on Who Is America? in 2018 and later contributed to Borat Subsequent Moviefilm, took the concept in a more psychological and ambiguous direction. Fielder has made a career out of constructing elaborate social experiments, like building exact replicas of people’s homes and hiring actors to impersonate their loved ones on The Rehearsal. Or when he came up with a marketing idea to rebrand a struggling coffee shop as a “Dumb Starbucks” on Nathan For You, and it turned into a viral sensation.

While Cohen wants to expose the consequences of the con (how conspiracy theories impact the world), Fielder wants to understand how the con works (how these lies start and spread, and why we’re so susceptible to them). “He’s really reinvented the genre, and he had a rule which was not to do anything that I had done,” Baron Cohen tells me. “He’d say ‘we’re going to do the stuff that Sacha would never do.’”

Now the former collaborators have released films that seem to be in conversation with one another, even if the filmmakers are approaching the same questions from opposite directions. (Baron Cohen told VF he had been talking with Fielder about his movie, though he had not yet seen it. Fielder’s reps could not be reached for comment.)

Baron Cohen’s creative innovation in the 1990s was to make people reveal prejudices they might otherwise be embarrassed of. Appearing as Borat, the fictional Kazakh journalist, he once got a roomful of Americans to sing along to a song called “Throw the Jew Down the Well.” The whole thing was revelatory at the time. The Anti-Defamation League didn’t even know what to make of it, and spoke out against Baron Cohen (who is Jewish) for spreading antisemitism.

“At that point we revealed this underbelly of antisemitism,” Baron Cohen tells me. “Now that would not be that revealing.”

He’s right about that. Social media platforms show their users the ugliest side of humanity. You don’t need a fictional Kazakh journalist to find proud racists or to catch politicians saying ridiculous things.

Baron Cohen also finds himself, once again, fighting allegations that Ali G is racist. “I almost got canceled in 1999,” he recalls, when critics began questioning whether his hip-hop-loving alter ego with a Jamaican accent was essentially a modern-day minstrel act. Cohen insists the joke was always on Ali G, not the Black culture the character was so desperately trying to emulate. The character, he explained, is “a white guy who’s a culture appropriator,” a wannabe Tupac Shakur whose swagger far exceeds his self-awareness.

Cohen knew the character’s return would stir up some outrage. In Britain, comedians including Gina Yashere, Mo Gilligan, Curtis Walker, and Dane Baptiste have been critical of Ali G’s reliance on racial stereotypes. So he decided to beat them to the punch by recruiting progressive influencers and putting their outrage into the movie. But the joke took on a life of its own. NewsCord also launched a coordinated campaign urging people to email movie chains in an effort to prevent the film from hitting screens.

There was a report that Amazon/MGM Studios chose to limit the number of theaters screening the movie because of all the controversy, with most locations showing the film only once a day. The movie will begin streaming on Amazon on Wednesday. Meanwhile, Fielder’s film is on track to be the highest-grossing documentary in 15 years.

“This is my first movie in six years, so it’s financially disastrous. It’s a terrible business model. There’s a huge risk, but I feel I have to do it. I feel I really want my fans to laugh really hard,” Baron Cohen tells me. “And I want them to think, and I want to create a society that is a little more tolerant, as Ali G says, to increase the peace, and one where there’s a bit more respect,” he says, using his Ali G accent to punctuate “respect.”

Much of the film is about an invented conspiracy involving a Mexican cartel leader named Alejandro Gonzalez, who supposedly has fathered over 140,000 children. The premise is preposterous on its face (Baron Cohen tells VF it was “a laughable, absurd idea”). Nevertheless, Baron Cohen persuades real politicians, including Gingrich and Congressman Andy Harris, to condemn the fake cartel leader. Harris could not be reached for comment.

But the movie’s most disturbing sequence takes the deception a lot further.

Disguised as an Israeli intelligence operative, Baron Cohen recruits a group of MAGA supporters, including a local Republican Party leader in El Paso, into what they believe is a mission to prevent the birth of the fictitious cartel leader’s child on American soil. We find out that the participants, unaware that they are inside a meticulously produced fiction, are willing to kill an innocent baby in order to prevent the birth of an “anchor baby.”

Baron Cohen described the sequence as a kind of Milgram experiment, invoking the famous psychological tests conducted after World War II to study the power of authority, and get a better understanding about why so many Germans bought into Naziism.

The film also features former congressman George Santos participating in a game involving dolls representing liberal stereotypes, a mock gas chamber, and a button labeled Zyklon B, the poison used by the Nazis at their death camps. Santos did not respond to the request for comment that VF sent on October 20.

I found the elaborate operation involving the newborn particularly dark. Baron Cohen seemed to demonstrate that the same conditions that made fascism possible in 20th-century Europe are very much in place today. Convince people that an innocent person represents an existential threat, furnish them with a frightening conspiracy theory about an immoral enemy, and some may become willing to kill.

When I excitedly described the sequence to colleagues, however, several seemed unimpressed. Of course conspiracy theorists are capable of violence, they intimated. Hadn’t we already established that?

Their reaction struck me as almost as revealing as the movie itself. Baron Cohen spent years building an elaborate psychological experiment to demonstrate something horrifying about Western society; however, it seems like everyone already knows what he was trying to tell us.

“The reason I made the movie is hopefully people stop for a moment and think about where we are,” Baron Cohen tells me. “These hateful lies and conspiracies that were spread on social media and now are being incorporated into AI could lead to murder on a large scale, because once people believe in conspiracy theories, they can do heinous acts.”

He places considerable responsibility on the technology companies that make so much money spreading lies. He singled out Mark Zuckerberg, whom he called “probably the biggest inciter of Jew hatred in 100 years,” referring to the spread of antisemitism on Meta’s platforms. Meta did not respond to a request for comment after VF inquired last week.

His broader indictment was directed at the incentives governing the technology industry and Section 230, which largely shields social media companies from liability for content posted on their sites. “One must assume that these tech companies are in no way moral companies,” he said. “They are concerned about making money.”

Which brings us to Elizabeth Holmes, who for a short period, schemed her way into becoming a billionaire.

The crazy thing about Holmes is that even after her fraud was exposed, she still had enough confidence in her own rhetorical powers to invite another master manipulator, Fielder, into her life. Does she have no shame about the fraud she perpetrated? Or was she too naive to realize that Fielder was going to expose her as a fraud all over again?

Holmes didn’t need a disguise or a hidden-camera setup to fool her marks. She had a black turtleneck, a deep voice and the vocabulary of Silicon Valley. Government officials, executives, and prominent public figures mistook her confidence and ability to perform credibility for evidence that her technology worked. They wanted to believe that they were given a once-in-a-lifetime investment opportunity that could shower them with wealth, and they didn’t bother to scrutinize her claims.

Both films expose the massive gullibility and brazenness in every rung of American society. They collectively reveal that people will believe almost anything if it confirms their fears or hopes, and how far people will go in service of lies.

Luckily for my sanity, I found some hope in a conversation I had with a communications director for one of the politicians Baron Cohen duped in his new movie.

When I explained that her boss had appeared in the fake PSA about the imaginary cartel leader, it was clear that she was digesting the information in real time.

“Now I’m horrified,” she said, before defending herself: “I’ve only been here a year and a half.”

#5469. California is still without a functioning community solar program

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

California, the state with the most installed solar capacity in the country, has spent years trying, but still doesn’t have a community solar program. That means residential solar and storage systems are often still only accessible by the affluent, and low-income residents and renters continue to experience the country’s second-highest electricity rates without any self-generated relief.

A community solar project in Colorado, developed by Pivot Energy.

Despite the California Legislature mandating that the state create a community solar program in 2022, nothing yet has been established. Solar and community advocates have been battling with the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) and investor-owned utilities to establish a working community solar market, but talks have reached a stalemate. Outgoing Gov. Gavin Newsom vetoed the latest community solar legislation in September, and the state continues to lose out on this new solar market development.

In 2022, Newsom signed AB 2316 into law, which aimed to create a community renewable energy program that would support both solar and storage projects. The bill required large utilities serving more than 100,000 customers to create and implement programs that “enable ratepayers to participate directly in offsite electrical generation facilities that use eligible renewable energy resources.”

In 2024, the CPUC rejected a proposal from solar advocacy groups that would support community solar in the state, instead putting more decision-making power into local utility hands. State legislators then attempted in 2025 to pass a new bill (AB 1260) designed to strengthen and expand the initial framework for California’s community solar-plus-storage program, guaranteeing affordability to low-income customers, among other stipulations. AB 1260 was never fully adopted.

Instead, community and solar advocates threw their support behind AB 1813, the Community Renewable Energy Program Act. Groups like Californians for Local, Affordable Solar and Storage (CLASS) said AB 1813 was a workable community solar and storage program that could overcome the CPUC’s “built-to-fail” program outline.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom Photo credit: Gage Skidmore

The California Legislature passed AB 1813, which directed regulators to value community solar and storage using the CPUC’s own Avoided Cost Calculator, an existing tool the state has declined to apply, and required paired battery storage so projects deliver power when the grid needs it most. Ultimately, Newsom vetoed the bill in September, and community solar development in California is still stalled.

“With Gov. Newsom’s veto of AB 1813, California continues to fall further behind other states who are aggressively moving forward with successful community solar programs. Unfortunately, the voices of community leaders, environmental justice organizations, ratepayer advocates, labor, community choice aggregators, the building industry and so many others were denied a solution they have long fought for,” said Derek Chernow, executive director of CLASS. “Study after study demonstrates the benefits of community solar-plus-storage to households, businesses, our environment and the grid. We are committed to working with the next administration to make community solar and storage part of the solution to California’s growing affordability crisis.”

Newsom, whose gubernatorial term ends in January 2027, stated in his veto that AB 1813 “would effectively require an administratively set price for the generation of power that exceeds the value that generation provides to the grid. This would result in significant upward pressure on consumer energy bills without delivering reliable, high-performance capacity to the grid at the times we need it. Fundamentally, this bill would shift costs and work counter to the state’s energy affordability and reliability goals. For these reasons, I cannot sign this bill.”

Without an approved process for bringing community renewable energy efforts online, project developers will focus on opportunities outside the state.

A Renewable Properties solar + storage project in California that was unable to be classified as a community solar array. Credit: Renewable Properties

Aaron Halimi, founder and CEO of Renewable Properties, a community solar developer headquartered in San Francisco, said Newsom’s veto relied on outdated cost assumptions and would hurt local rate payers looking for utility bill relief.

“Twenty-two other states have workable community solar programs that are attracting investment, creating local economic benefits and expanding consumer access to clean energy,” he said. “As a result of this veto, Renewable Properties will invest more of our resources into those 22 community solar states instead of our home state of California.”

There’s not much else advocates can do until the next legislative session begins in December. Then a new governor would have until September 2027 to sign any community solar legislation into law. Chernow said CLASS will continue putting pressure on legislators to build a successful community solar market in California.

Regardless of the rotations and positional adjustments, the end goal remained the same: establishing a 3-2-5 attacking structure.

How Milan arrived at that shape was secondary. The players were given the freedom to interpret their positions and adapt to the situation, provided the overall structure was maintained.

Milan employed several mechanisms to achieve their desired build-up structure, including the use of passing diamonds to progress possession from the back.

They adopted a patient, possession-oriented approach compared to Udinese’s more direct style. Milan averaged 6.2 passes per possession, against Udinese’s 4.9, reflecting the contrasting approaches of the two sides.

#3347. The Jaylen Brown situation is getting icky

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Sometimes life immitates art — so they say.

I recently finished NBC/Peacock's Crystal Lake series. Great watch. The premiere season follows Pamela Voorhees as she grieves the supposed loss of her son whose body was never found after he drowned at summer camp. Without spoiling too much, Pam, progressively pushes boundaries of what she'll do to bring the boy home, or to at least avenge the loss.

It reminds me a lot of what we're seeing with Jaylen Brown.

It's a sad spiral to watch. A largely sympathetic character repeatedly does what they can to fumble away the benefit of the doubt.

All over someone named Ja(y)son, nonetheless.

You'd have to be living under the rock to have missed this past weekend's exchange of words between the two former Celtics teammates and NBA champions.

In short, Jayson Tatum posted a summer docuseries episode where he claimed to have known nothing of the eventual blockbuster that sent Brown to the rival Philadelphia 76ers. Brown called "cap" (or BS) publicly in an Instagram comment.

Tatum addressed the not-so-subtle jab after Boston's win over the New York Knicks on Friday. He insisted there was no beef. A day later, Brown agreed, but said there might be some "lamb chops and gravy" and that Tatum should "stay ready at all times."

Then, NBC Sports Boston's Brian Scalabrine went on his Starting Lineup show on Sirius XM and shared details from the final months of No. 7's time with the Cs, saying that Brown had a list of demands he wanted the organization to meet and if they didn't, he wanted to be traded.

Brown's camp refuted the report and Scal walked back some of what he said during Monday's pregame show.

Nonetheless, the damage was already done and you'll never guess how the fanbase reacted.

Those who were already only tolerating Brown while he was still wearing the green and white pounced on the opportunity to smear him and took their repsective victory laps online. I'm not going to link a bunch of tweets here, but trust me, it's not hard to find it.

The speed in which a large majority of the fanbase has turned its back on JB is dissappointing. He's undeniably one of the great players in the franchise's storied history and will likely be the last to ever wear No. 7 (unless this gets brutally ugly).

It's been less than four months since the trade and his status in the Celtics community has already plummetted. But regardless of how you feel about it, Brown won an NBA Finals MVP here. He played an undeniable role in one of the winningest eras in franchise history (in terms of winning percentage). Plus, JB embedded himself in the Boston community with his 7uice Foundation.

He also, at least partly, did this to himself.

I'm not trying to pick sides here, and frankly probably still land pro-Jaylen, but I also dont' think Scal is just making things up. He might've misspoke and inadvertently suggested that there was a trade request, but there has to be some truth mixed in there.

Maybe. It depends what the alleged demands were. If Brown wanted the organization to favor him over Tatum, really in any way but specifically in some sort of "me or him" scenario, then he was out of bounds.

We'll probably never know what they were, but it's safe to assume it wasn't something small.

Then, of course, the Twitch stream following Boston's playoff elimination didn't sit well with anyone. Brown reitterated that he was proud of his teammates (as he should've been with the low expectations publicly put on the group ahead of the season), and that the 2025-26 season was his favorite of his 10-year career.

Mind you, the Celtics won a title two years ago in which he was Finals MVP.

So now, we're looking at a formerly beloved player now playing for a rival team and a fanbase that is willing to go to war for Tatum, who still wears the uniform and is the undisputed face of the franchise. The unstoppable force will meet the immovable object on Jan. 21, when Brown makes his regular-season return to TD Garden.

When the NBA released its schedule back in August, fans circled that date imagining a celebration of a tremendous 10 years. Now it's tough to know what to expect. For all we know, Brown's name might be met with 18,000+ boos when it's called along with the rest of Phildelphia's starters.

The worst part is that it's tough to even say what's appropriate at this point. Brown hasn't been shy about the bitterness he feels towards both the Celtics and Tatum. That bitterness, though, comes from one of two places: ego or betrayal (it's probably a mix of both).

Regardless, a nasty reunion is going to be a bad look for everyone involved. Of course, JB will look foolish or egotistical for burning bridges in Boston. The fans would come across as ungrateful — not the best message for any future potential Celtics.

Who knows what the story will be by then, though. We're still three-ish months away. There's plenty of time for more drama and back-and-forth. For now it just feels icky.

#3331. The Pacers got Tyrese Haliburton back. Now comes the hard part

INDIANAPOLIS — The needle drop hit in the Indiana Pacers' starting lineup announcement, and the chorus of the "Welcome Back, Kotter" theme song played just long enough for the fans at Gainbridge Fieldhouse to fill their lungs with air for what was coming next. The audio flipped again to 50 Cent's "Many Men," and Tyrese Haliburton ran out for the home fans for the first time since he tore his Achilles in June 2025, though you couldn't hear his name over the deafening sound of 17,000 people screaming in unison.

Unfortunately for the home crowd, that was the last time they really got to roar. By the second quarter, the only noise coming from the fans was boos condemning the Pacers for a totally flat performance in a game that was so eagerly anticipated for so long.

The Dallas Mavericks routed Indiana from the start, dropping 44 first quarter points on their way to a 37-point first half lead. The Pacers would rally back a bit in the second half after 19-year-old Dallas star Cooper Flagg exited the game with a shoulder injury, but this was a premium example of a game not being as close as the 16-point final margin would indicate.

"It was one of the best crowds I've ever seen here," head coach Rick Carlisle said after the loss to Dallas. "But a great crowd doesn't guarantee you anything if you don't compete the way you need to compete."

The Pacers needed a big fourth quarter comeback to win their opener against an uninspiring New Orleans Pelicans game a few days earlier. A few days later, the Pacers fully looked like the blitzkrieg offense we expected as they trounced the Nets in Brooklyn. On Monday night, Indiana gave the Milwaukee Bucks their first win of the season in the most jarring result of the new year so far.

"That's what the NBA is," Haliburton said after the game. "It's not all fairytales, sunshine, and rainbows."

Indiana's roster remains similar to the one that went to the 2025 NBA Finals, but every year is different, and it leaves this team with new questions to answer from the last time it was whole. That's especially the case in an Eastern Conference is significantly improved. The idea that the Pacers were going to hit the ground running this season was probably always unrealistic. It's important to remember that they started 10-15 back in 2024-25 before eventually finding some special chemistry on the way to the Finals.

The challenge for the Pacers right now is using the regular season as a lab to figure out their most existential questions without losing too much ground in an ultra-competitive East. Like every team this early in the season, they have a few things that need deeper exploration.

The biggest change from the team Haliburton left to the one he rejoined is the substitution at center. The Pacers let Myles Turner walk because they didn't want to pay him. That looks like a wise move, but it also forced the team to put its best assets on the table to fill its void at center. Indiana shocked the league by trading for Ivica Zubac at last season's trade deadline during a lost year, sending out two first-round picks to get him. One of those was top-4 protected for 2026 when Indiana entered the lottery tied for the best odds. When the pick fell to No. 5 and conveyed to the Clippers, it felt like a double loss for a team that just endured a 19-win season without Haliburton.

Ultimately, centers don't come cheap, and the Pacers had to pay to play in the big man market. Zubac's skill set is so much different than Turner's in ways that are both obvious and less intuitive, and Indiana will spend this season tinkering with the best ways to leverage his talent within their system.

Everyone will notice the spacing component. Turner took 8.7 three-point attempts per 100 possessions during his last season in Indiana. Zubac is a total non-shooter who hasn't attempted a three-pointer the last three seasons. While Turner would pop after setting his screens, Zubac is going to roll, and it takes away some of what made the 2025 Pacers special while giving him a new element with some real benefits.

Zubac is going to play with a lot more power than Turner ever did. His ability to add a component of smashmouth basketball will be welcomed for a team that felt built on finesse even at their best. While the offensive adjustment with Zubac is readily apparent, the defensive end will require some tinkering, too.

Zubac often played in a deeper drop with the Clippers. He was so good at walling off the paint in these coverages that he earned my (fake) vote for 2025 NBA Defensive Player of the Year. The Pacers want their bigs playing more at the level of the screen, which requires Zubac to get up to the touch on ball handlers to see if he can make plays with his hands before retreating back. The Pacers just don't have the point of attack defenders to viably run a deep drop right now, and it's going to require an adjustment period from everyone involved.

The reality is that Zubac didn't look quite as nimble last season with the Clippers before the trade. Now he's already suffered an ankle sprain against Brooklyn that should keep him out of the lineup in the short-term and could linger beyond that. How the Pacers integrate Zubac is the biggest question they need to answer in their 82-game season, and it's likely to remain a work in progress for now.

Carlisle played 11 guys in the first half against Dallas. He played 12 players in the first half against Brooklyn, including a starting lineup switch that subbed out Jarace Walker for Aaron Nesmith. Nesmith was nursing a hamstring injury during the preseason, and his return to the lineup will go a long way toward answering some of Indiana's biggest questions.

"It's hard to run when you're taking the ball under the net the whole game," Haliburton said after the Dallas loss.

The Pacers can probably bank on a top-5 offense with Haliburton back. How they fix the defense is a much more pressing issue. Through three games, Indiana ranks No. 19 in defensive efficiency, which feels about right despite the small sample. Indiana needs Nesmith and Andrew Nembhard to get after it defensively the same way they were during the 2025 Finals run. They also need to figure out what the bench rotation looks like beyond Obi Toppin as their sixth man, and likely Kelly Oubre — the team's only real free agent addition this summer — as their seventh man.

T.J. McConnell hasn't played yet as he recovers from a hamstring strain, and Ben Sheppard is also out with a meniscus tear. Jalen Slawson was firmly on my too good for Summer League list, but his opportunities to add some downhill force and defensive playmaking will still be limited by his two-way contract. Quenton Jackson adds some similar elements as perhaps the most athletic player on the team on a standard contract, and Indiana would benefit from adding his quickness and defensive havoc if it can get around his shooting concerns. Jay Huff was impressive last season filling the center void on a bad team, but Carlisle doesn't appear to really trust him yet. Every time it feels like Walker could take another step forward as a former lottery pick, he stagnates at both ends of the floor.

The Pacers should be able to count on their top-7, which is more than some teams can say. McConnell's return will be big, but he took a noticeable step back last season and may be experiencing some age-related decline at 34 years old. Can anyone else on this roster help force more takeaways to get the offense out running? I'm not sure. If Jackson, Walker, Huff, or even Slawson can make it a viable 8- or 9-man rotation come playoff time, Carlisle should be pleased.

Some will tell you the Pacers were a legitimate Finals threat coming into the season with Haliburton back. Others saw the defensive questions and wondered how this team would matchup against the Knicks, Celtics, and Raptors. I suppose I fall more on the latter end, putting the Pacers at No. 8 for Eastern Conference regular season predictions, and ranking them No. 13 in my league-wide championship picture entering the season.

The great thing about the East this season is that it really feels like every game matters with 10 teams realistically dreaming of making a Finals run. It's just important to remember that 82 games is a long, long, long season. Don't forget that a year ago, the Chicago Bulls started 5-0 before sputtering so much that they blew the team up at the deadline. The Pacers are 2-2 with the easiest schedule in the NBA so far.

The path for Indiana to get back to greatness starts with Haliburton leading this group to a top-5 offense while managing to get the defense into the top half of the league. This was a good-not-great team in 2024-25 before finding an unforeseen level of excellence in the playoffs, which is the exact same thing the Knicks did one year later on their way to a championship. Haliburton is back, but questions remain.

On Monday evening, Harry Kane won the 2026 Ballon d’Or. Could the new status enjoyed by the England striker shake up his future?

It was perhaps one of the most open editions of the Ballon d’Or in years, with many legitimate contenders. In the end, Kane claimed football’s most prestigious individual award, ahead of Kylian Mbappe and Lamine Yamal. The recognition comes as the England striker’s future appeared to be taking shape at Bayern Munich.

According to Sky Sport, the two parties were close to an agreement on an extension until June 2029. However, the 33-year-old has strong arguments to negotiate the terms of his future contract. Could the Ballon d’Or encourage Kane to ask for more, as Ousmane Dembele did a year ago, and test the financial limits of Bayern?

It would be surprising if he risked jeopardising negotiations that are already well advanced. In this matter, the main sticking point could be the hidden clause that Bayern want to remove. It would allow Tottenham to match any transfer offer made for Kane. Will he accept its removal?

A living legend at Tottenham, the striker has no sporting interest in considering a return to the London club, who have struggled since his departure.

From a personal point of view, his move to Bayern Munich in 2023 took him to another level. Having always carried the label of a great striker unable to win major team trophies, he has won titles and found the ideal environment in which to perform in Munich.

This Ballon d’Or win vindicates his excellent career choice. By staying in the Bundesliga, Kane can keep winning titles, dream of winning the Champions League and, why not, of another Ballon d’Or. A return to the Premier League therefore seems unlikely for the London native.

He had also attracted the attention of other European heavyweights in recent months, notably Barcelona and Real Madrid.

However, Barcelona have found other solutions for the centre-forward role and appear more interested in Julian Alvarez should they decide to sign a new goalscorer, while Real Madrid are already well stocked in that area.
