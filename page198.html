#2575. Bundesliga coaches reflect on relentless demands of the job after Vincent Kompany collapse

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Vincent Kompany’s collapse on Friday has prompted a number of coaches across German football to reflect on the demands of their profession while sending their best wishes to the Bayern Munich head coach. Kompany collapsed while watching Bayern’s reserve team train at the Säbener Straße on Friday and was subsequently taken to hospital.

Having struggled with a viral infection since the previous weekend, Kompany was initially considered doubtful for Bayern’s Champions League fixture against Arsenal on Wednesday. The Bayern coach nevertheless returned to the touchline and handled the club’s post-match media duties.

Bayern have confirmed that the 40-year-old is in a stable condition and will remain under medical supervision while further examinations are carried out. Board Member for Sport Max Eberl has indicated that Kompany’s absence is likely to be measured in weeks rather than days.

Several of Kompany’s coaching colleagues addressed the situation before or after their Bundesliga and 2. Bundesliga fixtures this weekend. Kicker and the Deutsche Presse-Agentur collected a number of their reactions.

“I was shocked too. It’s not easy to look after yourself as a coach, because we’re all stubborn. We have to carve out free time for ourselves to clear our heads. I notice it myself: I have one day a week where I really try to leave a little earlier. But I don’t always manage it.”

“I know it. The day is sometimes very, very long. Our job is very demanding. That’s why — as we say in Austria — ‘coaching hygiene’ is very important. I wish Vincent all the very best. I hope it’s nothing too serious and that we see him back on the touchline soon.”

“The important thing in this phase is to give him peace and quiet. He needs the time. We all know how exhausting a coaching job like this is.”

“I used a moment in the dressing room before the game to send him a message. After the final whistle, I saw that he had already replied. That shows that he’s probably already doing a little better.

“From my side, only the very best wishes. We’re opponents so often, but as coaching colleagues we also stick together and know that health is our most valuable asset.”

Thioune’s comments underlined the sense of solidarity among coaches despite the competitive nature of their work.

“As coaches, we think 24 hours a day about what we can improve. Our heads probably work far too much. He’s a tough guy as well and has an incredible personality and a lot of passion. I wish him rest and a speedy recovery.”

“Of course you notice something like that. It’s not nice, but it’s part of life. At this point, I wish Vincent a speedy recovery and send my best wishes to Munich. I hope he comes back as quickly as possible.”

#5197. Mysterious Poem Appears on Bitcoin Blockchain

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Crypto investment giant Galaxy Digital's research unit Galaxy Research has spotted a mystery poem popping up on the Bitcoin blockchain for the last two weeks.

According to Galaxy Research, an unknown user has sent 26 duplicates of the same poem onto the Bitcoin blockchain.

The mysterious poem is: "thoughtless quartz clocks drift / blocks stack each a strict proof / timechain keeps score"

Galaxy Research took to X on Oct. 24 to explain that the mystery sender has been injecting the same message into Bitcoin transactions.

It stored the duplicates in 103-byte OP_RETURN outputs that have surpassed the previous default relay limit on Bitcoin of 83 bytes.

Moreover, the research unit found that the same cryptographic keys have been used to issue another poem twice.

Therefore, the total number of mystery messages has now reached 28 in only 12 days.

The researchers are yet to figure out the mystery sender's motive.

Galaxy Research said that it is unlikely the activities are associated with traditional money laundering, as the funds are just moving between the sender and the custodial service.

Of 28 deposits and 14 round trips, the researchers found only 8,015 satoshis of mining fees and the service's 100k satoshi deduction one time (on Oct. 19).

The researchers from Galaxy Research then theorized about what the mystery activities might be.

The sender may be trying to fake the deposit metrics or transaction volume for a custodial platform.

The activities may also be an automated integration test of sorts in production.

It is also possible that an autonomous AI agent has been given access to a Bitcoin wallet and has been tasked to make transactions.

Nevertheless, the researchers could not confirm these theories.

According to Galaxy Research, if the current trend continues, we will see the 27th duplicate of the poem appear about 14 hours after the 26th.

As of now, no one knows the mystery sender's identity or what the mysterious Bitcoin transactions are for.

Midway through September 2018, Sir Alex Ferguson made his first visit to a deserted Old Trafford for four months – the longest period he had been absent from the stadium since he'd arrived as manager in 1986.

Ferguson had suffered a severe brain haemorrhage in May 2018 – following life-saving surgery, he temporarily lost the ability to speak. After his recovery, his return to an empty Old Trafford was to walk from the directors' lounge to his seat, to ensure he could manage it ahead of a planned visit to watch a game against Wolves. A week later, he took his seat in the stands alongside the doctors and nurses who had saved his life, while being clapped and cheered by the 74,489 inside the stadium.

During his 56 years in the professional game, Ferguson's name was rarely out of the public consciousness. In his new book Games of My Life, Ferguson selects 21 matches that mean the most to him, revealing some of the deeply personal experiences and stories behind the headlines, lifting the lid on tales from childhood, his time as a player and his decades in management.

Here, FourFourTwo pick out two extracts from the book – in his own words, Ferguson talks about his final game as a manager in 2013, and the match that paved the way for his first Premier League title triumph 20 years earlier. Like some of his other choices in the book, it's perhaps not the match you would have expected…

“My hopes for the 1992-93 season went up in smoke after an innocuous pass from Brian McClair from just inside our penalty box towards the centre circle. It was the sort of pass normally picked up by a player who shields himself by turning his back to an opponent.

“On this occasion, one month into the season, Dion Dublin did just that, but was felled by Palace's Eric Young. It was a commonplace tackle, but it resulted in Dion breaking his fibula and dislocating his ankle. He was out for six months.

“I'd signed Dion less than a month earlier to solve the goalscoring woes that had hampered us the previous season, when we had lost the league to Leeds. My number one target had been Alan Shearer, but money was no object for Jack Walker at Blackburn, and he shelled out £3.6 million for him. We'd also made a run at David Hirst. I had several conversations with Sheffield Wednesday's manager Trevor Francis, but couldn't pry him loose.

“I'd first heard about Dion from John Beck, the manager of Cambridge, who had sent me a video of his young striker. I'd liked the way that he had played an important part propelling Cambridge from the Fourth Division to the Second Division, and transformed himself from a centre-back to a centre forward. I thought he could develop into a natural partner for Mark Hughes.

“After Dion was injured though, we were back to where we started. We had several players in our crosshairs, including, yet again, Shearer and Hirst. Once more, our efforts came to nothing, even though we were prepared to pay £3m for Hirst. Finally, I thought about Peter Beardsley, who was out of sorts with Everton, but nothing came of this either. Then, during a conversation about Leeds wanting to bid for Denis Irwin, we accidentally landed on Eric Cantona.

“Like other natural leaders, Eric must have been born a rebel – a boy, and then a man, who questioned convention, hierarchy and normal expectations. He was a man capable of playing as a lone striker or as a deep-lying forward. He was also someone who relished long conversations about football and an arm around the shoulder.

“After Eric settled in, our fortunes changed. We only lost twice more in the league that season, ending the title drought that extended back to 1967. Without him, I doubt we would have won four Premier Leagues in five years.

“The only year we didn't win the title was 1994-95, when he was banned for lashing out at a Crystal Palace supporter. That fan and that decision cost us the league. Bear in mind that fan assaulted the prosecutor at his own trial, for which he was sentenced to a week in jail. “As for Dion, he made a few more appearances and then we sold him. I was glad to see him go on to distinguish himself, and his abiding contribution to United – as he himself has pointed out – was to help us bag Eric Cantona.”

“Any team of mine that squandered a 3-0 or 5-2 lead, to draw a game, knew what would greet them in the dressing room: a proper bollocking. But that wasn't the case at The Hawthorns after United's final game of the 2012-13 season against West Bromwich Albion.

“Instead of a post-mortem where most of the players would have been staring at the floor and avoiding eye contact, there were celebrations. It was not just the end of a season in which we had won the league; this match marked Paul Scholes' 718th game and the last of a spectacular career, as well as the final professional game I would participate in as a player or manager.

“I had been careful to keep my decision to retire under wraps because I didn't want to repeat my previous mistake [in 2001], when my premature announcement made us lose focus.

“I hadn't been contemplating retirement until October 2012, when Bridget, my wife Cathy's sister, had died. On the surface, and for fans, I'm sure nothing had seemed wrong. But Bridget's death changed everything.

“Except for Cathy and our sons, nobody knew about my decision until David Gill came to see me one Sunday afternoon in February and told me that he too had decided to retire.

“I managed to keep my emotions in check while I conveyed my news to the coaching staff, players and everyone else at Carrington. It wasn't until I got back to the office and shut the door that everything caught up with me; I just sat at my desk and cried. I knew then it was over. I'd not be taking this team to Wembley or European games, and I'd not be sending a team out to win a cup or a league. After I wiped my eyes, I thought I had better clear out quickly before the news was made public. The last thing I wanted was to have to deal with the press.

“Shortly after that game, I went to France on holiday, to the hotel we'd stayed at for many years. When we checked in, the concierge confirmed that I wouldn't be needing special messages any more.

“He was referring to the release of the Premier League fixture list. Normally I'd be anxious to see it. But for the coming season it was no longer my responsibility and I could concentrate on enjoying my first free summer since I was a boy.”

Ferguson's book also throws up several more intriguing pieces of information about his life – including the time he ran not one, but two pubs

Growing up in a two-bedroom flat in Govan, south west Glasgow, Ferguson shared a room with his brother Martin. The second bedroom was rented out to an Irish family, while his parents slept on a recessed bed in the kitchen. He grew up a Rangers fan, but explains in the book that his dad Alex Sr supported Celtic. Ferguson joined St Johnstone as a teenager in 1960, but struggled to hold down a place and started going out more at night, causing his father to give him the cold shoulder. An injury crisis, though, meant he got a chance to face Rangers – he hit a hat-trick in a shock win, earning his father's praise.

The Scot began his managerial career with East Stirlingshire and St Mirren – alongside both jobs, he also operated two pubs. He owned one outright, buying it aged 32 for a steady source of income and renaming it Fergie's. In the book, he talks about how it taught him to become a manager, with the role also requiring him to put on special Gaelic nights and bring in entertainment like The Sensational Alex Harvey Band.

Weeks after his treble against Rangers, Ferguson caught the eye of Cathy Holding, who he married two years later. While he became the Manchester United boss, in the book he credits Cathy as not just manager but “CEO, assistant manager, team doctor, kit woman and club ambassador” of “Ferguson United” – a household that included eldest son Mark, plus twins Darren and Jason.

Ferguson respected Brian Clough's achievements as a manager, but didn't like him as a man – he'd call Clough's assistant Ron Fenton when he wanted to buy a player from Nottingham Forest. He also tells a story of when Clough brought his Forest side to Old Trafford and gave his dirty shoes to a Manchester United trainee, to be cleaned before the match. An angry Ferguson ordered that no-one touch them, and when Clough returned for the shoes, Ferguson handed them back dirty and told him never to demand such a thing again.

After overcoming criticism from fans early in his Old Trafford reign, Ferguson won the Premier League in 1993, then the Double a year later. That still wasn't enough for complete support when he sold Paul Ince, Mark Hughes and Andrei Kanchelskis in the summer of 1995, shortly after losing the title to Blackburn. In the book, Ferguson shares letters he received in the post – one labelled him an “idiot” and a “traitor”, while another used letters cut out from magazines to menacingly tell him “Don't sell Incey to Inter or regret it”. He didn't regret it – the sales allowed the Class of 92 to come through, and the rest was history.

Ferguson met Pep Guardiola for dinner in New York in 2012, during the Catalan's sabbatical following his Barcelona exit. At that point, the Scot hadn't definitely decided to retire, and Guardiola was already making plans to join Bayern Munich. In his book, Ferguson says he wishes he'd met Guardiola a couple of months earlier, and tried to persuade him that his future lay in Manchester. As Ferguson rues, eventually it did…

#482. We Linked 150 of Ikea’s $10 Speakers to Make a Wall of Sound

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Ikea launched its super-cheap $10 Bluetooth Kallsup speakers in April, aside from the rock-bottom price and bright, white/green/pink colors, the claim that really caught WIRED’s ear was that the Swedish furniture brand said it was possible to chain 100 of these 2.75-inch sonic cubes together, should you so wish.

Now, because it’s highly unlikely any one household will ever own more than a handful of mini Bluetooth speakers, this is one of those consumer-tech statements that rarely gets tested. So we asked the good folks at Älmhult in Ikea headquarters if they’d let us do just this. To our surprise, Ikea not only agreed, but it also upped the ante, saying it might be possible to link up to 150 without breaking the connection, so that’s exactly how many it sent us. “No one has ever tried this, though,” an Ikea spokesperson said. “We haven’t.”

That is how we ended up building a literal wall of sound at our London office, stacking 150 Kallsups carefully on top of one another to test how many would chain-play from a smartphone. Sadly, one casualty during shipping from Sweden—a single Kallsup—despite lighting up correctly and looking ready to chain, made an ominous rattling sound as if its innards weren’t entirely secure. Sure enough, it refused to play ball (or anything else), so we ended up with 149 speakers.

The chain process itself is simple. Once you have paired a single Kallsup with your music device—in our case, an iPhone 16 Pro—and it’s up and running, simply power on another cube while playing and hold down the Play button until a chime tells you it’s in “multi-speaker” mode. It should then immediately start playing whatever the Bluetooth-connected Kallsup is playing.

Thanks to this near-effortless chaining process, linking up all these Kallsups took much less time than we'd expected, about 30 minutes. We stopped at 100 to test how loud the “system” sounded at that stage before resuming the build and moving on to the maximum amount. As the music continued while we chained the speakers, it would be obvious when the collective refused to add more cubes. But this didn’t happen, and the plucky Kallsups joined forces without grumbling right up to the full 149.

It’s tempting to preface the next few paragraphs with the phrase “for a $10 speaker,” because, despite Ikea’s vast buying power, experienced research and development team, and global presence, the ability to produce a tiny speaker that doesn’t sound absolutely horrible for less than a pint of half-decent craft beer is pretty incredible.

Each Kallsup has a single 1.8-inch, full-range driver powered by a 3-watt amplifier, with Bluetooth 5.3 support. No separate tweeter or woofer, just the one tiny driver.

On its own, the Kallsup speaker sounds fine. Yes, it lacks anything resembling the deep rumbling bass people talk about in audio reviews, but did we mention it’s a $10 speaker? It is a bit tinny, and you won’t want to push the volume very hard, but as a little desktop speaker, one by the side of the bed, or to throw in a suitcase, the Kallsup does the job. It doesn’t match the performance of one of our best Bluetooth speaker recommendations, but keep the cost in mind, and it’s hard to be disappointed.

Even at low background volumes, the Kallsups worked surprisingly well together, especially playing acoustic tracks. As we paired more speakers, the sound gradually grew nicely in scale. Nothing was overpowering or dominating, just the cumulative effect of all those tiny drivers moving small amounts of air.

With 149 individual 1.8-inch drivers, the combined surface area is roughly equivalent to one enormous 22-inch driver. Each Kallsup also has its own 3-watt amplifier, giving our wall a combined rated output of 447 watts. That doesn’t mean it behaves like a giant subwoofer or a conventional 447-watt speaker, but it explains why things got so loud.

But before we turned these up, if you will, to 149, we listened at 100, and when we pushed the volume, the collective Kallsups were seriously loud. But for the record, loud like a fire alarm, not a hi-fi system. With the wall complete, we worried the collective vibrations might send the whole thing crashing down. Disappointingly for the socials, it stayed resolute. Instead, we played Trans Lunar by Twin Dimensions (WIRED director of consumer tech and culture Mike Calore’s excellent band) and slowly increased the volume to maximum.

We almost wish we hadn’t. The noise was brain-numbingly loud as we blasted, somewhat appropriately, Richard Strauss’ Also Sprach Zarathustra. We couldn’t hear each other speak, and we could feel our internal organs vibrating. Any sense of fidelity disappeared, but—all power to the Kallsups—it worked. We’d successfully linked 149 Bluetooth speakers, though sadly we’re well short of the world record of 1,500 speakers set by Majority Audio in March 2026.

At more modest, realistic volumes, everything sounded considerably more composed, even if the music lacked levels of depth and detail. The overall lack of clarity could also be due to minor timing differences between the individual drivers.

At $1,490, a wall of Kallsups is quite an investment, and not one we’re recommending, but it is extremely impressive that we managed to sync and play music wirelessly through so many small, cheap speakers.

We also tested the wall alongside WIRED’s choice for best overall party speaker, the $500 LG Xboom Stage 501. Cranked up, both reached similarly ear-splitting, frankly uncomfortable volumes. But with two 5.25-inch woofers, two 2.5-inch full-range drivers, and clever digital signal processing, the LG delivered understandably fuller, deeper bass and a considerably more musical experience, as expected.

What’s remarkable about this experiment is that so little went wrong. Only one of the 150 speakers shipped didn’t work, clearly broken in transit. We managed to get all 149 sound cubes chained easily and quickly. And when we weren’t blasting the wall of sound at max volume, instead keeping levels moderate, the speakers sounded surprisingly competent as a collective.

However, there were hints of the Kallsup chaining capacity reaching its limit, as Ikea suspected. As we got beyond 100 units linked together, the incidence of failing to chain on the first try increased. Still, no speaker refused more than twice as we got toward the end of the build.

We also would have preferred indentations on the tops of the speakers, which would have given us a bit more confidence that the whole stack wasn’t going to collapse in a heap as volume ramped up (which it didn’t). Such a simple design feature would help whether you’re dealing with just a couple placed on top of each other, let alone 149—but this is splitting hairs.

Lastly, although this setup was impressively loud, no one in their right mind would do this in their own home. Ikea has been a good sport letting us try this out, but the ultimate, unsurprising lesson is: If you want high-quality audio, spend your money on a better speaker. As for the Kallsup? Simple to use, easy to chain, with approachable sound and clean design. You really can’t ask much more for $10.

#1867. Xabi Alonso has lost goodwill of Chelsea fans with his tactical disaster against Spurs... they cannot be considered title contenders with so little authority and ingenuity, writes KIERAN GILL

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 08:56 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 09:46 GMT, 25 October 2026

There was one lone Chelsea fan shouting obscenities at the Tottenham Hotspur team bus a little after full-time. You’re effing this, effing that, a bunch of so-and-sos and what-nots.

Basically a stream so filthy that, if repeated here, it might prompt the reader who emails me a telling-off every time I mention a swearword in copy to throw his computer out of his window.

Trouble is, nobody was on it. Just the driver and the chef prepping the Tottenham players’ din dins for their drive home. It was parking by the mixed zone in preparation for picking up the winning squad. The bloke was wrecking his voice at an empty bus.

It was that kind of afternoon for all associated with Chelsea. Whatever outcome they hoped to achieve, they wound up looking foolish, and so to Xabi Alonso and his tactics.

Chelsea’s record against Tottenham at Stamford Bridge was ridiculous: one Premier League away win in their last 34 visits. Spurs had secured only one victory this season, and that was aided by Coventry goalkeeper Carl Rushworth. They were supposed to still be relegation candidates after this.

Xabi Alonso could only watch as his side failed to assert their authority on the game

Yet Chelsea never looked like winning. They had the odd chance. Pedro Neto came closest when he belted the ball at Antonin Kinsky. But it was all a passive exercise, and that was on Alonso, whose approach betrayed what this derby is supposed to be about: defeating their detested rivals.

His tactical set-up was not befitting of a club that say they want to be competing with the biggest and best soon enough. They showed no authority, no ingenuity, no sense that they were prepared to make the most of what little possession they had as the ball was hoofed in hope amid their 32 per cent.

The one time they pieced together a few first-time passes, Jordan Henderson tested Antonin Kinsky from outside of the box. Otherwise, it was all too mundane for those supporters who had grown used to seeing them exert their dominance over Tottenham down the years.

If we see a similar approach against Manchester United next weekend, it would be surprising, because Alonso lost a great deal of the goodwill that he had gained from the match-going fans with this performance.

It was poor enough to conclude that Chelsea have no right to be considered contenders until they show greater initiative in games, though Alonso’s tactics were not the only reason for this limp loss, as Cole Palmer cannot say he is earning his new contract bump as of yet.

Chelsea’s disciplinary record under Enzo Maresca contributed to them finishing tenth in the Premier League last season. August, September and October last term saw them receive six red cards, including one for Maresca. They had yet to go down to ten men under Alonso.

Yet Palmer had already threatened to bring back that naivety. He had risked a second yellow away at Everton last weekend, sliding in on Jarrad Branthwaite when Chelsea were already leading 2-0 after 100 minutes. Henderson appeared to have a word with him about that silliness after full-time at the Hill Dickinson Stadium.

He got away with it there but not here, and we can have no sympathy with Palmer.

Cole Palmer was given his marching orders for standing on Conor Gallagher's ankle

He deserved his first yellow for barging into Mateus Fernandes when he grabbed the ball to kill a Chelsea attack. Palmer’s frustration may have partially lain in how he had hardly had a chance to get at Tottenham, so he reacted aggressively.

Despite him shaking his head as he walked to the tunnel in the 80th minute, his second booking was clear, too. Conor Gallagher won the ball, and Palmer stood on his ankle. Alonso initially adopted the Arsene Wenger response by saying he had not seen it, but did concede he had been told it was the correct call from the referee.

We have seen before how Chelsea players like to rough up the odd alumnus. Usually it was Enzo Fernandez leading that charge. After Palmer’s second yellow, Morgan Rogers likewise gave Gallagher a kicking, which Sky Sports co-commentator Gary Neville reckoned deserved a red. It all made for a flashback to last term under Maresca.

The only silver lining to Palmer now being suspended for Chelsea’s trip to Liverpool in the Carabao Cup this Wednesday is it should give Estevao a rare starting opportunity to impress.

Losing Joao Pedro to an injury in their final training session was a blow, of course it was. But it is how you handle his absence which tells us the true strength of a side.

Alonso bizarrely did not bring in Danny Welbeck as a like-for-like replacement. He shuffled his pack and started Geovany Quenda, who did not do enough with his 72 minutes from wide.

Nicolas Jackson playing so well for Aston Villa earlier in the day – scoring twice as they downed Maresca’s Manchester City – was bad timing for Chelsea. We can understand why he was sold to Villa for £65million in the summer. Jackson wanted to be a starter, and that was not going to happen while Chelsea had Joao Pedro as a striker.

Geovany Quenda came in wide with Joao Pedro absent but failed to make an impact

But the lack of stand-in was alarming, as was Alonso’s call not to bring in a proven focal point at the top of his team to try to hold up the ball like Jackson would have done.

We do not yet know how long the Brazilian will be sidelined with his injury still being assessed by Chelsea. A source close to Joao Pedro’s camp said they too do not yet have a timeframe.

One of the questions I asked Alonso at his pre-Tottenham press conference was to do with Penders and whether he hoped Chelsea’s supporters would understand that he is only a 21-year-old understudy who may make mistakes – say from set-pieces.

‘There will be good moments, and there will be tough moments,’ he said within his answer. Here was a tough one. Penders was starting in the Premier League for the first time, filling in for the injured Emiliano Martinez, who was sat behind the bench at Stamford Bridge.

#4648. Why do dead bodies move and make noise?

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 25, 2026 7:54 AM EDT

Death is supposed to be the end of the road. The lights are out, the curtain has fallen. No breathing, no heartbeat—end of discussion, right? Not quite.

In some cases, corpses twitch, flex, shift, change positions, or even make noises that could easily be mistaken for messages from beyond the grave. But before you call the Ghostbusters, take a deep breath. The explanation is less zombie apocalypse and more basic biology.

Even after the heart stops beating, the body still has a few tricks left up its sleeve. The results can be creepy, sure, but experts say they’re perfectly natural. Here’s why corpses sometimes move and make noise.

Our bodies can continue moving long after death. When researchers in Australia used time-lapse cameras to keep tabs on a decomposing human body, they discovered that it continued to shift for at least 16 months, with some parts moving as much as 20 total inches over the course of the study period.

Researchers call this “post-mortem movement”—and it’s a lot less dramatic than you might imagine.

“A body can move, but not necessarily in the way that I think most people envision,” Derek Boyd, a biological anthropologist who co-directs the Forensic Anthropology and Bioarchaeology Laboratory at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, tells Popular Science.

“The movements are very subtle—you’re not going to see a body sit up or turn its head.”

After death, the human body moves through a pretty predictable series of decomposition stages. One of the earliest is rigor mortis, when the muscles stiffen and the body becomes rigid. As this process unfolds, a corpse may shift, creating the appearance of movement—but it’s easy to miss, Boyd says.

“You’re not necessarily going to see [the movement] unless you’re laser-focused on the body for the first 48 hours—and even then it’s just going to look like tension, just the body stiffening,” he says.

The next stages in which movement might occur are boat and post-bloat, says Boyd. When someone dies, the body’s own enzymes begin breaking down its cells, a process known as autolysis.

Meanwhile, bacteria and microbes start spreading throughout the body, digesting tissues and producing gases. This process, known as putrefaction, can “cause the body to become distended or start to bloat, and that bloating can result in a dramatic expansion,” he says. “The body inflates in various places. But, again, this is something that happens gradually.”

Eventually, those gases escape—and the body gradually deflates, sometimes causing small shifts in its position.

Finally, in the advanced stages of decomposition, the body’s soft tissues begin to dry out, a process known as desiccation. “We have ligaments that are attaching bones together, tendons connecting muscle to bone, and in these later stages of decomposition, those connective tissues can shrink and tighten—and, in doing so, they can pull on our skeleton in various ways,” he says.

Of course, not all postmortem movement comes from decomposition itself. Outside forces can also cause a body to shift or change position. Scavengers may drag or disturb remains while feeding, for example, while insects can move small body parts or contribute to the breakdown of tissues. Environmental factors, such as wind, flowing water, or shifting soil, can also displace remains over time.

While we’re alive, our bodies are constantly making noise—burps, farts, stomach gurgles, and growls, creaks, and cracks. Considering how noisy our living bodies can be, it shouldn’t come as much of a surprise that they can continue making noises after death.

As gases build up during putrefaction, for instance, the body can produce gurgles, bubbles, groans, or even noises that sound like sighs, Sarah Chavez, executive director of the Order of the Good Death, a nonprofit that aims to change how people think about death and make end-of-life care and funeral options more accessible and equitable, tells Popular Science. Fluids and gases can also get pushed through the mouth or nose, which can sometimes create surprising sounds, she says.

“It can be startling if you’re not expecting it, but there’s nothing supernatural happening,” Chavez adds. “The body is just continuing to do what bodies do—break things down, move fluids and gases around, and occasionally make unintentional noises.”

Ultimately, the fact that bodies can move or make noises after death is a reminder that the human body is “a beautiful ecosystem of its own,” says Boyd.

In life, our bodies are home to countless microorganisms that interact with one another and help keep us alive. After death, those same microorganisms continue to play a role in the body’s breakdown, creating the conditions that can cause a twitch, a shift in position, or an unexpected sound.

The body no longer has thoughts, feelings, or aspirations, but it remains a complex biological system in its own right. “We are walking ecosystems, and we remain ecosystems even after death,” says Boyd.

And scientists are still learning what these postmortem ecosystems can tell us. For example, Boyd says researchers are exploring whether changes to the body’s microbial communities could help forensic investigators estimate how much time has passed since someone died—a “microbial clock” of sorts. In other words, the very same processes that can make a corpse move or make noises may also offer valuable clues about the biology of death itself.
