#5747. Segway launches the $1,999 Xyber Air, bringing its futuristic moped-style e-bike to the masses

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Segway launched the original Xyber last year, it was one of those e-bikes that immediately turned heads. With its futuristic styling, oversized frame, and high-performance off-road ambitions, it looked more like a concept vehicle than something you could actually buy.

Now the company is hoping to capture a much wider audience with the new Segway Xyber Air, a lighter, street-legal version that keeps much of the original’s aggressive styling while fitting neatly into the familiar Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bike categories.

At first glance, you’d be forgiven for mistaking the Xyber Air for its larger sibling. It retains the angular frame design and moto-inspired proportions that made the original stand out, complete with Segway’s signature X-shaped front and rear lighting.

But beneath the familiar styling is a much more approachable machine.

Power comes from a 750W nominal rear hub motor producing 80 Nm of torque, with peak output reaching 1,600W.

Riders can choose between Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3 operation depending on local regulations, with speeds of up to 20 mph (32 km/h) in Class 1 and 2 modes or 28 mph (45 km/h) in Class 3.

Segway has even included a Hybrid Mode that allows throttle operation up to 20 mph while pedal assist continues to 28 mph without requiring riders to switch between ride modes.

That’s still kosher in many states, but some are starting to require true Class 3 e-bikes to have no throttle operation at all, so check your local regulations to make sure you’re compliant.

The bike is powered by a 717 Wh battery that Segway claims can deliver up to 58 miles (93 km) of range, though as always, real-world mileage will depend heavily on rider weight, terrain, and how enthusiastically that throttle gets used.

Suspension duties are handled by an 80 mm dual-crown front fork, paired with 20×4-inch fat tires and hydraulic disc brakes with 180 mm rotors.

The bike also borrows several features more commonly associated with motorcycles, including traction control, hill-start assist, regenerative hill descent control, and automatic lighting.

Whether or not an e-bike requires traction control or hill hold remains to be seen, but it’s neat to know that such moto-inspired tech is heading into the e-bike space.

Segway continues to lean heavily into connected features as well. The Xyber Air includes AirLock automatic unlocking, Apple Find My compatibility, GPS tracking, Lost Mode remote disabling, over-the-air updates, and a full-color TFT display with navigation and smartphone integration.

Some connected services will require a subscription after the first year, which is unfortunately becoming a trend in the micromobility world, but makes sense given the data connection required for those services. Still, it’d be nice to see more of a grace period, like two to three years, for connected features, since it’s not like we’re downloading gigabyte-heavy movies on our bikes.

Despite all of those features, components, and tech, the Xyber Air weighs a claimed 84 lb (38 kg), which is noticeably lighter than the original Xyber, while still supporting a 330 lb (150 kg) payload. It’s also UL 2271 and UL 2849 certified.

Sure, 84 pounds is a lot of poundage for an e-bike, but compared to the moto-styled original, it’s on a significant diet.

Ordering will open on October 27 at a launch price of $1,999, though customers can already sign up on the sales page to get availability notifications.

I actually think Segway made the right move here.

The original Xyber had that cool flair to it, but it also blurred the line between e-bike and electric motorcycle, or shot right past it in many places. The Xyber Air looks like Segway took a step back and asked, “What if we kept the styling that people liked but made it fit within the existing e-bike framework?”

Basically, they kept the “Let’s take a shot at SUPER73” mindset but aimed for a more acceptable format considering today’s regulatory landscape.

The result is still unmistakably moto-inspired, but it’s also something that can legally access bike infrastructure in many places instead of existing in a regulatory gray area. At under $2,000, it also lands at a price point that opens the door to a larger audience.

It’s still not going to compete against the value proposition in something like the Juiced Scrambler, either in hardtail or full-suspension format, but it definitely looks like a fun ride. And with more options for more riders, there’s always going to be something for everyone!

#771. A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

#5639. How Russia Sees the World Now

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Since its founding in 1922, Foreign Affairs has been the leading forum for serious discussion of American foreign policy and global affairs. The magazine has featured contributions from many leading international affairs experts.

DMITRY ADAMSKY is Professor at Reichman University and an Associate with the Project on Managing the Atom at Harvard University. He is the author of The Russian Way of Deterrence: Strategic Culture, Coercion, and War.

The Kremlin’s New Mental Map Is Changing the War in Ukraine—and the Confrontation With the West

In 2022, when Russia launched its full-scale war in Ukraine, the Kremlin’s strategic thinking was shaped around several core ideas. It viewed the United States as its principal geopolitical rival, an adversary that had encouraged Kyiv to impose strategic defeat on Moscow. It considered Europe and NATO largely as an extension of American power. And it saw nuclear muscle flexing as efficient means to exert coercive pressure.

Today, after nearly five years of war, and 22 months of the second Trump administration, the mental map guiding Russian strategy has changed. First, Russia now sees Europe, instead of the United States, as its principal geopolitical adversary, a shift that has driven Russia’s defense establishment to call for an approach known as “castling” to deal with the main threats. Related to this, Moscow now attributes new strategic agency to Europe itself because of the dramatic military modernization taking place in many European countries and the growing interest among some European leaders in developing or extending their own nuclear deterrent. A third shift concerns Russia’s theory of deterrence: Moscow’s traditional understanding of nuclear coercion is giving way to new thinking about nonnuclear strategic deterrence using advanced conventional weapons systems aimed at Europe.

These new lines of thought, which have gained prominence among influential defense intellectuals and policy experts close to the Kremlin over the past year do not need to be endorsed in formal security doctrine to matter. As the past decade has shown, ideas circulating within Russia’s national security establishment have shaped how officials frame problems, define options, and ultimately make major strategic choices. The three recent shifts in Russian strategic thinking will likely play an important part in how Moscow approaches any negotiation to end the war, as well as how it manages future escalation and competition with Europe and the United States.

As the Trump administration steps up its efforts to engage Moscow, it is now negotiating with a Russian leadership whose strategic mindset has evolved considerably since 2022. For the United States, understanding Russia’s new mental map will not only be crucial to any attempt to find an off-ramp with Moscow. It will also be essential to reducing the risk of inadvertent escalation between Russia and Europe and to ensuring strategic stability on the continent in the years to come.

One of the most notable trends in Russian strategic thought over the past year has been the emergence of the idea of “castling.” A term borrowed from chess, castling in geopolitics describes the repositioning of forces and resources to optimize a state’s strategic posture. Moscow’s defense experts have used the concept to describe a strategy for dealing with a new reality: for the first time since World War II, they have argued, Europe, rather than the United States, has become Russia’s principal geopolitical adversary. According to this view, European countries are now the principal source of Ukraine’s military capacity, political resolve, and strategic resilience; it is Europe and not the United States that has made Ukraine its proxy and is encouraging Kyiv to pursue Moscow’s strategic defeat.

As a consequence, Moscow must direct coercive pressure at Europe—its elites, its decision-making centers, and its critical infrastructure. By doing so, Russia can instill into Europe’s calculus what theorists in Moscow call a “deterring fear”: a European reluctance to antagonize Russia through further support to Ukraine. Such action, they argue, can also rebuild Russian credible deterrence for long-term competition with the West, which is now centered on Europe.

Castling does not imply a change in Russia’s overall objectives, which remain the same as those invoked by Russian President Vladimir Putin at the time of the 2022 invasion. These include banning NATO enlargement to Ukraine and other former Soviet republics; rolling back NATO’s eastern flank; and restricting NATO military exercises, as well as the conventional and nuclear deployments of NATO members, near Russian borders. The difference is that Russian coercion must now be aimed at Europe rather than the United States to achieve these objectives.

Along with this revised assessment of Europe, Russian strategists have interpreted the Trump administration’s own strategic stance as evidence that Asia has replaced Europe as the principal theater of U.S. strategic competition. They point to the administration’s reluctance to back Ukraine, its desire to terminate the war, and its continued overtures to the Kremlin. The administration’s decision in mid-October to provide sanctions relief to Russia and apply new pressure on Ukraine amplify this view in Moscow. Russian strategists also assume Washington seeks to solidify its control of the Western Hemisphere and views China, not Russia, as its primary long-term adversary.

Moscow believes that Washington is thus seeking to reduce its commitments in secondary theaters—particularly Europe and the Middle East—in order to concentrate political, military, and organizational resources on Beijing. As Russia sees it, the new American assertiveness in Venezuela, Cuba, and the Caribbean is unsurprising. By contrast, the current war in Iran—the biggest U.S. military engagement in recent years—has been read in Moscow as an unintended deviation from the new direction of U.S. policy, one that Washington seeks to correct as soon as possible. Whether this reading reflects actual American intentions matters less than that it has become a prevailing understanding in Moscow.

A related trend in Russian discourse is a growing fixation on what defense experts refer to as Europe’s “nuclear curiosity” and new military assertiveness near Russian borders. Specifically, Russian strategists have been concerned by the French and British governments’ modernization of their nuclear programs and apparent readiness to wield them more assertively. Moscow has also noted the prospect of French or British nuclear guarantees extending to European states and nonnuclear European capitals considering the development of nuclear options of their own.

Russian sources identify Germany, Poland, and the Nordic-Baltic states as “nuclear curious,” whether in pursuing nuclear-threshold status or seeking protection under French and British nuclear umbrellas. In this regard, comments by Polish officials in early 2026 expressing support for a Polish nuclear deterrent have been particularly noted. Russian strategists have also closely monitored discussions by Paris and London about offering extended deterrence to the continent as an alternative to the U.S. umbrella.

From Moscow’s perspective, any new attempt by a European state to become a nuclear power would cross a redline and could potentially create a casus belli. Moreover, Europe’s growing assertiveness on nuclear issues is happening in parallel with the deployment of conventional deep-strike precision weapons near Russian borders. For NATO, this more active European posture mitigates the risk of Washington shifting away from its European allies, but to Moscow it signals that Europe could potentially undertake strikes aimed at disarming Russia’s nuclear arsenal or decapitating its leadership.

To Russian analysts, these various developments also suggest Europe’s growing strategic autonomy from the United States. Traditionally, Moscow has viewed Europe largely as a “parking lot” for U.S. forces: a region of the world that is relevant but strategically subordinate to Washington. That assumption is changing. Along with the perception that Europe is now Russia’s principal geopolitical adversary, the continent’s increasing strategic autonomy, including on both nuclear and conventional issues, has become a crucial variable in Russia’s calculus. According to Moscow’s defense intellectuals, Russia may need to change its posture to contain this trend, including by seeking new tools to keep Europe at bay and to coerce it below the nuclear threshold.

To align with Russia’s new mental map, Russian defense intellectuals and military officers have been examining how to make Moscow’s coercive threats more effective. During the first years of the war, Russia’s nuclear muscle flexing and hybrid warfare failed to prevent Ukraine’s allies from funneling financial support, military aid, and targeting intelligence to Kyiv. This approach also failed to deter Ukraine’s land incursions into Russia and strikes on symbolic Russian targets and Russian strategic infrastructure, let alone compel Kyiv to surrender.

Today, NATO is preoccupied with Russia’s escalating cross-domain coercion, or what U.S. and European analysts call hybrid warfare. But that is only one part of the Russian approach. As Moscow concentrates on Europe as the new primary threat, it is also drawing on new tools of coercion that Russian experts call nonnuclear strategic deterrence. For Russian military authors, NNSD involves using advanced conventional capabilities, including precision-guided weapons, drones, ballistic, cruise, and hypersonic missile systems, as well as “weapons based on new physical principles”—a Russian catchall term for electromagnetic-pulse and high-power microwave weapons to deter and compel adversaries.

Conceptually, NNSD belongs to what Russian experts refer to as the “art of strategic gestures,” a euphemism for using, or threatening to use, nuclear and nonnuclear capabilities to deter and coerce an adversary. The concept is not new. Since the early years of this century, Russian strategists have tried to develop conventional alternatives to nuclear deterrence. But until the full-scale war in Ukraine began, in 2022, these ideas remained underdeveloped. NNSD has emerged from the lessons learned during the conflict.

From the outset of the war, Russia used tactical ballistics, cruise missiles, and hypersonic capabilities, which it has paired with drones and maneuver warfare. But even when coupled with the threat of nuclear coercion, this approach underdelivered, in the view of many Russian analysts. As a result, as the war has become entrenched, in particular since 2024, Moscow has shifted its focus to more advanced ballistic salvo waves, including strikes using Russia’s intermediate-range Oreshnik ballistic missile.

Drawing on this experience, Russian military theoreticians center NNSD on using massive, deep, precision strikes against the adversary’s critical military, political, and economic infrastructure. The strategy, they suggest, should be aimed not only at Ukraine but also at its European supporters. Although the exact contours of NNSD are still emerging, the concept appears to be more escalatory than the hybrid warfare and deniable asymmetric campaigns that Russia is currently pursuing in Europe.

This is more than a peripheral theoretical discussion. There are already indications that NNSD has gained traction among Russia’s senior military officers: for example, since 2025, the flagship journal of Russia’s general staff, Military Thought, has published several articles on the subject. All the branches of the Russian military are also developing their own versions of NNSD and are seeking to claim a comparative advantage in its execution. This interservice competition may increasingly normalize what constitutes acceptable coercion below the nuclear threshold and shape targeting priorities, as well as escalation management.

As the Kremlin sees it, the war in Ukraine began as an attempt to impel Europe to reshape its security architecture. Yet the strategic landscape confronting Moscow today may look even more threatening than the one it sought to change in 2022. Washington needs to recognize that the Kremlin regards Ukraine not as a discrete conflict but as part of a broader struggle over Europe’s strategic order. Any U.S.-led effort to broker an off-ramp in Ukraine will need to reckon with Moscow’s emerging castling logic and its mounting anxieties about Europe. A settlement that addresses the immediate battlefield while leaving Moscow’s wider frictions with Europe untouched is likely to provide only a temporary pause in this larger conflict. Washington needs to understand that Russia’s nonnuclear coercion has become a part of its ongoing deterrence strategy against Europe rather than simply a feature of the war itself.

Even if Moscow engages in negotiations to end the war, it is likely to continue using military coercion against Ukraine, as well as Europe. It will do so for several reasons: to align the terms of the settlement with Russian interests, to refine Russia’s emerging theory of nonnuclear strategic deterrence, and to force Europe to adopt a different security architecture. Russia may therefore undertake precision strikes on critical infrastructure, and even possible decapitation attempts, as well as calibrated attacks of varying intensity in Ukraine, alongside diplomatic engagement. By using such tactics, Moscow will be seeking not only to gain leverage in negotiations with Kyiv but also to restore its strategic deterrence with Europe. In response, Europe, doubting the credibility of the Trump administration’s commitments and seeking to demonstrate its own resolve, may decide to escalate.

Washington should thus be prepared for the possibility that stepped-up efforts to terminate the war may coincide with heightened security risks in Europe. Given that the dynamic between Europe and Russia is now characterized by pervasive mutual suspicion, conspiracy theories, and expectations of war, there is a growing danger of inadvertent escalation. Actions by either side that are intended as limited coercion, signaling, or leverage might be misread, thus triggering a self-reinforcing action-reaction cycle in which each side’s effort to restore deterrence instead erodes it. Paradoxically, this dynamic could emerge precisely at the moment when all sides—including the Kremlin—are seeking an off-ramp. Both Europe and Russia risk crossing a “culmination point of coercion,” beyond which any additional threats to use force, or its actual use, could expand the war further.

For Russia and Europe, then, the main challenge will be to bolster deterrence without crossing this tipping point. For Washington, the primary task is not only to mediate the substance of a deal but also to learn how to choreograph negotiations amid ongoing military action. It will be imperative for U.S. officials to anticipate coercive moves by all sides without mistaking them for a rejection of diplomacy—and without allowing them to derail negotiations. Washington will need to help both Europe and Russia contain their use of coercion before the security dilemma locks them into a dangerous escalatory spasm.

#5752. Tesla and SpaceX take first step towards merger, would dilute all shareholders

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tesla and SpaceX have both formed special committees within their corporate boards to explore the possibility of merging the two companies according to Elon Musk, who is CEO of both companies.

The merger could have big consequences not just for Tesla and SpaceX shareholders, but could dilute the retirement savings of everyone with exposure to major stock indices.

SpaceX held its quarterly earnings call today, just a few months after going public for the first time.

And for us in the electric car world, the big and unsurprising news was that Tesla and SpaceX are now formally reviewing the possibility of merging the companies together.

The possibility of a merger has been rumored for a long time. Musk has long complained of the need to respond to shareholders’ concerns as CEO of Tesla. It is odd, then, for him to take SpaceX public when he has complained for more than a decade about Tesla being public and having to respond to shareholders.

The problem, as Musk sees it, is that he does not have enough control of Tesla. Over the years, his ownership percentage had dwindled as Tesla sold stock in offerings or granted stock to employees, though today after the restoration of his illegal pay package, his ownership percentage runs around 22%.

This means he can’t ram anything through that he wants, he has to build consensus around his ideas. (That said, shareholders have proven themselves happy to oblige any ridiculous idea that he proposes to them…)

But that’s not enough for Musk. He wants total control of the company, and he’ll try any financial shenanigans to get it. He’s gone so far as to market this idea by saying that he needs $1 trillion so he can control a robot army.

If he gets that most recent pay package, a huge chunk of shares will be created out of thin air, going right into the pocket of the one person on the planet who needs them the least, and diluting the voting power and value of every other person’s shares.

So, he cooked up a plan. Sell the most ridiculous CEO pay package ever devised to Tesla shareholders, making big claims that the stock will only be delivered if Tesla manages to deliver a million robots, a million robotaxis, and various other operational milestones that would surely indicate Tesla’s success as a company.

Then do the same with SpaceX, promising to put a million colonists on Mars (which will not happen). Make sure to give yourself super-voting shares before SpaceX goes public, so you don’t have to worry about ever listening to anyone else.

Let these high-profile targets grab headlines, and trick shareholders who don’t know any better into voting for these pay packages, thinking they’re safe because even if their shares are diluted by these massive stock grants, they’ll just end up with a slightly smaller piece of a much larger pie. It’s a win-win, right?

Well… unless we include a provision in the pay package that says the shares are automatically delivered in the case of a merger. That’s right, if SpaceX and Tesla merge, then all the talk about robots and Mars colonies is irrelevant. Musk gets his stock regardless of any operational milestones.

He gets stock, you get diluted, and you didn’t even see a single robot delivered.

And so, after only the second quarterly report ever presented by SpaceX, the companies are already exploring the possibility of merging.

Musk said today during SpaceX’s earnings call that each company has formed a “special committee” of “independent board members” (and those quotes around the word “independent” are doing heavy lifting) which are reviewing the possibility of a merger.

This does not mean that the merger is a certain thing. First, the “independent” board members need to agree that it’s a good idea – but, given that Tesla’s board has always sought Musk’s interests and not those of the company, it certainly seems that if Musk thinks it’s a good idea, the committee probably will too.

After all, the special committee on pay signed off on Musk’s ridiculous trillion-dollar pay package, despite that all that package did was attach the company to the very anchor that is dragging it down. That package would benefit Musk at the cost of all other shareholders and the company’s own operations, so the board’s independence is called into question if that’s the kind of idea it signs off on.

Musk is not officially allowed to be involved in these discussions, nor are boardmembers not deemed “independent,” like his brother, Kimbal Musk. But Elon has made it clear that he wants this to happen. He did so again today, when he said that he thinks a merger would be a good idea, because “we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity” (notably, we are not, and neither would that be particularly relevant to an auto manufacturer).

So, a board sign-off seems reasonably certain given what Musk has publicly (and probably illegally) directed Tesla’s captured board to do. After that, shareholders of both companies need to approve the move.

For SpaceX this is an easy one – Musk controls more than 50% of voting shares, and therefore the company will approve of the merger. He set it up this way, and the merger would give him more money and more control, so he’s going to vote for it.

For Tesla, the vote could theoretically fail to pass. The company would need a vote of 50% of all outstanding shares, not just 50% of shareholders who turn out to cast their votes.

For this reason, Tesla has already prepared a questionable “auto-vote” procedure. This would allow shareholders to set an instruction to automatically vote all of their shares with board recommendations, no matter how bad those board recommendations are to the shareholders’ own interests.

#870. Scientists discover ghostly, eyeless creature hidden in Chinese cave

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A newly identified freshwater cave snail in China is offering scientists a rare glimpse into the little-known aquatic ecosystems hidden beneath East Asia. Published in the journal Subterranean Biology, the discovery represents an important step forward in understanding the region's underground wildlife. Until now, no freshwater cave snails had been documented in China, and the country's subterranean freshwater mollusks had received relatively little scientific attention.

The newly discovered species, Erhaia dijiang, has evolved remarkable features that allow it to live in complete darkness. As a true cave-dwelling animal, it has entirely lost its eyes, with the structures normally associated with vision having fully degenerated. The tiny snail also has a cone-shaped, nearly transparent shell, a delicate body without pigmentation, and long, slender tentacles.

Its unusual appearance inspired a name drawn from ancient Chinese mythology. Researchers chose dijiang in honor of "Di Jiang," a mountain patron god portrayed as a faceless beast in the ancient text Classic of Mountains and Seas (山海经). The connection is particularly fitting because the snail is extremely sensitive to light. During laboratory observations, individuals consistently retreated into dark spaces beneath rocks whenever they were exposed to illumination.

The location of the discovery is just as remarkable as the animal itself. Scientists found Erhaia dijiang at an elevation of 2,062 meters, making it the highest-altitude cave snail yet recorded within the family Erhaiidae. The finding demonstrates that freshwater cave snails can inhabit limestone cave systems at elevations above 2,000 meters.

Life in the Dark Waters of a Mountain Cave

Within its underground habitat, Erhaia dijiang lives among submerged gravel and broken rock in water less than one meter deep. The cave's water temperature stays close to 20°C, providing a relatively stable environment for the tiny mollusks.

Even when heavy seasonal rains increase the flow of water and occasionally wash a few snails out through the cave opening, they have not been observed in nearby streams. Researchers found that the animals remain confined to the cave environment, staying within two meters of its entrance.

Laboratory observations also provided clues about what these elusive creatures eat. The snails were seen feeding on diatoms, microscopic algae that grow on rock surfaces, and they also consumed fish food supplied by the researchers.

Only Three Living Snails Found, Raising Conservation Concerns

Although the discovery sheds new light on China's largely unexplored underground aquatic biodiversity, researchers are concerned about the species' future. They describe its conservation outlook as "far from optimistic."

During the first field investigation in August 2025, the team encountered an exceptionally small number of snails. Despite searching the shallow cave waters, researchers located only three living adults and one empty shell. They found no juveniles, suggesting that the known population is extremely sparse.

The scientists are now urging more comprehensive investigations of cave ecosystems throughout China to uncover additional species and better understand these fragile underground habitats. They also emphasize the need to develop conservation protections quickly for these vulnerable mollusks, which are native to highly restricted environments and could face serious threats before their populations are fully understood.

#5260. Scientists find 10 different diets linked to slower aging

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A large study involving approximately 7,500 people suggests that eating a healthy diet may help slow biological aging, and there is no single eating plan that stands above all others. Scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) investigated 10 different dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, and DASH diets. The DASH diet was specifically developed to help reduce blood pressure.

Every dietary pattern examined was associated with slower biological aging, as measured by chemical changes in DNA that reflect the body's aging process. The findings were first identified using data from DZNE's Rhineland Study and then independently confirmed in another population. The research was published in Nature Communications.

"Our study suggests that healthy eating goes hand in hand with slower biological aging. The effects are not massive, but they are measurable and relevant for prevention. By slowing the aging process, the risk of age-related diseases such as dementia or cardiovascular disorders can be reduced. Thus, healthy eating contributes to healthy aging," says Prof. Monique Breteler, Director of Population Health Sciences at DZNE and head of the Rhineland Study. "Importantly, there is probably no single 'correct' diet. According to our data, various dietary patterns are associated with slower aging, some more, some less. That is an encouraging finding, because it leaves room to tailor healthy eating to personal and cultural preferences, budget and taste."

To investigate the connection between diet and aging, the scientists analyzed dietary information and blood samples from roughly 7,500 men and women. The initial analysis included 6,470 participants in DZNE's Rhineland Study in Bonn, Germany. The researchers then tested their findings using data from another 1,034 people enrolled in the EPIC-Potsdam Study, conducted by the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbrücke (DIfE).

Rather than relying only on participants' chronological ages, the team looked for molecular signs of aging in their blood. Specifically, they measured DNA methylation, a process involving chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity and influence how cells function.

These chemical patterns change in relatively predictable ways as people grow older, allowing scientists to estimate biological age. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures how long someone has been alive, biological age offers clues about how the body is aging at the molecular level.

"We applied three different approaches, so-called epigenetic clocks, to read biological aging from DNA methylation," explains Juliana Tavares, a doctoral researcher at DZNE and lead author of the current publication. "To this end, we leveraged state-of-the-art technology, which allowed us to cover about 850,000 sites in the DNA. This is roughly twice as many as in most previous studies."

The next step was to determine how closely each participant followed the 10 dietary patterns. Researchers compared reported food consumption with the recommendations for each diet and assigned scores reflecting how well individuals followed those guidelines.

Because the diets emphasize different foods and nutritional goals, a person could receive a high score for one eating pattern while scoring poorly on another. In fact, participants who met one definition of healthy eating often did not meet the requirements of other diets.

"One and the same person could therefore rank high on one diet score and low on another. In other words: People who ate healthily by one standard were often not the same people who ate healthily by another. According to our data, hardly anyone counts as a healthy eater by every standard at once," says Tavares.

Despite those differences, the overall pattern was remarkably consistent. Better adherence to every one of the 10 diets was associated with slower biological aging. Some eating patterns showed stronger associations than others, but the absolute differences were relatively small.

"While there are measurable differences between the diets, they are modest in absolute terms. It's the big picture what matters," says Tavares. "In summary: When it comes to slowing down the aging process, there isn't just one healthy diet, but indeed a whole repertoire of possible diets with a positive effect."

One unexpected result emerged when researchers examined the association between diet and aging among smokers. The link between healthier eating and slower biological aging was stronger in smokers than in nonsmokers, even though smokers did not generally have healthier diets.

The researchers have not yet determined what biological processes might explain this difference. One possibility is that the extensive damage caused by smoking leaves greater room for measurable improvements associated with healthier eating. However, the finding does not lessen the well-established dangers of tobacco use.

"Smoking is very harmful to health," says Tavares, "One explanation for our findings might be that there is just more health benefit to gain for smokers by eating healthy because of the enormous detrimental effects of smoking."

Different Diets Share Common Aging Pathways

The scientists also investigated which biological processes might connect the different diets to slower aging. Although each eating pattern was associated with changes at a distinct collection of DNA methylation sites, the underlying biological pathways were surprisingly similar.

More than 70% of the affected pathways were shared across all 10 diets. These processes help regulate essential functions such as cellular structure, communication between cells, and metabolism. They also play important roles in aging and the development of chronic diseases.

The researchers identified some differences that reflected the specific goals of individual diets. For example, the DASH diet was associated with pathways related to heart health, while the MIND diet, which was developed to support brain health, showed distinct associations with pathways involved in memory and cognitive function.

"More than 70 percent of the affected biological pathways were shared across all diets. These involve cell structure, cell signaling and metabolism -- processes central to aging and chronic disease. Therefore, it is understandable that the dietary patterns we examined were all linked with a slowing of biological aging," says Tavares. "A few pathways were diet-specific, and they matched each diet's main objectives: heart-related pathways were unique to the DASH diet, and pathways related to cognition and memory were unique to the so-called MIND diet, which aims to support brain health. You don't need a perfect diet -- that may be the most practical message here. But moving forward, the diet-specific pathways point to future research on targeted dietary interventions. This could be helpful in designing targeted health recommendations."
