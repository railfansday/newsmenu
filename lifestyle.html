Brand is accused of seven offences against six women in allegations spanning 10 years from 1999. He denies all the charges.

Wearing a grey suit and pale blue shirt, he sat in the dock on Tuesday as his statements to police were read to Southwark crown court in London.

The jury was told Brand was interviewed four times between November 2023 and July 2025, when he read prepared statements in which he insisted he had “never engaged in any non-consensual sexual activity”.

“I led a very promiscuous life in the past, which I have spent years openly denouncing,” he said in his first statement on 16 November 2023.

Referencing joint reporting by Channel 4, the Times and the Sunday Times, Brand added: “On 16 September 2023, a defamatory and fictitious documentary was broadcast in conjunction with the publication of two national newspapers containing false, anonymised allegations which led to unprecedented media reporting.

“Subsequently, both the media and the police publicly invited additional allegations to be made, which resulted in further encounters being invented or reimagined.”

Addressing an allegation that he had touched an MTV worker’s breasts before dragging her into a toilet and raping her in 2004, he said he did not recall meeting the woman.

“At no point would I have touched [her] breasts unless it was consensual,” he said. “I have had consensual sex in toilet cubicles during parties. I have never forced anyone into a toilet and I have never forced my penis into anyone’s mouth.”

In a statement on 14 December 2023, Brand said he did not remember the women who had come forward to accuse him.

But he added: “It is entirely possible that I may have encountered them and that we may have engaged in consensual sexual activity or physical contact that has since been reimagined.”

Brand described aspects of his past celebrity lifestyle, saying: “My engagement with interested members of the public was friendly, consensual and warm. There was often physical contact. “I have shared kisses with hundreds of members of the public during press events and photos of many such kisses, in front of hundreds of people, are available online. Everything I did in public once I was famous could be photographed or recorded and end up in the tabloids – I was aware of this.

“I routinely made provocative jokes or comments both with members of the public and during live performances and broadcasts.”

He also went on to detail his promiscuity, saying: “I did on occasion find reasons for leaving, or for asking partners in casual sexual encounters to leave, shortly after the experience had concluded.

“I had sexual encounters in countless locations and scenarios often with people I had just met. The way of life that I have described, the magnitude of the promiscuity, deceptively rendered in recent media attacks, accompanied by advertisements for complainants, have caused consensual encounters to be unfavourably reframed decades later.”

Brand gave similar statements to police in his subsequent interviews on 17 January 2024 and 17 July 2025. In response to police questions after each statement, Brand said: “I have nothing further to add at this time.”

#4164. The surprisingly simple exercise that could lower your blood pressure

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that exercising just once or twice a week may be enough to maintain some of the blood pressure benefits gained from regular physical activity, at least over a short period. However, people who continue exercising three times weekly may experience larger and more sustained improvements.

The findings, published in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, suggest that even a single weekly session of isometric exercise could help people preserve improvements in blood pressure when they cannot commit to a more frequent workout schedule.

Isometric exercise involves tightening muscles while holding the body in a fixed position for several minutes. Common examples include wall squats, planks, and handgrip exercises.

Earlier research has found that isometric training can lower blood pressure more effectively than other types of exercise, sometimes producing reductions comparable to or greater than those achieved with certain medications. However, researchers have not fully understood how often these exercises need to be performed to maintain their cardiovascular benefits.

To investigate, researchers enrolled 100 adults whose average age was 37.4 years. Participants had systolic blood pressure ranging from normal to high-normal levels (120-140 mmHg) and did not regularly participate in structured exercise programs.

Under expert guidelines, systolic blood pressure between 130 and 139 mmHg falls into the "high-normal" category, which warrants specific lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals. None of the participants had been diagnosed with hypertension.

To qualify, volunteers had to be free of injuries and illnesses, have no current or previous use of blood pressure medication, and be nonsmokers. They also had to consume no more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups. One served as a control group, while the other four began the same four-week exercise program at home, focusing on isometric movements involving the lower body.

The training consisted of four wall squat holds per session, with two minutes of seated recovery between each hold. Participants completed this routine three times weekly during the first four weeks.

After this initial training period, the four exercise groups followed different schedules for another four weeks. One continued with three weekly sessions, another reduced its frequency to twice weekly, and a third performed the exercises only once a week. The fourth group stopped training altogether.

Researchers evaluated blood pressure, heart rate, and additional measures of cardiovascular function at the beginning of the experiment and again after four and eight weeks.

Even One Weekly Workout Helped Preserve Blood Pressure Benefits

After the first four weeks, all four exercise groups showed significant improvements compared with the control group. These included reductions in systolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart contracts, and diastolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart relaxes. Mean arterial pressure, a measure of average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, also decreased significantly.

These initial results were consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the blood pressure benefits of isometric exercise. However, after participants switched to their assigned maintenance schedules, the groups no longer showed statistically significant differences in diastolic blood pressure.

By the end of the eight-week study, participants exercising just once or twice weekly had maintained significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure compared with the control group. Neither schedule maintained a statistically significant improvement in diastolic blood pressure.

The greatest benefits appeared among participants who continued performing wall squats three times per week. Their systolic blood pressure fell by an average of 13.73 mmHg (-13.73 mmHg) from the beginning of the study.

By comparison, the twice-weekly group experienced an average reduction of 6.95 mmHg, while participants exercising once a week recorded a decrease of 6.56 mmHg.

Those who maintained the three-session schedule also preserved improvements in other cardiovascular measurements, including total peripheral resistance (an indication that blood vessels have narrowed) and heart rate variability.

Stopping Exercise Caused Blood Pressure Benefits to Fade

The results also revealed what happened when participants stopped exercising completely.

Those who discontinued wall squats after the initial four weeks experienced a reversal of their earlier blood pressure improvements. By the end of the study, their readings were no longer statistically different from those of the control group.

This finding suggests that maintaining some level of regular isometric exercise may be important for preserving the cardiovascular improvements gained during training.

However, the researchers emphasized that the maintenance phase lasted only four weeks. Additional studies following participants for longer periods will be needed to determine whether less frequent training can preserve these benefits over months or years.

"The study demonstrates that while lower frequency isometric exercise training may be sufficient to maintain blood pressure reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations," the researchers write.

"The study offers evidence that lower frequency isometric exercise training can help maintain short-term blood pressure reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges."

#5244. A simple 15-minute treatment could help patients avoid carpal tunnel surgery

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

A simple procedure that takes about 15 minutes could offer lasting relief to people with carpal tunnel syndrome, a painful condition that can cause numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hands. A new study suggests that the treatment, which uses ultrasound to guide an injection of ordinary saline around a compressed nerve, provides longer-lasting benefits than traditional steroid injections, without the risks associated with steroids.

The findings, published in Radiology, a journal of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), point to a promising alternative for patients whose symptoms have not improved with conventional treatments. Researchers say the minimally invasive technique could also help some patients delay or avoid surgery.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

The study, "Efficacy of US-guided Hydrodissection for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial," was conducted by Anupama Tandon, M.D., Anindita Bose, M.D., Gopesh Mehrotra, M.D., Siddharth Maheshwari, M.D., and Ayush Khandelwal, M.D.
